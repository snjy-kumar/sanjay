Ten weeks to ship something ten real people use, instrumented, iterated three times, with a case study at
the end. The code skills are already in place from phases 0 to 6; this phase is about the gap between
what I build and what someone needs. The companion reading, if I want a book beside the work, is *The Mom
Test* for interviews and *Inspired* or *Continuous Discovery Habits* for the product loop. Each one is
used for one thing, the week I need it.

#### Phase 7: how I would learn it

Users before features. Weeks 47 and 48 have no product code at all: a one-page problem statement and five
conversations. The build starts in week 49 with the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption, and
every two weeks after that is one iteration with a written before-and-after. I keep a single file,
`product-log.md`, with every decision and the evidence behind it, because the case study is written from
it.

#### Phase 7: Problem framing

**Covers:** turning "I want to build an app for X" into one sentence about a person, a situation and a
pain, with a way to know if I solved it.

<Compare
  left="A solution looking for a problem"
  right="A framed problem"

  rows={[
    ['"A habit-tracking app with AI"', 'Developers who start learning plans abandon them in week three, and lose track of what they covered'],
    ['Audience: everyone', 'Audience: people following a written roadmap, alone, part-time'],
    ['Success: launch', 'Success: a user still logs progress after four weeks'],
    ['Features first', 'Assumptions first: they want tracking; they will log; they will come back'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, problem framing">

<Level name="Beginner">

A problem statement says who has the problem, when it happens, what it costs them, and what they do about
it today. If I cannot write it in one or two sentences, I do not understand it yet. The current
workaround is the most important part: it is what my product competes with.

```md
## Problem (one page, v1)
Who: developers following a long self-study roadmap, part-time.
When: weeks 3–6, when the first excitement fades.
Pain: they lose track of what they covered and quietly stop.
Today: a notes app, a spreadsheet, or nothing.
Success looks like: a user logs progress weekly for four weeks.
Riskiest assumption: they will log at all.
```

Common mistake: starting from a technology ("something with agents"). Fix: start from a person and a
moment; the technology is chosen later.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I list the assumptions that must all be true for the product to work, and rank them by risk (how likely
wrong times how fatal). The riskiest one is what v1 tests. A pre-mortem helps: imagine it is week 56 and
the product failed; write the three most likely reasons. They are usually the riskiest assumptions in
disguise.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Framing includes non-goals: who this is not for, what it will not do. Non-goals protect a ten-week
timeline more than any estimate does.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- I am the user: useful for the first version, dangerous after it, because I forgive my own product.
  Five other people must use it before I trust my judgment.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- Two user groups with conflicting needs (beginners want guidance, experienced users want speed): choose
  one for v1 and write the other as a non-goal.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The one-page spec lives in the repo and is revised after each iteration, with dates, so the case study can
show how the understanding changed.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Building is cheap now; choosing what to build is not. The bottleneck has moved to framing and judgment.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The problem statement constrains the architecture: who the user is decides auth, data retention and
scale. "Ten users" does not need queues and microservices; the boring monolith from phase 3 is right.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Write the one page, then a pre-mortem, then rank the assumptions. Done when the riskiest assumption is
named and v1's single job is to test it.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: User interviews

**Covers:** learning what people actually do, without leading them toward the answer I want.

<Compare
  left="Leading question"
  right="Behaviour question"
  caption="Ask about the past and the specific, not the future and the hypothetical"
  rows={[
    ['"Would you use an app that tracks your learning?"', '"Tell me about the last time you followed a course. What happened in week three?"'],
    ['"Is this a good idea?"', '"How do you keep track of what you covered now?"'],
    ['"How much would you pay?"', '"Have you paid for anything to solve this? What?"'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, user interviews">

<Level name="Beginner">

People are polite. Asked whether my idea is good, they say yes. Asked about what they did last week, they
tell the truth. So I ask about specific past behaviour and listen for the workaround, the cost and the
emotion.

```md
Interview guide (20 minutes)
1. Tell me about the last time you started learning something on your own.
2. What happened after the first couple of weeks?
3. How did you keep track? Show me if you can.
4. What did that cost you (time, money, frustration)?
5. What have you tried to fix it?
6. Who else should I talk to?
```

Common mistake: pitching in the first five minutes. Fix: no product mention until the last question, if
at all.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Notes are written straight after, separating what they did (facts), what they said they feel (quotes in
my words), and what I think it means (my interpretation). Patterns across five interviews matter; one
vivid story does not.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Commitment is the best signal: they give time (a follow-up call), reputation (an introduction) or money
(a pre-order). Compliments are noise.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- Friends and family: kind, and therefore unreliable. Use them for practice, not evidence.
- A person who loves the idea but has never tried to solve the problem: the problem may not hurt enough.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- An interviewee who is also a potential customer and a friend: note the bias next to their answers and
  weigh them less.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Consent and privacy: I ask before recording, store notes without unnecessary personal data, and delete
recordings after writing notes.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The goal of an interview is to be wrong early and cheaply.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow
  caption="From interviews to decisions"
  steps={['five interviews', 'facts per person', 'patterns across people', 'assumption confirmed or broken', 'spec revised']}
/>

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Five interviews before code, two after each iteration. Done when at least one assumption changed because
of what someone did, not what they said.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Scope

**Covers:** cutting the first version to the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption, and saying
no to the rest in writing.

<Figure art={`
          must test the riskiest assumption?
                 /                 \\
               yes                  no
                |                    |
          in v1, done           nice to have?
          plainly               /          \\
                              yes           no
                               |             |
                        later list       not doing
`} caption="Every feature idea goes through the same question first" />

<Levels label="Phase 7, scope">

<Level name="Beginner">

Scope is what is in and what is out. A small scope that ships beats a big scope that does not. v1 does
one job: test the riskiest assumption. Everything else goes on a "later" list.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I estimate in days, not weeks, and if v1 is more than about a week of work, I cut until it is. Manual work
behind the scenes is fine for v1 (I can send a weekly summary by hand before automating it).

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Scope also means quality bars, not just features: v1 still has auth that isolates users, accessible forms
and backups, because those are not features, they are the floor from phases 3 and 4.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A feature that one user asks for loudly: on the later list until a second user needs it.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A cut feature that turns out to be required for the riskiest assumption (users will not log without
  reminders): it moves into scope, and something else moves out.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Feature flags let me ship unfinished work dark and turn it on for a few users, which keeps scope small
without long-lived branches.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Feature creep is the most common way a small product dies. Every yes costs the next ten weeks.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

#### Phase 7: Analytics

**Covers:** choosing one metric that reflects real value, instrumenting a few events, and reading the data
without fooling myself.

<Compare
  left="Vanity metric"
  right="Value metric"
  caption="Measure what a user got, not what I did"
  rows={[
    ['Page views', 'Users who logged progress in at least three of the last four weeks'],
    ['Sign-ups', 'Sign-ups who completed the first log within a day'],
    ['Total events', 'Weekly active users doing the core action'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, analytics">

<Level name="Beginner">

An event is a record that something happened: `signed_up`, `logged_progress`. A metric is a number
computed from events. I pick one metric that means the product is working and track it weekly.

```ts
await track({ event: 'logged_progress', userId: user.id, at: new Date().toISOString(), props: { minutes } });
```

```sql
-- weekly active loggers
select date_trunc('week', at) as week, count(distinct user_id)
from event where event = 'logged_progress'
group by 1 order by 1;
```

Common mistake: tracking every click "in case". Fix: a short event list in the spec, each tied to a
question.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Funnels and cohorts: of people who signed up in week 1, how many logged in week 2, 3, 4? A cohort table is
the honest view of retention with small numbers.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

With ten users, statistics are mostly not meaningful. I treat numbers as prompts for questions and pair
them with watching users and talking to them. "Three of ten logged in week four" is a story to
investigate, not a percentage to celebrate.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- My own usage counted: exclude my accounts by id.
- A bot or crawler generating events: server-side events for the core action, not client-side only.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A change that raises the metric by making it easier to game (auto-logging on page load): the metric
  goes up, the value does not. Guard metrics with a second check (minutes logged greater than zero).

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Privacy-respecting by default: no personal data in events, documented retention, and a privacy-friendly
analytics tool or my own table. Cookie banners exist because of tracking choices; fewer trackers, fewer
banners.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Confusing activity with progress is a trap for products and for people. The metric keeps me honest.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow
  caption="From action to decision"
  steps={['user acts', 'server records event', 'weekly query', 'one metric on a dashboard', 'question for the next interview']}
/>

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Define the metric and five events before v1 ships. Done when every iteration in the case study has a
before and after on the same metric.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: SEO

**Covers:** helping the right people find the product through search: crawlable pages, honest titles and
descriptions, fast loading, and content that answers real questions.

<Layers
  caption="SEO from the floor up"
  layers={[
    { name: 'Content', note: 'pages that answer a real question the user types' },
    { name: 'On-page', note: 'one h1, unique title and description, headings, internal links' },
    { name: 'Technical', note: 'canonical URLs, sitemap, robots, status codes, no JS-only content' },
    { name: 'Performance', note: 'Core Web Vitals from phase 4' },
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, SEO">

<Level name="Beginner">

Search engines read the HTML. Each public page needs a unique title, a meta description, one `h1`, and
real text. This site's own build check enforces exactly those, which is a good model to copy.

```html
<title>Track a self-study roadmap week by week | Logbook</title>
<meta name="description" content="Log what you studied each week, see what is left, and keep going past week three." />
<link rel="canonical" href="https://example.com/" />
```

Common mistake: the same title on every page. Fix: a build check that fails on duplicates.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A sitemap lists pages; `robots.txt` says what not to crawl; canonical links pick one URL when several show
the same content. Private app pages (dashboards) are `noindex` and behind auth anyway.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Content is the lever that compounds: a page per question the users actually asked in interviews. Server-
rendered or static HTML is safest for crawling; content that only appears after client JavaScript may be
indexed late or not at all.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A page reachable at two URLs (with and without a trailing slash): one canonical and a redirect.
- A 404 page that returns status 200: search engines index the error page. Return 404.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A staging site indexed because it was public and had no `noindex`: duplicate content and leaked
  features. Staging is behind auth.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A search console account to see real queries and indexing errors, checked monthly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

SEO done well is just clarity for strangers: say what the page is, plainly.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Static marketing pages and a dynamic app can live on the same domain, built differently: the marketing
side static and fast, the app side behind auth.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Add the build checks from this site to the product. Done when every public page passes them and three
pages answer questions from interviews.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Accessibility

**Covers:** making sure people using a keyboard, a screen reader, zoom or high contrast can do the core job,
from the first version.

<Flow
  caption="An accessibility pass for every iteration"
  steps={['keyboard only through the core flow', 'screen reader through the same flow', 'zoom to 200%', 'automated check with axe', 'fix, retest']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, accessibility">

<Level name="Beginner">

Semantic HTML does most of the work: real buttons, labels on inputs, headings in order, landmarks. If I
can do the core job with only a keyboard, I have caught many problems already.

```html
<label for="minutes">Minutes studied</label>
<input id="minutes" name="minutes" type="number" min="1" required />
<button type="submit">Log progress</button>
```

Common mistake: a `div` with a click handler as a button. It is not focusable or announced. Fix: `button`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Errors are announced and tied to fields (`aria-describedby`), focus moves sensibly after actions, and
colour is never the only signal. WCAG 2.2 is the reference standard; automated tools catch only part of
it, so I also test by hand.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Dynamic updates (a streak count changing) use a polite live region so a screen reader hears it without
losing focus. Modals trap focus while open and return it when closed; the native `dialog` element helps.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- An empty state with only an icon: needs text.
- A chart of progress: needs the same data as text or a table.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A form error appearing while the screen reader is reading another field: announce once, not on every
  keystroke.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

An automated check in CI (axe with Playwright) on the core pages; a manual pass before each release; a
contact for accessibility issues.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Accessibility is a feature, not a chore. One of the ten users may need it, and every user benefits from
the clarity it forces.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Accessible components built once in the design system from phase 2 make every new page accessible by
default.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Keyboard and screen-reader pass on v1. Done when the core flow passes both by hand and the automated
check in CI.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Billing with Stripe

**Covers:** taking money safely: hosted checkout, webhooks as the source of truth, test mode, and the
states a subscription goes through.

<Figure art={`
  browser          my server                     Stripe
    |  "Upgrade"  -> create Checkout Session --->  |
    |  <---------- redirect to session.url ------- |
    |  pays on Stripe's hosted page ------------->  |
    |                 webhook: checkout.session.completed
    |                 my server <------------------- |
    |                 verify signature, mark plan,   |
    |                 idempotent on event id         |
    |  success page reads plan from MY database      |
`} caption="The redirect is for the user; the webhook is the truth" />

<Levels label="Phase 7, billing">

<Level name="Beginner">

Stripe Checkout is a payment page hosted by Stripe. My server creates a session, the user pays on Stripe's
page, and Stripe tells my server through a webhook. I never touch card numbers. Test mode lets me try
everything with test cards before real money.

```ts
const session = await stripe.checkout.sessions.create({
  mode: 'subscription',
  line_items: [{ price: process.env.STRIPE_PRICE_ID!, quantity: 1 }],
  customer_email: user.email,
  client_reference_id: user.id,
  success_url: `${APP_URL}/billing/success`,
  cancel_url: `${APP_URL}/billing`,
});
return Response.redirect(session.url!, 303);
```

Common mistake: marking the user as paid on the success page. Anyone can open that URL. Fix: only the
verified webhook changes the plan.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Webhooks must be verified with the signing secret, using the raw request body (a parsed and re-serialized
body fails verification).

```ts
const event = stripe.webhooks.constructEvent(rawBody, req.headers['stripe-signature'], WEBHOOK_SECRET);
if (await seenEvent(event.id)) return ok();          // Stripe may deliver more than once
switch (event.type) {
  case 'checkout.session.completed': await activate(event.data.object); break;
  case 'customer.subscription.updated':
  case 'customer.subscription.deleted': await syncSubscription(event.data.object); break;
  case 'invoice.payment_failed': await flagPastDue(event.data.object); break;
}
```

With a body-parsing middleware in front, verification fails with a signature error. That error message is
the hint to read the raw body.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

A subscription has states (trialing, active, past due, canceled, and others) and my app reacts to each.
The customer portal lets users manage cards and cancel without me building screens. Taxes and invoices
depend on country; I read Stripe's docs for my country before going live.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- Webhook arrives before the user returns to the success page: the page polls my database briefly.
- Webhook arrives twice: idempotent on event id.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- Events arrive out of order (an update before the create was processed): fetch the current object from
  Stripe instead of trusting the event's snapshot, then save.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Separate test and live keys, never in Git. Alert on webhook failures. A reconciliation script compares my
database with Stripe nightly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Money code is where idempotency, verification and audit logs stop being theory.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

  caption="Billing boundaries"
  layers={[
    { name: 'App', note: 'reads plan from my DB, gates features' },
    { name: 'Billing service', note: 'creates sessions, handles webhooks, reconciles' },

/>

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Build it in test mode end to end, including a failed payment with a test card. Done when the plan changes

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Email

**Covers:** sending email that arrives: a transactional provider, an authenticated domain, templates, and
handling bounces and unsubscribes.

<Compare
  left="Transactional"
  right="Marketing"
  caption="Two kinds of email with different rules"
  rows={[
    ['Triggered by the user (password reset, receipt)', 'Sent by me to many (newsletter)'],
    ['Expected, time-sensitive', 'Needs consent and an unsubscribe link'],
    ['Separate stream or subdomain', 'Separate stream, so complaints do not hurt resets'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, email">

<Level name="Beginner">

I send email through a provider's API, not my own mail server. To arrive in inboxes, my domain needs SPF,
DKIM and DMARC records, which prove the provider may send for my domain.

```txt
TXT  @            v=spf1 include:<provider> ~all
TXT  sel._domainkey  (DKIM public key from the provider)
TXT  _dmarc       v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com
```

Common mistake: sending from a free webmail address through an API. It fails authentication and lands in
spam. Fix: my own domain, authenticated.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Emails are sent from a background job (phase 3's queue), not inside the request, with retries. Templates
have a plain-text version.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Bounces and complaints come back as webhooks. Hard bounces stop future sends to that address.
Marketing email has a one-click unsubscribe that works without login.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A user changes email: confirm the new address before switching.
- A password reset requested twice: the newest link wins, older ones expire.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A reset link pre-fetched by a corporate mail scanner, which "uses" a one-time link: the link opens a page
  with a button, and the button uses the token.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Delivery rates, bounces and complaints watched per stream. Templates tested in a few clients.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

An email that does not arrive is a bug users cannot report, because they never saw it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow
  caption="A transactional email"
  steps={['event in app', 'enqueue send', 'worker renders template', 'provider API', 'delivery or bounce webhook', 'update user record']}
/>

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Authenticate the domain first, then one transactional email. Done when a reset email arrives in a major
inbox provider's inbox, not spam, and a bounce is handled.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Onboarding

**Covers:** getting a new user from sign-up to the first moment of value as fast as possible.

<Flow
  caption="Time to first value, the only onboarding metric"
  steps={['sign up', 'one question (what are you learning?)', 'first log, prefilled', 'see it saved', 'value reached']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, onboarding">

<Level name="Beginner">

Onboarding is everything between sign-up and the first useful result. Every extra step loses people. I
remove steps until the core action happens in the first minute.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Empty states teach: an empty dashboard shows one sentence and one button for the first action, not a
blank grid. Sample data, clearly marked, helps the user see what it will look like.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

I measure the funnel from sign-up to first core action and watch three people go through it on a call.
The drop-off point is usually obvious once seen and invisible in the numbers alone.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A user who signs up and leaves before confirming email: let them use the product first, confirm later.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A returning user who skipped onboarding months ago and now sees new features: a short, dismissible
  note, once.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Onboarding changes are iterations with before-and-after on the funnel, like any feature.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The user did not come to use my product; they came to get something done. Get out of the way.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Onboarding state (steps done) lives in the user record, so it survives devices and can be measured.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Watch three sign-ups live. Done when time to first value is measured and halved at least once.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Support

**Covers:** a simple, fast way for users to reach me, and turning their problems into fixes and roadmap
changes.

<Flow
  caption="A support thread's life"
  steps={['user writes', 'reply within a day', 'reproduce', 'fix or explain', 'tell the user', 'log: bug, confusion or request', 'pattern changes the roadmap']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 7, support">

<Level name="Beginner">

Support is one visible contact (an email or a form) and a reply within a day. With ten users, every
message is precious information.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Each thread is tagged: bug, confusion, or request. Confusion is a design bug. Three of the same request is
a roadmap item.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Support data plus traces from phase 4 let me find the exact failing request from a user's description.
The bug log format (error, cause, fix, rule) applies.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A user reporting a security issue: a separate, prioritized path, and thanks.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- A user asking to delete their account and data: a documented process, done fully, including backups
  according to the retention policy.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The plan's proof asks for one testimonial or support thread that changed the roadmap: I keep the thread
and the resulting change linked in the product log.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Every complaint is a free usability test someone ran for me.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Support is an input to the iteration loop, with the same weight as analytics.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Tag every thread from day one. Done when one roadmap change cites a support thread.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: Iteration

**Covers:** three planned cycles of change, each with a hypothesis, a small change and a measured result.

<Figure art={`
  iteration  hypothesis                      change              metric before -> after
  1          reminders bring users back      weekly email        3/10 -> 5/10 active wk4
  2          logging takes too long          one-tap log         ...
  3          ...                             ...                 ...
  (numbers above are a template to fill, not results)
`} caption="The iteration table the case study is written from" />

<Levels label="Phase 7, iteration">

<Level name="Beginner">

An iteration is: a belief about what will help, the smallest change to test it, shipping it, and checking
the metric. Three iterations is the plan's proof, because one could be luck and three show a process.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I write the hypothesis before the change, with what result would prove it wrong. Otherwise any result
looks like success.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

With ten users, A/B tests are not meaningful. I use before-and-after with the same cohort, plus
interviews. I state that limit in the case study.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- An iteration with no effect: still a result, and it goes in the table.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- Two changes shipped together: I cannot tell which worked. One change per iteration.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Each iteration ships behind a flag with a rollback, and is tagged in Git so the case study can link to the
diff.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Progress is learning per week, not features per week.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The product log, the metric and the flags together make iteration cheap and traceable.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

One iteration every two weeks from week 51. Done when the table has three rows with real numbers.

</Level>

</Levels>

**Covers:** choosing a price as an experiment, based on value and on what users already pay for their
workaround.

<Levels label="Phase 7, pricing">

<Level name="Beginner">

Price says who the product is for. Free is a price too, with its own costs (support, abuse, no signal of
value). Asking someone to pay, even a little, is the strongest test of whether the problem hurts.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I anchor to the workaround: if people pay for courses to stay on track, my price is compared with that.
I offer one paid plan, not three, until I know what differs between users.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Costs matter as a floor: AI features have a per-use cost from phase 5. The price must cover heavy users,
or the plan needs a usage cap.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- Users in different countries with very different purchasing power: regional pricing is a later
  decision; note it.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- Changing price for existing users: honour the old price for a stated period, and tell them first.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Prices live in Stripe and config, not hard-coded in the UI.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

A price is a hypothesis, tested like any other.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Plans map to feature flags (entitlements), so changing what a plan includes is data, not code.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Ask five users to pay for the next month. Done when I know how many would, and why the others would not.

</Level>

</Levels>

<Levels label="Phase 7, distribution">

<Level name="Intermediate">

Writing in public compounds: each note about the problem brings a few readers, and some become users. On

#### Phase 7: The case study and the postmortem

**Covers:** writing up the product honestly: the problem, the constraints, the decisions and the outcome,
plus what went wrong.

<Levels label="Phase 7, the case study">

<Level name="Beginner">

A case study is a short story with evidence: what problem, what I built, what happened. It is the main
artefact phase 8 uses.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Decisions are the heart: each one names the options, the choice and the reason. A reader learns how I
think from these, not from the feature list.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The postmortem is blameless and specific: timeline, impact, cause, what I changed, what I would do
differently. It is part of the plan's proof.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- A product that failed (fewer than ten users): the case study is still valuable if it explains why with
  evidence. The gate is not met, and the honest write-up is the next step.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- Users' data in screenshots: anonymize, and ask permission for any quote.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The case study links to the running product, the repo and the tagged iterations.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The goal is to make my judgment visible to a stranger.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The product log, the iteration table and the metric feed the case study directly, so it is written from
records, not memory.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Draft it in week 55 from the product log. Done when someone who has never seen the product can tell me
what problem it solves and what I learned.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 7: the gate

Ship to ten users, and show: ten real users (from the user table, my accounts excluded), three iterations
with a before-and-after on the same metric, usage data, a postmortem, and one testimonial or support
thread that changed the roadmap. Then the AI-assisted half: explain every decision in the case study and
the alternatives I rejected.
