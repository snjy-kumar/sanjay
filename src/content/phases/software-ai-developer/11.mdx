Two weeks, weeks 61 and 62, though the habits run through the capstone after it. A product is not a
repository; it is a loop of problem, user, solution, feedback and iteration. The books are *The Mom
Test* and *Inspired*, with *Continuous Discovery Habits* for depth, plus Google Search Central and
web.dev for distribution. Explanations are my own paraphrase.

#### Phase 11: how I would learn it

Two weeks is short, so I work on the real capstone with real people from day one: interviews in the
first three days, the smallest version shipped by the end of week 61, two more iterations in week 62.
I keep a feedback log (every piece of user input, verbatim where allowed, dated) and a decision log
(every change, the evidence behind it).

#### Phase 11: Problem framing: who has it, how often, what they do today

<Figure art={`
  PROBLEM STATEMENT
  who:     solo consultants with 50+ client documents
  problem: finding what a contract says takes 10-20 min of searching
  how often: several times a week (from interviews)
  today:   folder search, Ctrl+F, asking a colleague
  cost of today: time, missed clauses
  signal it is real: they built their own spreadsheet index
`} caption="A problem statement with evidence in every line. The last line is the strongest kind of evidence." />

<Levels label="Phase 11, problem framing">

<Level name="Beginner">

Problem framing answers three questions before any feature: who has the problem, how often they hit
it, and what they do about it today. If people do nothing about it today, it may not hurt enough to
pay for a solution.

Mistake: starting from my solution ("an AI Q&A for documents") and looking for a problem. Fix: write
the problem statement without mentioning my product.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I write the statement as a hypothesis with evidence slots, and fill the slots from interviews, not
imagination. A workaround people built themselves (a spreadsheet, a script, a habit) is the strongest
evidence: they spent effort on it.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Framing includes who it is not for. A narrow first segment (consultants with many contracts) makes
interviews, messaging and features sharper than "anyone with documents".

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **A problem that is real but rare** (once a year): hard to build habit or revenue on.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **The person with the problem is not the person who pays:** two sets of interviews, two value
  stories.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The problem statement is the first page of the product doc and is revised when evidence changes.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Fall in love with the problem, not the solution.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The problem statement decides the success metric, which decides the analytics events.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the statement, then try to kill it in interviews. Done when every slot has interview evidence.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: User interviews: open questions, observed behaviour, compliments versus commitments

<Compare
  caption="Questions that produce opinions versus questions that produce facts"
  left="Leads to polite noise"
  right="Leads to evidence"
  rows={[
    ['Would you use a tool that does X?', 'When did this last happen? Walk me through it.'],
    ['Do you like this idea?', 'What did you try? What did it cost you?'],
    ['How much would you pay?', 'What are you paying for today to deal with it?'],
    ['Great, thanks!', 'Can I show you a version next week? Who else should I talk to?'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, user interviews">

<Level name="Beginner">

An interview is for learning about someone's life, not pitching. Ask about past, specific behaviour
("the last time you needed a clause from a contract, what did you do?"), not hypothetical futures
("would you use..."). People are kind; they will praise ideas they would never use.

Mistake: describing my idea in the first minute. Fix: do not mention it until the end, if at all.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A compliment ("cool idea") is not evidence. A commitment is: time (a follow-up meeting, a trial with
their real documents), reputation (an introduction to a colleague), or money (a pre-order). I record
which commitments each interview produced.

```md
## Interview 03 — consultant, 6 years, ~80 clients
Last time: searched 4 PDFs for a termination clause, 15 min, gave up, asked partner.
Today's tool: shared folder + memory. Built a spreadsheet of key dates once; abandoned.
Commitment: agreed to try it on 10 real contracts next Tuesday.
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Observed behaviour beats reported behaviour: asking someone to show their screen while they do the
task reveals steps they would never mention. I watch for workarounds and moments of frustration.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **An enthusiastic friend:** the least reliable interviewee; count their data separately.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **A user who commits and then does not show up:** that is evidence too; record it.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Interview notes are stored, tagged and searchable; consent for recording is explicit; personal
details are kept private.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

My job in an interview is to be wrong as early as possible.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Interview tags (problem, workaround, objection) feed the feedback log alongside in-app feedback.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Five interviews in three days, each with notes in the format above. Done when at least two produced a
commitment, or I have changed the problem statement.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Scope: the smallest version that tests the riskiest assumption

<Figure art={`
  assumptions, ranked by (how unsure) x (how fatal if wrong)
  1. users trust AI answers on contracts enough to act   <- riskiest
  2. upload of 50 PDFs works in under 5 minutes
  3. they will pay monthly
  smallest test of #1: Q&A over 10 of their contracts,
  citations jump to the clause; no billing, no teams.
`} caption="Rank the assumptions, then build only what tests the top one." />

<Levels label="Phase 11, scope">

<Level name="Beginner">

The first version should test the assumption most likely to kill the product, with as little building
as possible. Everything else waits.

Mistake: building settings, teams and billing before anyone has used the core.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

For each assumption: how unsure am I, how fatal if wrong. The top one gets the first build. If trust is
the risk, the citation experience is the product; admin features are not.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Some assumptions can be tested without code: a concierge version where I answer questions by hand, a
clickable prototype. Code is the most expensive test.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **An assumption that cannot be tested small** (enterprise compliance): defer the segment, not the
  test.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **The smallest version is too small to be useful:** users cannot judge it. Smallest useful, not
  smallest possible.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Scope is a written list with an explicit "not now" section, reviewed each iteration.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Every feature built before the riskiest test passes is a bet placed blind.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The capstone architecture stays, but feature flags hide everything outside the current scope.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I rank five assumptions and design the smallest test for the top one. Done when the "not now" list is
longer than the "now" list.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Analytics: events, funnels, retention, the one metric

<Flow
  caption="An activation funnel for the capstone"
  steps={['signed up', 'uploaded first document', 'asked first question', 'clicked a citation', 'came back within 7 days']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, analytics">

<Level name="Beginner">

Analytics records what users do as events (`signed_up`, `document_uploaded`, `question_asked`). A
funnel shows how many get from one step to the next. Retention shows how many come back. The one
metric that matters is the single number that best reflects users getting value.

Mistake: tracking everything. Fix: a short event list tied to the funnel.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Events have a name, a user, a tenant, a time and a few properties. I write them from the server where
possible (more reliable than the browser) and keep a tracking plan in the repo.

```ts
track({ name: 'question_asked', userId, tenantId, props: { sources: chunks.length, answered: !refused } });
```

```sql
-- week-1 retention by signup week
SELECT date_trunc('week', u.created_at) cohort,
  avg((EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM events e WHERE e.user_id = u.id
     AND e.created_at BETWEEN u.created_at + interval '1 day' AND u.created_at + interval '8 days'))::int)
FROM users u GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

With ten users, percentages swing wildly; I read individual journeys, not just rates. The one metric
for the capstone: weekly users who asked a question and clicked a citation, since that means they
used and checked an answer.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **My own test account in the data:** exclude internal users.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **Privacy plus analytics:** no document content or question text in analytics events; counts and
  ids only.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A dashboard for the funnel and retention, annotated with releases.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

A metric is a question I ask every week. If I never look at it, it is not my metric.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Analytics events share the request id with traces, so a funnel drop can be investigated in traces.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the tracking plan, instrument the funnel and review it after iteration 1. Done when I can say
where most users drop off.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: SEO and distribution: intent, content, technical foundations

<Layers
  caption="Search distribution in layers"
  layers={[
    { name: 'intent', note: 'what people actually type when they have the problem' },
    { name: 'content', note: 'a page that fully answers that intent' },
    { name: 'technical', note: 'title, description, canonical, sitemap, structured data, fast static HTML' },
    { name: 'links', note: 'others referencing it because it is useful' },
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, SEO and distribution">

<Level name="Beginner">

Distribution is how people find the product. Search is one channel: people with the problem type it
into a search engine; a page that answers well can appear. Technical foundations make pages readable
by crawlers: a unique title and description, a canonical URL, a sitemap, server-rendered content.

Mistake: an app that renders everything client-side with one title for all pages.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

I write two pages for real search intents found in interviews ("find termination clause in
contracts"), each genuinely useful without signing up. The site already does the technical basics; I
check them with Google Search Central's guidance and a Lighthouse SEO audit.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Structured data (JSON-LD) describes the page type to search engines; it helps eligibility for rich
results, but it does not replace useful content.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **Pages behind login:** invisible to search; only public pages count.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **Duplicate pages** (with and without trailing slash): canonical tags pick one.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Search Console verified, sitemap submitted, indexing errors checked monthly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Distribution is part of the product, not something after it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Public content pages are static, fast and separate from the app shell.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Two intent pages and one other channel (a community where the users already are, with their rules
respected). Done when I know which brought the first users.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Onboarding: the first five minutes, empty states, time to value

<Figure art={`
  minute 0   sign up (email only)
  minute 1   empty state: "Upload a contract, or try our 3 sample contracts"
  minute 2   sample loaded; suggested question shown
  minute 3   answer with citation; citation opens the clause   <- value
  minute 5   prompt: upload your own
`} caption="Time to value is the minute where the user first sees the product work on something they care about." />

<Levels label="Phase 11, onboarding">

<Level name="Beginner">

Onboarding is the path from sign-up to the first moment of value. Empty states (screens with no data
yet) are where new users get lost; each should say what to do next and offer a shortcut.

Mistake: a blank dashboard after sign-up.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Sample data lets users see value before uploading their own. A suggested first question removes the
blank-prompt problem. I measure time from sign-up to first cited answer.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Every extra field at sign-up loses people; ask later, when it is needed (company name at invoice
time).

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **Upload fails on a scanned PDF with no text:** the empty state explains and suggests a fix.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **Sample documents in analytics:** questions on samples are activation, not retention; tag them.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The onboarding path has an end-to-end Playwright test that runs on every deploy.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The first five minutes decide whether there is a sixth.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Sample data is a seeded, read-only tenant shared safely, never mixed with user data.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I watch three people onboard without helping. Done when time to value is measured and the worst
moment is fixed.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Billing: pricing, trials, metering, invoices, failed payments

<Flow
  caption="A subscription's life"
  steps={['trial starts', 'trial ending email', 'payment method added', 'invoice paid', 'payment fails: retry + email', 'grace period', 'downgrade or cancel']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, billing">

<Level name="Beginner">

Billing is pricing (what it costs), the trial (free period), metering (usage counts from phase 8),
invoices, and failed payments. A payment provider handles cards; my app reacts to its webhooks.

Mistake: granting access on the redirect after checkout. Fix: grant access on the verified webhook.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

```ts
// webhook: verify signature, then handle idempotently by event id
const event = stripe.webhooks.constructEvent(rawBody, sig, process.env.STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET!);
if (await seen(event.id)) return ok();
if (event.type === 'invoice.payment_failed') await startGracePeriod(event.data.object.customer);
```

Test mode is enough for the capstone; the webhook handling is the learning.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Pricing for an AI product must cover variable cost: a plan includes a usage allowance tied to the cost
model, with clear behaviour at the limit.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **Webhooks out of order:** fetch the current state from the provider rather than trusting order.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **Downgrade with usage over the new plan's limit:** define it (read-only until under limit) before
  it happens.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Billing events are audited; a daily job reconciles subscriptions with the provider.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Billing bugs are trust bugs.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Billing is its own module, owning subscription state; others ask it "is this tenant entitled?".

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I build the pricing page and test-mode checkout, then simulate a failed payment. Done when the grace
and downgrade paths are tested.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Support: docs, macros, escalation, feedback capture

<Figure art={`
  user question --> docs search (self-serve)
         | not solved
         v
  support form --> triage tag --> macro reply (known issue)
                         |
                         +--> escalate: bug -> issue, with trace id
                         +--> feedback -> feedback log
`} caption="A documented support process, small enough for one person." />

<Levels label="Phase 11, support">

<Level name="Beginner">

Support is how users get help and how I learn what is broken. Docs answer common questions; macros are
saved replies for frequent cases; escalation moves real bugs into the issue tracker; every request is
also feedback.

Mistake: answering from memory each time. Fix: a runbook and macros.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The support form captures the request id or trace id automatically, so I can see what happened. Each
request gets a tag: question, bug, feature request, billing.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Support data feeds the roadmap: count tags weekly; a tag that grows is a product problem, not a
support problem.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **A request containing a customer's confidential document:** handle under the same privacy rules.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **An AI-generated support reply:** a draft for me, never sent automatically (what not to delegate).

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A response-time target, a runbook, and macros reviewed monthly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Every support request is a free user interview.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Support tooling links tickets to traces, users and feedback.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the runbook and three macros, then handle one real request with them. Done when the process
was followed and documented.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Iteration: ship, measure, learn, decide

<Compare
  caption="Three iterations, each with a decision"
  left="Iteration"
  right="What decides the next"
  rows={[
    ['1: core Q&A with citations', 'Do users ask a second question? Funnel and interviews'],
    ['2: fix the biggest drop-off', 'Did that step improve? Same metric, before and after'],
    ['3: the most requested change that fits the problem', 'Did the one metric move? Continue, change or stop'],
  ]}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, iteration">

<Level name="Beginner">

An iteration is ship, measure, learn, decide. Each ends with an explicit decision: continue, change
direction, or stop.

Mistake: iterating without a decision; features pile up.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Before shipping, I write what I expect to change and by how much. After, I compare. The decision log
records it.

```md
2026-xx-xx  Iteration 2: show "ask a follow-up" chips after an answer.
Expect: second-question rate up. Before 3/10 users. After 6/10. Keep.
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

With few users, qualitative evidence carries more weight; I combine numbers with what users said and
did, and I say so in the log.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **No change at all:** a learning, not a failure; the hypothesis was wrong.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **A metric moves for an unrelated reason** (a holiday week): note context in the log.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Every iteration ships behind a flag with a rollback.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Stopping is a valid outcome.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Flags, analytics and the decision log make iteration cheap.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Three iterations in two weeks. Done when each has an expectation, a result and a decision in the log.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: Launch: a checklist, a rollback plan, a communication plan

<Flow
  caption="Launch day, in order"
  steps={['checklist green', 'backups verified', 'flag on for 10%', 'watch dashboards 1 hour', '100%', 'announce', 'watch support and errors 48 hours']}
/>

<Levels label="Phase 11, launch">

<Level name="Beginner">

A launch is a deploy plus people arriving. The checklist covers the app (the full-app checklist), the
rollback (how to undo in minutes), and communication (who is told what, when).

Mistake: launching on a Friday evening with no one watching.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Rollback plan: the flag to turn off, the previous release to redeploy, the migration that is
backward-compatible so code can roll back without data loss.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Communication plan: the announcement, the status page message if something breaks, and the reply to
early users.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

- **A spike in sign-ups exceeds provider rate limits:** queue, cap and communicate.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

- **Rollback needed after a migration that dropped a column:** expand-and-contract migrations prevent
  this.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A launch retrospective within a week.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

A launch is an operation, planned like one.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Launch uses the same flags, dashboards and runbooks built in earlier phases.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the launch checklist and rehearse the rollback. Done when the rehearsal took minutes.

</Level>

</Levels>

#### Phase 11: the build and the gate

The build puts the capstone in front of ten real users for three iterations, with a feedback log, a
decision log, analytics, a pricing page and a support runbook, and one product metric measured before
and after.

The gate:

- A user changed my roadmap, and the decision log shows how.
- A metric moved in a direction I can explain.
- A support request was handled with the documented process.
- I can say what I would do with ten more users and what I would stop doing.

Done when each point links to an entry in the feedback or decision log.
