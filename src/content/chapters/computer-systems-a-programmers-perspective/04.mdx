## 4.1 The Y86-64 Instruction Set Architecture

### In one sentence

Y86-64 is a deliberately small subset of x86-64: fifteen registers, a
handful of move and arithmetic ops, jumps, call and ret, push and pop, and
four status codes, all encoded in a regular byte-oriented format I can
decode with a pencil.

### The idea

#### 4.1.1 Programmer-Visible State

What a Y86-64 program can see:

- **Fifteen registers:** `%rax` through `%r14`, with `%rsp` used as the
  stack pointer by push, pop, call and ret. There is no `%r15`; the value
  0xF in a register field means "no register".
- **Three condition codes:** ZF, SF, OF (no CF; Y86-64 arithmetic is
  signed-oriented for branches).
- **Program counter (PC):** the address of the next instruction.
- **Memory:** a single byte-addressable array, as in chapter 3.
- **Status code:** a four-valued enum that says whether the machine is
  running or has stopped for a reason.

#### 4.1.2 Y86-64 Instructions

The instruction set is small enough to fit on one page:

<Trace columns={["Group", "Instructions"]} rows={[
  ["Stops", "halt, nop"],
  ["Moves", "rrmovq, irmovq, rmmovq, mrmovq"],

  ["Arithmetic", "addq, subq, andq, xorq"],

Semantics match the x86-64 cousins from chapter 3, with two simplifications:
only quad-word data, and `subq rA, rB` computes R[rB] ← R[rB] − R[rA]
(destination last, same as AT&T). There are no shift instructions, no
byte or long operations, and no floating point.

#### 4.1.3 Instruction Encoding

Every instruction starts with a one-byte **icode/ifun** pair: high nibble
is the opcode class, low nibble the function (condition or ALU op). Optional
fields follow in a fixed order: a register byte (two 4-bit register ids),
an 8-byte constant or destination (little-endian).

I wrote an encoder and checked the bytes:

```text
assembly                         hex                                      len
halt                             00                                       1
nop                              10                                       1
irmovq 256, %rsp                 30 f4 00 01 00 00 00 00 00 00            10
irmovq 3, %rax                   30 f0 03 00 00 00 00 00 00 00            10
addq %rax, %rbx                  60 03                                    2
rmmovq %rbx, 0, %rsp             40 34 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00            10
jmp 23                           70 17 00 00 00 00 00 00 00               9
pushq %rax                       a0 0f                                    2
popq %rax                        b0 0f                                    2
ret                              90                                       1
cmovle %rax, %rbx                21 03                                    2
```

A small program that sums 1 through 10 encodes to 53 bytes; the loop sits
at address `0x28` and the `jne` carries that absolute address:

```text
0x00: irmovq 512, %rsp
0x0a: irmovq 0, %rax
0x14: irmovq 10, %rdi
0x1e: irmovq 1, %rcx
0x28: addq %rdi, %rax     # loop
0x2a: subq %rcx, %rdi
0x2c: jne 0x28
```

#### 4.1.4 Y86-64 Exceptions

An exception in Y86-64 sets the status to ADR or INS (or HLT for `halt`)
and stops fetching. There are no privilege levels, no handler tables and
no resumption; the simulator just reports the status. That is enough to
practise precise exception handling in the pipeline later: an exception
detected in a deep stage must not let earlier (younger) instructions
change programmer-visible state.

#### 4.1.5 Y86-64 Programs

Y86-64 assembly looks like a trimmed x86-64. A function that adds an array
of longs would use `mrmovq` to load, `addq` to accumulate, `irmovq` for
constants, and `call`/`ret` with `%rsp`. The Architecture Lab ships an
assembler `yas` and a simulator `yis` that run these programs; on this page
I use my own encoder and reason about the bytes directly.

#### 4.1.6 Some Y86-64 Instruction Details

A few details that matter for the hardware:

- `rrmovq` and the six conditional moves share icode 2; ifun selects the
  condition. Unconditional move is ifun 0.
- `pushq` and `popq` adjust `%rsp` by ±8 and move data; their encodings
  leave the second register nibble as F.
- `call` pushes the return address (the next PC) and jumps; `ret` pops into
  the PC. Same stack discipline as chapter 3.
- Jumps and `call` encode an absolute destination, not a relative offset.
  That simplifies the SEQ hardware at the cost of position dependence.

Byte-length formula: 1 (always) + 1 if the instruction needs a register byte + 8 if it needs a constant. So halt is 1, addq is 2, jmp is 9, irmovq is 10. No Y86-64 instruction is any other length, which is why fetch's valP HCL is three terms long.


### One more encoding drill

I encode a five-instruction prologue the way a compiler would for a
small leaf function that uses `%rbx`:

```text
0x00: 30 f4 00 01 00 00 00 00 00 00   irmovq 0x100, %rsp
0x0a: a0 3f                           pushq  %rbx
0x0c: 30 f3 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00   irmovq 0, %rbx
0x16: b0 3f                           popq   %rbx
0x18: 90                              ret
```

Total 25 bytes. Tracing it on PIPE: the `pushq` is a store to memory and a
write to `%rsp`; the next `irmovq` does not depend on `%rsp`, so no stall;
the `popq` depends on `%rsp` from `pushq`, but by the time `popq` decodes,
`pushq` has already produced the new `%rsp` in execute and can forward it.
The `ret` will stall until its return address (whatever was on the stack
above our pushed `%rbx`) is read in memory. Even a trivial prologue shows
every major PIPE mechanism except a mispredicted branch.


The same prologue also shows why leaf functions matter: a leaf that needs
no callee-saved register can skip the push/pop pair entirely, removing a
store, a load and the `ret`'s dependence on a stack slot it just wrote.
Compilers know this; chapter 5 will count the cycles. After PIPE, I can
see those cycles as concrete stalls and memory ops rather than as a vague
"function-call overhead". The habit of translating "overhead" into named
hazard events is the lasting payoff of the chapter. I use it every time a profiler points at a branchy hot spot or a surprising load-use dependence chain.

### Worked example

Decode `60 03`:

1. High nibble 6 → arithmetic; low nibble 0 → `addq`.
2. Register byte `03` → rA = 0 (`%rax`), rB = 3 (`%rbx`).
3. Instruction is `addq %rax, %rbx`, length 2.

Decode `30 f4 00 01 00 00 00 00 00 00`:

1. icode 3 → `irmovq`.
2. Register byte `f4` → rA unused, rB = 4 (`%rsp`).
3. Next 8 bytes little-endian = `0x100`.
4. Instruction is `irmovq 0x100, %rsp`, length 10.

### Step by step

To encode an instruction:

1. Look up icode and ifun; write that byte.
2. If it needs registers, write the register byte (F for unused).
3. If it needs a constant or destination, write 8 little-endian bytes.
4. Sum the lengths; that is how far to advance the PC.

### Common mistake

Forgetting that Y86-64 destinations are absolute. Copying an x86-64 relative
`jmp` encoding into a Y86-64 program jumps into nowhere. Another: using
`%r15`, which does not exist; the id F means "no register", not `%r15`.

### In real systems

Real ISAs (x86-64, ARM64, RISC-V) use denser and more irregular encodings,
but the same ideas: an opcode, register fields, immediates, and a small
set of formats. RISC-V's fixed 32-bit formats are closest in spirit to
Y86-64's regularity; x86-64's variable length is what chapter 3's
`objdump` listings showed.

## 4.2 Logic Design and the Hardware Control Language HCL

### In one sentence

Hardware is built from gates that compute boolean functions without memory,
and from memory elements that hold values between clock edges; HCL is a
notation for describing the combinational part cleanly.

### The idea

#### 4.2.1 Logic Gates

Three gates are enough for every boolean function: AND, OR and NOT. Their
outputs respond continuously to their inputs (after a tiny gate delay).
They have no memory: the output is a pure function of the current inputs.

#### 4.2.2 Combinational Circuits and HCL Boolean Expressions

Wiring gates together without cycles gives a **combinational circuit**.
HCL writes the same thing as a C-like boolean expression:

```text
bool eq = (a && b) || (!a && !b);   # a equals b for one-bit signals
```

#### 4.2.3 Word-Level Combinational Circuits and HCL Integer Expressions

Real machines move 64-bit words. A word-level multiplexor selects one of
several words based on a control signal. In HCL:

```text
word Out = [
    s == 1 : B;
    1      : A;
];
```

The bracket is a case list: the first true condition's value wins. I built
the one-bit mux and lifted it to eight bits in Python to check:

```text
mux2(0, 0b10110010, 0b01001101) = 0b10110010
mux2(1, 0b10110010, 0b01001101) = 0b1001101
```

An ALU is a word-level circuit with a control code:

```text
word alu_out = [
    alufun == ADD : A + B;
    alufun == SUB : B - A;
    alufun == AND : A & B;
    alufun == XOR : A ^ B;
];
```

```text
alu(0,3,5) -> 8     # add
alu(1,3,5) -> 2     # sub (B - A)
alu(2,240,15) -> 0  # and
alu(3,255,15) -> 240 # xor
```

#### 4.2.4 Set Membership

HCL has a set-membership test that keeps instruction decoding readable:

```text
bool need_regids = icode in { RRMOVQ, IRMOVQ, RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ,
                              OPq, PUSHQ, POPQ };
```

That one line replaces a long OR of equalities, and it is exactly how SEQ
and PIPE decide whether to read a register byte from the instruction
stream.

#### 4.2.5 Memory and Clocking

Combinational circuits cannot store information. Storage needs a **clock**
and devices that load a new value only on a clock edge:

- **Registers** (pipeline registers, the program counter, the condition
  codes) load on every rising edge, or on an edge when an enable is high.
- **Memories** (register file, data memory, instruction memory) have
  address and data inputs; writes are committed on the clock edge, reads
  are combinational (the value appears after a short delay).

The discipline that makes SEQ and PIPE workable: **all combinational logic
settles between clock edges; all state changes happen at the edge**. If the
clock is too fast, the logic does not settle and the machine computes
garbage. That is the speed limit of a stage, and the reason a pipeline's
clock cannot be faster than its slowest stage.

<Figure art={`
  combinational logic          state elements
  (gates, ALUs, muxes)         (registers, memories)

   inputs ---> [ logic ] ---> values ready
                                |
                     clock edge |  copies values into state
                                v
                              new inputs for next cycle
`} caption="The clocked-circuit contract. Combinational logic has most of the cycle to settle; the rising edge snapshots its outputs into registers that feed the next cycle." />

### Worked example

HCL for "next PC" in a simplified SEQ, choosing among the sequential next
instruction, a jump target and a return address:

```text
word new_pc = [
    icode == CALL : valC;               # dest from instruction
    icode in { JXX } && Cnd : valC;     # taken branch
    icode == RET : valM;                # from stack
    1 : valP;                           # default: PC + length
];
```

### Step by step

To write an HCL case expression:

1. List every condition that should produce a distinct value, most specific first.
2. End with `1 : default` so the result is always defined.
3. Use `in { ... }` for opcode sets; it reads as the prose.

### Common mistake

Writing a cyclic combinational circuit (an output wired back to an input
without a register). In real hardware that oscillates or settles to a
useless value; in the HCL tools it is rejected. Another: forgetting the
default case, so some input combinations produce no value.


HCL integer expressions also cover the arithmetic the ALU needs. Unlike C,
HCL addition on words is just another combinational function; there is no
undefined overflow. Subtraction is addition of a negated operand. Bitwise
ops match chapter 2. The condition-code outputs are separate boolean
signals computed from the result and the operands (for OF, from the carry
into and out of the sign bit). When I write `set_cc = icode in { OPQ }` I
am saying "only arithmetic instructions update the flags"; moves and
address calculations leave them alone, matching x86-64 behaviour for the
corresponding instructions.

### In real systems

Hardware description languages used in industry (SystemVerilog, VHDL, Chisel)
are richer cousins of HCL. The same split between combinational always-blocks
and clocked registers is the first thing every digital design course
teaches. The CS:APP HCL subset is enough to specify a CPU and small enough
to reason about on paper.

## 4.3 Sequential Y86-64 Implementations

### In one sentence

SEQ is a processor that does one instruction completely before starting the
next, organised as six stages that share a single clock edge at the end, so
every value I draw on the whiteboard is also a wire in the machine.

### The idea

#### 4.3.1 Organizing Processing into Stages

Six stages cover every Y86-64 instruction:

1. **Fetch.** Read the instruction bytes at the PC; extract icode, ifun,
   optional registers rA/rB, optional constant valC; compute valP = PC +
   length.
2. **Decode.** Read up to two operands from the register file, producing
   valA and valB. For instructions that need `%rsp`, decode reads it even
   when the register byte says F.
3. **Execute.** The ALU computes a value (valE) and optionally sets the
   condition codes. For address calculations (rmmovq, mrmovq, push, pop,
   call) the ALU adds an offset to a base. For conditional moves and jumps
   a condition signal Cnd is computed from ifun and the flags.
4. **Memory.** Read or write data memory: `mrmovq` and `popq`/`ret` read
   (valM); `rmmovq`, `pushq` and `call` write.
5. **Write back.** Up to two register writes: valE and/or valM into the
   destinations the instruction updates.
6. **PC update.** Set the PC to valP, valC or valM depending on the
   instruction and Cnd.

<Flow steps={["Fetch: bytes at PC -> icode, ifun, rA, rB, valC, valP", "Decode: register file -> valA, valB", "Execute: ALU -> valE, Cnd; maybe set CC", "Memory: data memory <-> valM", "Write back: valE / valM into registers", "PC update: PC <- valP or valC or valM"]} caption="SEQ's six stages. In the hardware they are all combinational paths fed by the old PC and register file; the clock edge at the end writes the new PC, registers, memory and flags together." />

#### 4.3.2 SEQ Hardware Structure

The hardware is one long combinational path from the old PC to all the
next-state values, plus the register file, data memory, condition-code
register and PC register that load on the clock. Instruction memory is
read-only in SEQ (a program is loaded before the run). Control signals
(ALU function, ALU A/B selects, memory read/write enables, register write
enables, PC select) are combinational functions of icode and ifun, written
in HCL.

#### 4.3.3 SEQ Timing

Because everything is combinational until the clock edge, the clock period
must be longer than the longest path through all six stages. That path
usually goes through fetch → decode → execute → memory → write-back setup.
A typical textbook estimate puts that around 600 ps, so SEQ's instruction
throughput is about one instruction every 600 ps. Pipelining will cut the
period to roughly the longest single stage.

No instruction's side effects become visible until the edge. That is why
SEQ has no hazard problems: the next instruction sees a fully updated
machine.

#### 4.3.4 SEQ Stage Implementations

The HCL for each stage is a page of case expressions. The patterns that
matter:

**Fetch.**

```text
bool need_regids = icode in { RRMOVQ, OPQ, PUSHQ, POPQ,
                              IRMOVQ, RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ };
bool need_valC   = icode in { IRMOVQ, RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ, JXX, CALL };
word valP = PC + 1 + need_regids + 8 * need_valC;
```

**Decode.** Select which registers to read:

```text
word srcA = [
    icode in { RRMOVQ, RMMOVQ, OPQ, PUSHQ } : rA;
    icode in { POPQ, RET } : RRSP;
    1 : RNONE;
];
word srcB = [
    icode in { OPQ, RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ } : rB;
    icode in { PUSHQ, POPQ, CALL, RET } : RRSP;
    1 : RNONE;
];
```

**Execute.** ALU inputs and function:

```text
word aluA = [
    icode in { RRMOVQ, OPQ } : valA;
    icode in { IRMOVQ, RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ } : valC;
    icode in { CALL, PUSHQ } : -8;
    icode in { RET, POPQ } : 8;
];
word aluB = [
    icode in { RMMOVQ, MRMOVQ, OPQ, CALL,
               PUSHQ, RET, POPQ } : valB;
    icode in { RRMOVQ, IRMOVQ } : 0;
];
```

**Memory.** Address and data selects; read/write enables from icode.

**Write back.** Two ports (Y86-64 needs them for `popq`, which writes both
the destination and `%rsp`):

```text
word dstE = [
    icode in { RRMOVQ } && Cnd : rB;
    icode in { IRMOVQ, OPQ } : rB;
    icode in { PUSHQ, POPQ, CALL, RET } : RRSP;
    1 : RNONE;
];
word dstM = [
    icode in { MRMOVQ, POPQ } : rA;
    1 : RNONE;
];
```

**PC update.** The next-PC HCL from 4.2.

### Step by step

To trace any instruction through SEQ:

1. Fetch: write down icode, ifun, registers, valC, valP.
2. Decode: which srcA/srcB? Read those registers.
3. Execute: which aluA/aluB/alufun? Compute valE and Cnd; note whether CC updates.
4. Memory: read, write or neither? Note valM.
5. Write back: which dstE/dstM? Which values?
6. PC: valP, valC or valM?

### Common mistake

Thinking SEQ takes six clock cycles per instruction. It takes one: all
stages are combinational in the same cycle. The six-stage vocabulary is
about the logical organisation, reused when we pipeline.


Looking at all four ALU ops and the four memory-touching instructions side by side shows the pattern SEQ relies on: the ALU either computes a program value or an address, and memory either idles, loads into valM, or stores valA. Everything else is steering.

Timing discipline in SEQ also explains a rule chapter 5 will use: a single-cycle machine cannot be faster than its longest instruction. Memory access and the ALU add dominate; instruction-memory fetch is on the same path. That is why even SEQ implementers care about cache hits, and why PIPE's stage balance starts by asking which of those cuts is the slowest.

### In real systems

No modern CPU is a SEQ, but every CPU's pipeline stages are the SEQ stages
cut apart. Understanding SEQ first is why PIPE's diagrams are readable
instead of mystical. Microcontrollers and teaching cores (PicoRV32,
many J. J. Hennessy/Patterson examples) are still essentially SEQ or
shallow pipelines.

<details>
<summary>Which stages does nop use for anything besides advancing the PC?</summary>

Only fetch (to know it is a nop and compute valP). Decode, execute, memory
and write back are idle; PC update sets PC to valP.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does popq need two register-write ports?</summary>

It writes the popped value into rA and the new stack pointer into `%rsp`
in the same instruction.

</details>

## 4.4 General Principles of Pipelining

### In one sentence

Pipelining overlaps the stages of successive instructions so that
throughput rises toward one instruction per stage delay, while latency of
any one instruction stays about the same and new hazards appear.

### The idea

#### 4.4.1 Computational Pipelines

A laundry analogy: wash, dry, fold. Doing one load completely before the
next takes 3 hours per load. Starting a new load every hour, with three
loads in flight, finishes one load per hour after the pipeline fills.
Throughput triples; the time from a shirt going in to coming out is still
about three hours.

#### 4.4.2 A Detailed Look at Pipeline Operation

In a processor the stages are fetch, decode, execute, memory, write back
(PC update folds into fetch of the next instruction). Ideal timing with a
300 ps stage budget:

<Figure art={`
  time -->
  I1: [F][D][E][M][W]
  I2:    [F][D][E][M][W]
  I3:       [F][D][E][M][W]
  I4:          [F][D][E][M][W]

  throughput once full: 1 instruction / stage-delay
  latency of I1: still 5 stage-delays
`} caption="A five-stage pipeline in steady state. A new instruction enters every cycle; five instructions are in flight; each instruction still takes five cycles to finish." />

#### 4.4.3 Limitations of Pipelining

Three limits:

1. **Non-uniform stage delays.** The clock must wait for the slowest
   stage. Unbalanced stages waste time in the fast ones.
2. **Overhead of pipeline registers.** Each stage boundary adds setup and
   hold time. Too many stages and the overhead dominates.
3. **Hazards.** Dependencies between instructions force stalls or extra
   hardware, cutting the ideal throughput.

#### 4.4.4 Pipelining a System with Feedback

A feedback path (an instruction that computes the next PC, or that produces
a value a young instruction needs) couples stages. Pure feed-forward
pipelines are easy; processors are not pure. The rest of the chapter is
about making feedback safe and fast.

### Worked example

SEQ clock 600 ps → 1.67 GHz, throughput 1.67 GIPS.
PIPE with five balanced 120 ps stages plus 20 ps register overhead →
140 ps clock → 7.1 GHz ideal, throughput 7.1 GIPS. Real PIPE with stalls
and mispredictions might average 0.5 to 0.8 instructions per cycle, still
several times SEQ.

### Step by step

To estimate pipeline speedup:

1. Find SEQ's longest path; that is T_seq.
2. Find the longest stage after splitting; add register overhead; that is T_pipe.
3. Ideal speedup is T_seq / T_pipe.
4. Multiply by the realised instructions-per-cycle after hazards (less than 1).

### Common mistake

Claiming pipelining makes each instruction faster. It makes the machine
complete more instructions per second; each instruction still walks every
stage.

### In real systems

Modern out-of-order cores have 10 to 20+ pipeline stages, issue several
instructions per cycle, and rename registers to remove false dependencies.
The hazards in this chapter are the same hazards those machines solve with
more hardware. Understanding PIPE is the prerequisite for reading about
Tomasulo's algorithm or a load/store queue.


A useful comparison across generations of machines I have used: a teaching
SEQ-like microcontroller finishes about one instruction every few cycles
at a low clock; a phone-class out-of-order core finishes two to four per
cycle at a few GHz; a big server core can be higher still on friendly
code. The gap is not mainly better silicon speed. It is deeper pipelines,
wider issue, better predictors and caches that keep the pipe full. This
chapter is the first rung of that ladder: once PIPE's hazards are
intuitive, every later idea is "the same hazard, more machinery".

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Does pipelining reduce the latency of one instruction?</summary>

Not materially; it raises throughput. Latency stays about the number of
stages times the new (shorter) clock.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Name the three limits on pipeline performance.</summary>

Unbalanced stages, pipeline-register overhead, and hazards.

</details>

## 4.5 Pipelined Y86-64 Implementations

### In one sentence

PIPE cuts SEQ into five stages separated by pipeline registers, predicts
the next PC up front, then spends most of its control logic on detecting
and repairing the data and control hazards that prediction and overlapping
create.

### The idea

#### 4.5.1 SEQ+: Rearranging the Computation Stages

Before inserting registers, SEQ is rearranged so that PC update happens at
the start of the next instruction's fetch rather than at the end of the
current one. The next PC is computed in the previous instruction and held
in the PC register. That makes the PC just another pipeline register.

#### 4.5.2 Inserting Pipeline Registers

Five registers sit between stages: F (holds prediction of next PC), D
(holds fetched instruction fields), E (holds decoded values), M (holds
execute results), W (holds memory results). Their fields have names like
`D_icode`, `E_valA`, `M_Cnd`, `W_valM`. On every clock edge the whole
bundle moves one stage forward.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: "F", note: "predicted PC" },
  { name: "D", note: "icode, ifun, rA, rB, valC, valP, status" },
  { name: "E", note: "icode, valA, valB, valC, dstE, dstM, srcA, srcB, status" },
  { name: "M", note: "icode, Cnd, valE, valA, dstE, dstM, status" },
  { name: "W", note: "icode, valE, valM, dstE, dstM, status" },
]} caption="PIPE's pipeline registers from fetch to write back. Each holds just enough of the instruction's state for the stages that follow." />

#### 4.5.3 Rearranging and Relabeling Signals

Signal names gain a stage prefix so that `valA` in decode is `d_valA` (the
combinational output) feeding `E_valA` (the registered value). Reading PIPE
HCL is mostly tracking those prefixes.

#### 4.5.4 Next PC Prediction

Fetch must choose a next PC every cycle, before the current instruction has
decoded. PIPE's prediction:

- For most instructions: predict valP (fall through).
- For `call` and unconditional `jmp`: predict the target valC (known in fetch).
- For conditional jumps: predict **taken** (the target valC).
- For `ret`: cannot predict easily; PIPE stalls until `ret` reaches memory
  and produces valM.

Wrong predictions are repaired when the branch resolves in execute: the
wrongly fetched instructions are turned into bubbles, and the correct PC
is fed into F.

#### 4.5.5 Pipeline Hazards

Two families:

**Data hazards.** An instruction needs a value that a previous instruction
has not yet written back. Example:

```text
addq %rax, %rbx    # produces new %rbx in write back
subq %rbx, %rcx    # needs %rbx in decode
```

Without help, `subq` would read the old `%rbx`. **Forwarding** (bypassing)
fixes most cases: the pipeline notices that `E_dstE` matches `d_srcA` and
routes `e_valE` (or `M_valE`, or `W_valE`/`W_valM`) into the decode stage's
valA/valB muxes. The one case forwarding cannot fix is a **load-use
hazard**:

```text
mrmovq 0(%rax), %rbx   # value arrives only in memory stage
subq %rbx, %rcx        # needs it in execute the next cycle
```

PIPE stalls the `subq` for one cycle (inserts a bubble into execute) so
that the load's valM can be forwarded from memory.

**Control hazards.** A jump's direction or a return address is not known
at fetch. Prediction plus squash-on-wrong handles conditional jumps;
`ret` stalls until the address is ready.

<Figure art={`
  cycle:     1    2    3    4    5    6
  addq:     [F]  [D]  [E]  [M]  [W]
  subq:          [F]  [D]  [E]  [M]  [W]
                       ^
                       decode of subq gets rbx via
                       forwarding from E_valE of addq

  load-use:
  mrmovq:   [F]  [D]  [E]  [M]  [W]
  subq:          [F]  [D]  [D]  [E]  [M]  [W]
                            stall: bubble into E
`} caption="Forwarding saves the add-then-sub case. A load followed by a use must stall one cycle because the data exists only after the memory stage." />

#### 4.5.6 Exception Handling

An exception can be detected in fetch (bad PC, bad instruction), in memory
(bad address) or by `halt`. Because younger instructions may already be in
the pipeline, PIPE transfers a status code along with each instruction and
only lets the exception take effect when that instruction reaches write
back. Instructions after the excepting one are cancelled. That gives
**precise exceptions**: the machine looks as if it stopped at the faulting
instruction, with every older instruction complete and every younger one
undone.

#### 4.5.7 PIPE Stage Implementations

Each stage's HCL is SEQ's HCL plus the forwarding muxes and the prediction
logic. The decode-stage forwarding mux is the heart of it:

```text
word d_valA = [
    D_icode in { CALL, JXX } : D_valP;         # return addr / fallthrough
    d_srcA == e_dstE : e_valE;                 # forward from execute
    d_srcA == M_dstM : m_valM;                 # forward from memory (load)
    d_srcA == M_dstE : M_valE;
    d_srcA == W_dstM : W_valM;
    d_srcA == W_dstE : W_valE;
    1 : d_rvalA;                               # from register file
];
```

Priority matters: the newest matching producer wins.

#### 4.5.8 Pipeline Control Logic

Four boolean signals per pipeline register: whether to stall it, whether
to bubble it (inject a nop), or to let it load normally. The combinations
PIPE needs:

  ["Load-use hazard", "stall", "stall", "bubble", "normal", "normal"],
  ["Mispredicted branch", "normal", "bubble", "bubble", "normal", "normal"],
  ["ret in progress", "stall", "bubble", "normal", "normal", "normal"],
  ["Exception reaching W", "bubble", "bubble", "bubble", "bubble", "normal"],
]} caption="PIPE control combinations. Stall holds a register's value; bubble overwrites it with a nop-like instruction; normal loads from the previous stage." />

When two special cases collide (a load-use hazard and a ret in flight, for
example), the control logic has a priority: the book works through the
combinations so that a single HCL block produces the right enables. The
Architecture Lab's testing regime catches the priority bugs.

#### 4.5.9 Performance Analysis

Ideal PIPE: CPI = 1. Real CPI is 1 plus penalties:

- Load-use: about 0.25 of instructions are loads, maybe 0.3 of those have
  a use next → roughly 0.075 cycles per instruction of stall.
- Mispredicted branches: about 0.2 of instructions are conditional jumps,
  prediction accuracy maybe 0.6 with the simple taken strategy → 0.2 × 0.4
  × 2-cycle penalty ≈ 0.16.
- `ret` stalls: rare in integer code, a few hundredths.

A rough CPI of 1.25 means PIPE at a 140 ps clock delivers about 5.7 GIPS,
still several times SEQ's 1.67. Better branch predictors (two-bit counters,
return stacks) cut the control penalty sharply; that is the next step past
this chapter.

#### 4.5.10 Unfinished Business

PIPE does not rename registers, issue out of order, or speculate past
multiple branches. It does not have a data cache with miss stalls, nor
virtual memory. Chapters 5 and 6 pick up performance from the program's
side and the memory side; the hardware story continues in computer-
architecture books that start where CS:APP stops.


Forwarding priority, written as a story: decode asks "who has the value I need?". Execute answers first (the instruction that just produced valE). Memory answers next, and must offer both valE (an ALU result that has moved one stage) and valM (a just-finished load). Write back answers last. The register file answers only if nobody else does. Implementing that story as a case list with newest-first order is the entire forwarding mux.

Branch recovery costs two bubbles because PIPE fetches on prediction for two cycles before execute resolves the condition: the instruction in D and the instruction just moving into E are both from the wrong path. A design with an earlier branch resolution (some RISC cores resolve in decode for simple cases) would cut that penalty to one; a design with a deeper pipe would raise it. The two-cycle number is a property of this pipe, not a law.

Return-address prediction is the obvious upgrade the book leaves as unfinished business. Real CPUs keep a small stack of predicted return addresses pushed on call and popped on ret, and they are right more than 90 percent of the time on ordinary code. Adding one to PIPE would replace the ret stall with a predict-and-squash path just like a branch; the Architecture Lab sometimes offers that as an open-ended challenge.

### Worked example

Hazard in this sequence:

```text
1: mrmovq 0(%rdx), %rax
2: addq   %rax, %rbx
3: je     target
```

Cycle-by-cycle after instruction 1 enters execute:

1. Instruction 2 must stall in decode for one cycle (load-use on `%rax`).
2. Instruction 3's fetch stalls with it (F stalls when D stalls).
3. After the stall, `%rax` forwards from M to E for instruction 2.
4. Instruction 3 is a conditional jump predicted taken; if the flags from
   instruction 2 (which set them) say not taken, instructions fetched from
   `target` are bubbled out of D and E, and F gets the fall-through PC.

### Step by step

To analyse a PIPE run:

1. Write the instructions in a column; draw stage slots across cycles.
2. Mark every read of a register and every write; draw forwarding arrows
   or stall bubbles where needed.
3. For each jump, mark the prediction and, when it resolves, any squash.
4. Count bubbles and stalls; CPI = 1 + (stalls + bubbles that occupy the
   commit slot) / instruction count.

### Common mistake

Forwarding from write back only. The critical path is execute to decode;
without that forward, every dependent pair stalls. Another: forgetting
that a bubble in E still moves; it is a real nop instruction occupying a
slot, not an empty cycle of the whole pipe.

### In real systems

Every modern core has the descendants of these mechanisms: a bypass
network, a load-use interlock (or a scheduler that refuses to issue the
use), a branch predictor with a rename-based recovery stack, and a
reorder buffer that commits exceptions precisely. The vocabulary of this
section is the vocabulary of CPU design papers and of performance
counters like `branches` / `branch-misses` in `perf`.

A load-use hazard, because the data is not ready until the memory stage,
one cycle too late for the next instruction's execute.

</details>

<details>

taken, the two wrongly fetched instructions are bubbled and fetch resumes
at fall-through.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is exception status carried to write back instead of acted on immediately?</summary>

To keep exceptions precise: older instructions must finish, younger ones
must not change architected state.

## 4.6 Summary

### In one sentence

A processor is a clocked pipeline of the stages an instruction needs, held
together by encodings, combinational HCL and control logic that repairs
hazards; Y86-64 is small enough that I can hold the whole machine in my
head.

### The idea

#### 4.6.1 Y86-64 Simulators

The CS:APP student site ships:

- **yas**, the Y86-64 assembler, which turns `.ys` files into `.yo` byte
  listings.
- **yis**, an ISA-level simulator that executes one instruction at a time
  and reports final register and memory state (useful as a reference).
- **ssim** / **ssim+**, SEQ and PIPE simulators driven by the student's
  HCL, with TTY and GUI front ends that show every wire each cycle.

The Architecture Lab asks for HCL changes (new instructions, a different
prediction policy, an optimisation) and tests them against reference
traces. My encoder on this page is enough to practise encodings; the lab
simulators are where PIPE control logic becomes real.

### Worked example

A one-line mental checklist before changing PIPE HCL:

1. Does every instruction still get correct valA/valB under forwarding?
2. Does load-use stall exactly one cycle, and not stall when the load
   destination is unused?
3. Does a mispredicted branch bubble D and E, and only D and E?
4. Does `ret` stall F and bubble D until it reaches memory?
5. Does an exception arriving in W prevent younger instructions from
   writing registers or memory?

### Step by step

How I would implement a new Y86-64 instruction (for example, a two-register
`mulq`):

1. Assign an icode/ifun and extend the encoder and `yas`.
2. Teach fetch to parse it (need_regids, need_valC).
3. Teach decode its src/dst.
4. Teach execute its ALU behaviour (or add a multiply unit).
5. Decide whether it sets CC and whether it touches memory.
6. Run SEQ tests, then PIPE tests, watching for new hazards (a multi-cycle
   multiply would need stalls PIPE does not yet have).

### Common mistake

Testing only the happy path. The lab's secret tests hit every combination
of hazard and exception; a change that works on a five-line program often
fails on a load-use during a mispredicted branch during a `ret`.

### In real systems

Industrial CPU verification runs millions of random instruction sequences
against a reference model, plus formal checks on the control logic. The
Architecture Lab is that idea at homework scale. Once I have changed PIPE
and survived the tests, reading about a real microarchitecture is much less
abstract.

</details>

### Building intuition for stage balance

Suppose measurement of a SEQ prototype shows these combinational delays:

<Trace columns={["Path", "Delay"]} rows={[
  ["Fetch (instruction memory + decode fields)", "150 ps"],
  ["Decode (register file read)", "70 ps"],
  ["Execute (ALU + condition)", "120 ps"],
  ["Memory (data memory)", "150 ps"],

A naive five-way split would give stages of 150, 70, 120, 150, 40 ps. The clock would be set by the 150 ps stages plus register overhead, and the 40 ps write-back stage would sit idle most of the cycle. Hardware designers merge write-back with memory or split fetch into more equal pieces; the book's five-stage PIPE is already a reasoned merge of the six SEQ stages into five roughly comparable ones. Whenever I see a real core described as "having a long fetch" or "memory-bound", this is the picture: one stage set the clock, and the others had slack.

### What I ignore on purpose in this chapter

Y86-64 has no virtual memory, no caches, no interrupts from devices, no
multiple cores, no floating point, and no privilege levels. Each of those
adds a feedback path and a hazard class. Chapter 6 puts caches under the
memory stage; chapter 9 puts page tables under both fetch and memory;
chapter 8 puts exception handlers behind the status codes. Keeping them
out of PIPE is not a denial that they matter; it is the only way to finish
the hazard story in one chapter.


### Worked SEQ vs PIPE timing numbers

I like to keep one numeric example that I can recompute without the book.
Assume:

- SEQ critical path 600 ps → clock 600 ps → frequency 1.67 GHz → peak 1.67 GIPS.
- PIPE stage budget 100 ps logic + 20 ps register overhead = 120 ps → frequency 8.33 GHz peak.
- Realised CPI on integer code with the book's predictor ≈ 1.25.

Then PIPE's sustained throughput is 8.33 / 1.25 ≈ 6.7 GIPS, about four
times SEQ. If I improve the branch predictor enough to drop CPI to 1.10,
throughput rises to 7.6 GIPS without changing the clock. If I unbalance
the pipe and the slowest stage grows to 150 ps, frequency falls to 6.67 GHz
and even with CPI 1.10 I only get 6.1 GIPS, worse than the balanced pipe
with the worse predictor. That is why stage balance and hazard rate are
the two knobs this chapter keeps returning to.

### Reading a PIPE trace from the simulator

A typical PIPE simulator trace line names the instruction in each stage
and any special event. I read it like this:

1. Scan for `bubble` or `stall` markers; each is either a hazard or a recovery.
2. When I see a stall in D, look at E's instruction: if it is a load whose
   destination matches D's source, that is load-use; if E is a `ret` or a
   not-yet-resolved branch, that is control.
3. When I see two bubbles enter D and E together, look at M: a conditional
   jump with `Cnd=0` when it was predicted taken is the usual cause.
4. Confirm that after recovery the instruction entering F is the correct
   fall-through or target.

After a few dozen lines of that reading, the control table stops being
abstract and becomes a pattern I recognise in the trace.

### Why the book uses a predict-taken policy

Predict-taken is wrong on every loop exit and right on every loop back
edge. Since back edges execute many times and exits once, the policy does
well on loop-heavy code without any history table. Predict-not-taken is
simpler in hardware (fall-through is already valP) but worse on loops.
A one-bit or two-bit predictor keyed by branch PC beats both once the
workload leaves the "tight loop" pattern. The book sticks with
predict-taken so the HCL stays short; the idea of "predict now, repair
later" is the transferable part.

### Connecting condition codes to cmov and jXX

The execute stage computes a six-way condition from ZF, SF, OF and ifun,
exactly like chapter 3's `set` and `j` families:

- le: (SF XOR OF) OR ZF
- l:  SF XOR OF
- e:  ZF
- ne: not ZF
- ge: not (SF XOR OF)
- g:  not ((SF XOR OF) OR ZF)

That boolean block is shared by conditional moves (which use Cnd to enable
the register write) and conditional jumps (which use Cnd to choose between
valC and valP at PC update, or to confirm a prediction in PIPE). Getting
those six formulas wrong is a classic Architecture Lab bug: every ALU op
looks fine, but every branch misbehaves.

### From Y86-64 back to x86-64

After this chapter, an x86-64 listing from chapter 3 looks different. A
`ret` is a control hazard. A load into a register used by the next
arithmetic instruction is a load-use. A dense block of independent
arithmetic is what PIPE (and real cores) eat for breakfast. Conditional
moves are attractive partly because they remove a control hazard. When
chapter 5 talks about a processor that can issue several operations per
cycle, I will picture several PIPEs sharing a register file, not a black
box.

### How SEQ stage HCL connects to PIPE stage HCL

Every PIPE stage HCL is the SEQ stage HCL with three kinds of edit:

1. **Rename** PC to `F_predPC` or `f_pc`, `valA` to `d_valA` / `E_valA`, and so on, so each value is tied to a stage.
2. **Add forwarding muxes** in decode (and sometimes execute) that prefer newer pipeline-register values over the register file.
3. **Add control** that can stall or bubble each pipeline register, driven by hazard detectors that look at icode and dst/src fields across stages.

If I can write the SEQ HCL for a new instruction, the PIPE HCL is mechanical once I decide whether the instruction creates a new kind of hazard. That is why the book insists on SEQ first.

### Memory and register-file ports in PIPE

The register file is written in write back and read in decode. Because write back of instruction i and decode of instruction i+4 happen in the same cycle, a naive register file would need the write to "forward" internally to a same-cycle read of the same register. Real register files support that (write-before-read in the same cycle), and the book's HCL assumes it. Data memory is used only in the memory stage, so a single port is enough for Y86-64; an instruction that both loaded and stored would need a redesign.

Instruction memory is read every cycle in fetch. In SEQ it was on the long combinational path; in PIPE it is a stage of its own. Separate instruction and data memories (a Harvard view inside the core) keep fetch and memory from fighting over one port. Real cores look like that from the pipeline's point of view even when the outer memory system is unified.

## Questions people ask

**Why invent Y86-64 instead of using real x86-64?**
Because x86-64 encodings and side effects would bury the pipeline ideas
under pages of special cases. Y86-64 is small enough that every instruction
has a clear path through SEQ and PIPE, yet close enough that the lessons
transfer.

**Is a five-stage pipeline still how CPUs work?**
It is the teaching form of how they work. Real cores are deeper, issue
wider, execute out of order and speculate much more aggressively, but they
still fetch, decode, execute, touch memory and commit, and they still
fight data and control hazards.

**Do I need to memorise all the HCL?**
I memorise the patterns (set membership for opcode classes, case lists for
muxes, forwarding priority order). Exact field names I look up. The lab
rewards understanding, not photocopying.

**What is the single most important idea in this chapter?**
That pipelining raises throughput by overlapping instructions, and that
almost all of the control complexity is there to preserve the illusion
that instructions ran one at a time.


**Why absolute jump destinations instead of relative?**
Relative jumps are position-independent and denser for short offsets; absolute jumps make SEQ's next-PC mux a pure select among valP, valC and valM with no adder on the PC path. Y86-64 chose the teaching simplification. RISC-V and ARM64 use mostly PC-relative branches for the reasons chapter 7 will touch when we talk about position-independent code.

**What is the Architecture Lab really testing?**
Whether I can change a working PIPE without breaking the hazard story. The new instruction or new prediction policy is the flavour; the invariant that every dependent pair still gets the right value is the substance.

## Key takeaways

- Y86-64 is a small, regularly encoded ISA with fifteen registers, four ALU ops, moves, jumps, call/ret and push/pop.
- Combinational logic settles between clocks; state changes at the edge; HCL describes the combinational part.
- SEQ does one instruction per long cycle through six logical stages; it is the reference for correctness.
- Pipelining splits those stages with registers, raising throughput toward one instruction per short cycle.
- Data hazards are mostly fixed by forwarding; load-use hazards need a one-cycle stall.
- Control hazards are fixed by predicting the next PC and squashing on a wrong prediction; `ret` stalls.
- Precise exceptions ride with the instruction to write back so younger instructions can be cancelled cleanly.
- PIPE control is a small table of stall/bubble/normal combinations; getting the priorities right is the Architecture Lab.
- Stage balance and hazard rate together set sustained throughput; a faster clock with a worse CPI can lose to a slower balanced design.
- Predict-taken is a teaching policy; the enduring idea is predict now and repair on a wrong prediction.

## Glossary

- **Bubble:** a nop-like instruction injected into a pipeline register to cancel a wrongly fetched or wrongly started instruction.
- **CPI:** cycles per instruction; 1 in an ideal PIPE, higher with stalls and bubbles.
- **Forwarding (bypassing):** routing a produced value from a later stage back to an earlier stage so a dependent instruction need not wait for write back.
- **HCL:** hardware control language; the book's notation for combinational logic.
- **Hazard:** a situation where the natural pipeline flow would compute the wrong result or read the wrong state.
- **icode / ifun:** the two nibbles of a Y86-64 opcode byte (instruction class and function).
- **Load-use hazard:** a data hazard where a load's data arrives too late for the next instruction's execute; needs a stall.
- **PIPE:** the book's pipelined Y86-64 implementation.
- **Precise exception:** the machine state at an exception matches having executed every instruction before the faulting one and none after.
- **SEQ:** the book's sequential Y86-64 implementation.
- **Stall:** holding a pipeline register's value for an extra cycle so a hazard can clear.
- **Throughput vs latency:** instructions finished per second versus time from start to finish of one instruction.
- **Y86-64:** the pedagogical ISA used in this chapter, a subset of x86-64 ideas with regular encodings.

<summary>1. What are the four Y86-64 status codes?</summary>

AOK, HLT, ADR, INS.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How long is rmmovq %rax, 8(%rbx)?</summary>

Ten bytes: opcode, register byte, eight-byte displacement.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does SEQ have no data hazards?</summary>

Because each instruction finishes completely, including write back, before
the next instruction's decode reads the register file.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What does PIPE predict for ret?</summary>

It does not predict; it stalls until `ret` reaches the memory stage and
produces the return address.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. In the forwarding mux, who wins if both M and W have the same destination?</summary>

M, the more recent producer. Forwarding priority is newest first.

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. What control row handles a load-use hazard?</summary>

F stall, D stall, E bubble, M normal, W normal.

</details>

<details>
<summary>7. What makes an exception precise in PIPE?</summary>

The status travels with the instruction and only takes effect at write
back, with younger instructions bubbled away.

</details>

<details>
<summary>8. Does pipelining change the result a program computes?</summary>

No. It changes when results are computed, not which results. That is the
whole point of hazard control.

</details>
