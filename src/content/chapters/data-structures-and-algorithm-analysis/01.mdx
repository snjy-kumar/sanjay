## 1.1 What's this book about?

### In one sentence

A program that gives the right answer is only half done; the other half is
knowing how long it will take when the input becomes large.

### The idea

Most programming courses stop at "does it work?". This book adds a second
question to every structure and every algorithm: how does its running time
grow as the input grows? The author's stance is that no structure should be
presented without an explanation of its running time, and every later
chapter keeps that promise.

The reason is practical. Many problems have an obvious solution that is
correct and hopeless at scale. The chapter opens with two such problems. The
first is selection: given n numbers, find the k-th largest. The second is a
word puzzle: given a grid of letters and a list of words, find every word
hidden in the grid in any of eight directions.

For selection, two obvious approaches exist. Sort all n numbers and read off
position k. Or keep the first k numbers sorted in a small array, then for
each remaining number, drop it if it is too small, otherwise slide it into
its place and push the smallest out. Both are correct. Both are fine for a
thousand numbers. For thirty million numbers, the book points out, neither
finishes in a reasonable time, and chapter 6 and chapter 7 later give
methods that do.

For the word puzzle, one approach tries every word against every position
and direction. Another tries every position, direction and length of
letters, and looks each candidate string up in the word list. Which is
faster depends on whether the grid or the word list is larger, and the
second approach needs a fast lookup, which is the subject of chapter 5.

So the book is about two skills at once: building data structures and
predicting their cost. A data structure is a way of organising data in
memory so that some operations are cheap. An algorithm is a precise,
finite list of steps that solves a problem. Analysis is the reasoning that
predicts cost from the steps, before running anything.

### Worked example

Simple version. I want the 3rd largest of `[8, 2, 9, 4, 7, 1]`. Sorting
descending gives `9 8 7 4 2 1` and the answer is the third item, 7. The work
is the sort.

Realistic version. I have a log of 30 million response times and I want the
99th percentile, which is the k-th largest for k equal to 300,000. Sorting
30 million numbers in JavaScript takes seconds and needs all of them in
memory as a copy. Keeping a sorted array of 300,000 and sliding each new
number in costs up to 300,000 moves per number in the worst case, which is
about nine trillion moves. A heap of size k (chapter 6) or quickselect
(chapter 7) cuts this to something a laptop finishes quickly. The question
"how does it grow?" is what tells me to reach for those before I write the
slow one.

n times k. When k is small this is fine. When k is half of n, it is about n
squared over two, which is why the book says neither obvious method scales.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat "it works on my test" as "it works". A
correct program that takes a week is, for the person waiting, not working.
The fix is to ask, before writing the code, "what is n here, and what is the
largest n this will ever see?" and then estimate. If the estimate is in
trillions of steps, choose a different algorithm now rather than after the
first incident.

### In real systems

Percentile dashboards, leaderboards, "top 10 products" and autocomplete are
selection problems. Search, spell checking and "find these words in this
document" are the word puzzle. Real systems pick the algorithm by the size
of the data they expect, and the ones that break are usually the ones that
were sized for the demo.

## 1.2 Mathematics review

### In one sentence

Five pieces of maths carry nearly every analysis in the book: exponent
rules, logarithms, a handful of sums, arithmetic modulo n, and three ways
to prove a claim.

### The idea

#### 1.2.1 Exponents

An exponent counts repeated multiplication: 2 to the power 5 is
2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2, which is 32. Four rules follow from counting the factors.
Multiplying two powers of the same base adds the exponents, because you
string the factors together: 2³ × 2⁴ is seven twos multiplied, which is 2⁷.
Dividing subtracts them. Raising a power to a power multiplies them:
(2³)² is two groups of three twos, which is 2⁶.

The fourth rule trips people up. Adding two equal powers does not add the
exponents. 2ⁿ + 2ⁿ is two copies of 2ⁿ, which is 2 × 2ⁿ, which is 2ⁿ⁺¹. So
doubling a power of two moves the exponent up by one, and adding a power to
itself does not square it. This one fact explains why a doubling array
(chapter 3) only needs about log n resizes.

#### 1.2.2 Logarithms

A logarithm answers the question "what exponent gives this number?". The
logarithm base 2 of 1,024 is 10 because 2¹⁰ is 1,024. In this book, and in
computer science generally, log with no base means base 2 unless said
otherwise.

The way I actually use a logarithm is as a count of halvings. Start at n
and keep halving, rounding down, until you reach 1. The number of halvings
is the floor of log₂ n. For a million it is 19, for a billion about 30. Any
algorithm that throws away half the remaining work with constant effort per
step runs in time proportional to log n. Binary search is the famous case,
and chapter 2 gives two more.

Three rules matter. The log of a product is the sum of the logs:
log(ab) = log a + log b. The log of a quotient is the difference. And the
change-of-base rule says a log in one base is a constant multiple of a log
in another: log₂ n equals log₁₀ n divided by log₁₀ 2. That constant is why
the base never matters inside big-O, which chapter 2 relies on.

One more fact: log n grows slower than any power of n, even n to the 0.01.
For every positive constant, eventually n to that constant overtakes log n.
That is why log n is "cheap" in every comparison the book makes.

#### 1.2.3 Series

A series is a sum of a sequence of terms. Three of them recur.

The **geometric series** adds powers of a fixed number. For powers of two,
1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 2ⁿ equals 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1. In words: the sum of all the powers up
to one point is one less than the next power. More generally, for a base a
other than 1, the sum of a⁰ through aⁿ equals (aⁿ⁺¹ − 1) / (a − 1). When a
is between 0 and 1, the infinite sum is at most 1 / (1 − a), so
1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ... never exceeds 2.

The **arithmetic series** adds consecutive integers: 1 + 2 + ... + n equals
n(n + 1) / 2, which is about n² / 2. A nested loop where the inner loop runs
1, then 2, then 3 times, and so on, does this much work. The sum of squares
is about n³ / 3, and more generally the sum of i to the power k is about
n to the power k + 1, divided by k + 1.

The **harmonic series** adds reciprocals: 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... + 1/n. Its sum,
called the n-th harmonic number Hₙ, is about the natural log of n. It grows,
but extremely slowly, and it appears whenever an algorithm does an amount of
work that shrinks like 1/i at step i. The average depth analysis of binary
search trees in chapter 4 and the analysis of quicksort in chapter 7 both
land on it.

#### 1.2.4 Modular arithmetic

Two numbers are **congruent modulo n** if they leave the same remainder
when divided by n. 81 and 61 are congruent modulo 10 because both end in 1.
The useful property is that congruence survives addition and
multiplication: if a and b are congruent modulo n, then a + c and b + c are,
and so are ac and bc.

That is what lets a hash function (chapter 5) take the remainder at every
step instead of building a huge number and taking the remainder once at the
end. The intermediate values stay small and the final answer is the same.

There is a JavaScript trap here. The `%` operator is the *remainder*, not
the mathematical modulus. `-7 % 3` is `-1` in JavaScript, C and C++, because
the sign follows the left operand. The mathematical answer is 2. When a
negative number can reach `%`, use a helper that adds n and takes the
remainder again.

The chapter also states two facts about primes that later chapters lean on.
If n is prime, then multiplying by any number not divisible by n permutes
the remainders 0 to n − 1, and a quadratic equation modulo a prime has at
most two roots. Quadratic probing in chapter 5 needs both.

#### 1.2.5 The P word

"The P word" is the book's joke name for proof. Three methods appear
throughout.

**Proof by induction** proves a statement for every whole number in two
steps. The base case shows it holds for the smallest value, usually 0 or 1.
The inductive step assumes it holds for every value up to some k (the
inductive hypothesis) and shows it then holds for k + 1. Like dominoes: the
first falls, and each one knocks over the next, so all fall.

**Proof by counterexample** disproves a general claim by finding one case
where it fails. One failure is enough. "Every number of the form n² + n + 41
is prime" holds for n from 0 to 39 and fails at 40, where the value is 41².

**Proof by contradiction** assumes the claim is false and derives something
impossible. The classic one shows there are infinitely many primes: suppose
there are finitely many, multiply them all and add one; that number is not
divisible by any of them, which contradicts the list being complete.

### Worked example

Simple: how many times can I halve 1,000 before reaching 1? The halving
sequence is 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62, 31, 15, 7, 3, 1: nine halvings. And
2⁹ is 512, 2¹⁰ is 1,024, so log₂ 1000 sits between 9 and 10. Both views
agree.

Realistic: a growable array starts at capacity 1 and doubles when full. To
hold a million items it doubles 20 times (2²⁰ is just over a million). Each
doubling copies the current items, so the total copying is
1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 2¹⁹, which by the geometric series is 2²⁰ − 1, about one
million. So a million pushes cause about a million copies in total: on
average about one copy per push. That is the whole amortised argument for
dynamic arrays, and it is just the geometric series.

My own check, in TypeScript:

```ts
function halvings(n: number): number {
  let k = 0;
  while (n > 1) { n = Math.floor(n / 2); k++; }
  return k;
}
console.log([1, 2, 3, 8, 1000, 1_000_000].map((n) => `${n}:${halvings(n)}`).join(' '));
let s = 0;
for (let i = 0; i <= 10; i++) s += 2 ** i;
console.log('sum 2^0..2^10 =', s, '2^11 =', 2 ** 11);
let h = 0;
for (let i = 1; i <= 1000; i++) h += 1 / i;
console.log('H_1000 =', h.toFixed(4), 'ln 1000 =', Math.log(1000).toFixed(4));
```

```text
1:0 2:1 3:1 8:3 1000:9 1000000:19
sum 2^0..2^10 = 2047 2^11 = 2048
H_1000 = 7.4855 ln 1000 = 6.9078
```

The harmonic number is a little more than ln n. The gap converges to a
constant, about 0.577, called Euler's constant. For analysis, "about ln n"
is all we need.

Modular arithmetic, with the negative-number fix:

```ts
console.log(-7 % 3);                       // remainder, sign of the left side
const mod = (a: number, n: number) => ((a % n) + n) % n;
console.log(mod(-7, 3), mod(7, 3), mod(-3, 3));
console.log((81 * 7) % 10, ((81 % 10) * (7 % 10)) % 10); // same answer
```

```text
-1
2 1 0
7 7
```

And a counterexample, step by step: the claim "n² + n + 41 is always
prime". Try n from 0: 41, 43, 47, 53, all prime. Keep going and every value
to n equal to 39 is prime. At n equal to 40 the value is 1600 + 40 + 41,
which is 1681, which is 41 × 41. One failure kills the claim, no matter how
many successes came before.

### Common mistake

Three mistakes show up again and again. First, writing 2ⁿ + 2ⁿ as 2²ⁿ; it
is 2ⁿ⁺¹. Second, using `%` on a possibly negative number in JavaScript and
indexing an array with the result, which gives `arr[-1]`, which is
`undefined`, not a crash, so the bug hides. The fix is the `mod` helper
above. Third, in induction, proving the step but skipping the base case. A
"proof" that every horse is the same colour works this way: the step is
fine for large groups and breaks between groups of one and two. Always
check the base case, and check that the step really works from the base.

### In real systems

Logarithms are how I estimate tree depth, the number of binary search
steps, the height of a B-tree index (chapter 4), and how many rounds a
retry with exponential back-off takes to reach a cap. Geometric series are
how growth strategies (doubling buffers, exponential back-off total wait)
are costed. Modular arithmetic is in every hash function, every ring buffer
index, every sharding key (`hash(userId) mod shardCount`). Induction is how
I convince myself a loop or a recursive function is right, which chapter 2
and the
<a href={withBase('/books/introduction-to-algorithms/02-getting-started/')}>loop invariants of CLRS chapter 2</a>
formalise.

## 1.3 A brief introduction to recursion

### In one sentence

A recursive function solves a problem by calling itself on a smaller
version of the same problem, and it is correct and fast only if it obeys
four rules.

### The idea

Some definitions are naturally self-referential. "The sum of the first n
numbers is n plus the sum of the first n − 1 numbers, and the sum of the
first zero numbers is zero." That is a complete definition, and it turns
straight into code. A function defined in terms of itself is **recursive**.

Recursion is not circular logic, because each call is on a *smaller*
problem and the smallest problems are answered directly. The answer to a
call does not depend on itself; it depends on answers to strictly smaller
calls, which bottom out. Under the hood, each call gets its own **stack
frame**: a block of memory holding that call's parameters and local
variables. The frames pile up as calls go deeper and pop off as they
return.

#### The four rules

The book distils recursion into four rules. I keep them as a checklist.

1. **Base cases.** There must be at least one input that is answered
   without recursing. Without it the calls never stop.
2. **Making progress.** Every recursive call must move towards a base case.
   Calling on n − 2 when the base case is n equal to 0 skips past it for
   odd n.
3. **Design rule: assume the recursive calls work.** When writing the
   function, trust that the call on the smaller input returns the right
   answer, and only check that you combine it correctly. Tracing every
   level by hand is how people get lost.
4. **Compound interest rule.** Never solve the same instance of a problem
   in separate recursive calls. Doing so can turn linear work into
   exponential work, because the repeated work itself repeats.

Rule 3 is induction in disguise. The base case is the induction base; the
assumption that smaller calls work is the inductive hypothesis; checking
the combination is the inductive step. That is why recursive code and
induction proofs look the same.

#### Printing a number digit by digit

The book's classic small example prints a positive integer one digit at a
time, given only a routine that prints a single digit. The recursive idea:
to print 76234, first print 7623 (recursively), then print the last digit
4. The last digit is n mod 10; the rest is n divided by 10, rounded down.
The base case is a number under 10, which is a single digit.

### Worked example

Simple: the sum of the first n numbers.

```ts
function sumTo(n: number): number {
  return n === 0 ? 0 : n + sumTo(n - 1);
}
console.log(sumTo(100)); // 5050
```

Base case n equal to 0, progress by 1 each call, and the combination
"n plus the answer for n − 1" is correct if the smaller answer is.

Printing digits, in my own version that collects them:

```ts
function printDigits(n: number, out: string[] = []): string[] {
  if (n >= 10) printDigits(Math.floor(n / 10), out); // the rest first
  out.push(String(n % 10));                          // then the last digit
  return out;
}
console.log(printDigits(76234).join(' ')); // 7 6 2 3 4
```

Realistic: walking a directory tree, a JSON document, or a component tree
in a UI framework is recursion with the same shape. The base case is a leaf
(a file, a primitive value, a text node). The recursive case handles a
folder, object or element by recursing into each child. Rule 3 is why this
code is short: I write "size of a folder is the sum of the sizes of its
children" and trust the calls.

Breaking rule 4. Fibonacci numbers are defined by F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1 and
F(n) = F(n − 1) + F(n − 2). Written directly:

```ts
let calls = 0;
const fib = (n: number): number => { calls++; return n < 2 ? n : fib(n - 1) + fib(n - 2); };
console.log('fib(30)=', fib(30), 'calls=', calls);

calls = 0;
const memo = new Map<number, number>();
const mfib = (n: number): number => {
  calls++;
  if (n < 2) return n;
  const hit = memo.get(n);
  if (hit !== undefined) return hit;
  const v = mfib(n - 1) + mfib(n - 2);
  memo.set(n, v);
  return v;
};
console.log('mfib(30)=', mfib(30), 'calls=', calls);
```

```text
fib(30)= 832040 calls= 2692537
mfib(30)= 832040 calls= 59
```

Same answer, 2.7 million calls against 59. The naive version computes
F(28) twice, F(27) three times, F(26) five times, and the counts are
themselves Fibonacci numbers, which grow exponentially. The memoised
version stores each answer the first time, so each F(k) is computed once.
That is the compound interest rule, and it is the seed of dynamic
programming in chapter 10.

Breaking rule 2:

```ts
function bad(n: number): number { return n === 0 ? 0 : bad(n - 2) + 1; }
bad(3); // 3, 1, -1, -3, ... never hits 0
```

```text
RangeError Maximum call stack size exceeded
```

The cost of naive Fibonacci: let T(n) be the number of calls. T(n) is
1 + T(n − 1) + T(n − 2), which is at least F(n). Fibonacci numbers grow
roughly like 1.618ⁿ, so the call count is exponential. Memoisation makes
each value cost constant work after its sub-answers exist, so the total is
linear in n.

### Common mistake

The commonest mistake is a missing or unreachable base case: recursing on
`n - 2` with a base case only at 0, or on a list's tail with no check for
the empty list. JavaScript reports it as `RangeError: Maximum call stack
size exceeded`. The fix is to list every input that should stop, and check
that each call's argument gets strictly closer to one of them.

The second is breaking rule 4 without noticing. Any recursion with two or
more calls on overlapping inputs deserves a minute's thought about how many
times each subproblem is solved. The fix is memoisation (cache by input) or
rewriting bottom-up as a loop.

The third is deep recursion on large input. A recursive linked-list walk of
a million nodes overflows the default stack in Node. The fix is a loop, or
an explicit stack array.

### In real systems

React rendering, JSON serialisation, file system walkers, parsers and
compilers are recursive. Every one of them has a depth limit in practice:
`JSON.stringify` on a cyclic object throws, deeply nested JSON from an
attacker can exhaust the stack, and production parsers often use an
explicit stack to bound memory. Memoisation is everywhere too: React's
`useMemo`, HTTP caches, and build systems that skip work whose inputs did
not change are all rule 4 applied to systems.

## 1.4 C++ classes

### In one sentence

A class bundles data with the functions that are allowed to touch it, so
that the rest of the program uses a small public interface and cannot break
the data's rules.

### The idea

Every data structure in the book is a class: a vector, a list, a tree, a
hash table. The class idea is the same in C++ and TypeScript, with
different syntax.

#### 1.4.1 Basic class syntax

A class has **members**: data members (fields) that hold state, and member
functions (methods) that act on it. Members are either **public**, usable
by anyone, or **private**, usable only inside the class. The book's first
class is a cell that holds one integer, with a read method and a write
method. The point is not the cell; it is that the field is private, so all
access goes through methods, and the class can later change how it stores
the value without breaking callers. That rule, hide the representation, is
called **information hiding** or **encapsulation**.

A **constructor** is the function that runs when an object is created; it
puts the object into a valid starting state.

#### 1.4.2 Extra constructor syntax and accessors

C++ adds details here that TypeScript does not have. A default parameter
lets one constructor serve as both "no arguments" and "one argument". An
**initialiser list** sets members before the constructor body runs, which
matters for members that cannot be assigned later (constants and
references). The `explicit` keyword stops a one-argument constructor from
being used as a silent type conversion, so writing `IntCell c = 37;` by
accident becomes an error rather than a surprise.

An **accessor** is a method that reads state without changing it; a
**mutator** changes it. C++ marks accessors with `const` after the
parameter list, and the compiler then refuses to let them modify fields.
TypeScript's closest tool is `readonly` on fields and returning copies.

#### 1.4.3 Separation of interface and implementation

C++ puts the **interface** (what a class offers: its member declarations)
in a header file and the **implementation** (the method bodies) in a
source file. The header is what other files include; the source is compiled
once. Preprocessor guards stop a header being included twice. Methods
defined outside the class use the scope operator, `IntCell::read`, to say
which class they belong to.

In TypeScript, the interface is an `interface` or the exported class's
public signature, and modules give separation for free. The idea that
transfers is: callers depend on the interface, so the implementation can
change.

#### 1.4.4 vector and string

The standard library gives two classes the book uses everywhere. `vector`
is a growable array that knows its own size, the C++ version of a
JavaScript array. `string` is a growable sequence of characters with
comparison and concatenation built in. Both replace the error-prone
C-style arrays and strings of section 1.5.7. The book's newer C++ also lets
a vector be initialised with braces and iterated with a range-based `for`
loop, which reads like `for (const x of xs)`.

### Worked example

Simple: the cell, my way, in TypeScript.

```ts
class IntCell {
  #stored: number;                       // private field
  constructor(initial = 0) { this.#stored = initial; }
  read(): number { return this.#stored; }  // accessor
  write(x: number): void { this.#stored = x; } // mutator
}
const c = new IntCell(37);
c.write(c.read() + 5);
console.log(c.read()); // 42
// c.#stored = 1;      // syntax error outside the class
```

The same in C++, as the book would structure it (my names and layout):

```cpp
class IntCell {
 public:
  explicit IntCell(int initial = 0) : stored{initial} {}
  int read() const { return stored; }   // const: cannot change state
  void write(int x) { stored = x; }
 private:
  int stored;
};
```

Realistic: a bank balance class whose invariant is "never negative". If
the balance field is public, any code anywhere can set it to −100. With it
private and a `withdraw` method that checks, the invariant has exactly one
place to be enforced and tested. Every data structure in this book is this
idea at a larger scale: the AVL tree's balance rule (chapter 4) is safe
only because no caller can rewire nodes directly.

Walking through an object's life:

1. `new IntCell(37)` allocates the object and runs the constructor, which

2. `c.read()` runs the accessor, which returns the field's value and
   changes nothing.
3. `c.write(42)` runs the mutator, which replaces the field.
4. When nothing refers to `c` any more, the JavaScript garbage collector
   reclaims it. In C++ the object's destructor runs at a defined moment
   instead, which is section 1.5.6.

### Common mistake

The common mistake in C++ is a one-argument constructor without `explicit`.
A function taking an `IntCell` then quietly accepts an `int`, constructing
a temporary cell, and type errors vanish. Mark one-argument constructors
`explicit` unless the conversion is genuinely wanted.

The common mistake in TypeScript is believing `private` protects at run
time. The `private` keyword is checked only by the compiler; the field is
still visible to plain JavaScript and to `as any`. The `#field` syntax is
enforced by the engine. Use `#` when the invariant matters.

### In real systems

Encapsulation is the reason a library can ship a faster version without
breaking you: `Map` in V8 has changed its internals several times, and no
program noticed. In service design the same idea is "other teams call your
API, never your database". When a table is shared directly, its schema
becomes a public interface that can never change, which is the system-scale
version of a public field.

## 1.5 C++ details

### In one sentence

C++ makes you say who owns each piece of memory and whether a value is
copied, shared or moved, and the book's container code is correct only
because it answers those questions deliberately.

### The idea

TypeScript hides memory behind a garbage collector: objects live until
nothing refers to them. C++ does not, so its programs must decide when
memory is freed. This section is the vocabulary for those decisions. I read
it once now and again when phase 5 makes memory concrete, because the same
questions (who owns it, is this a copy or a view) cause bugs in every
language.

#### 1.5.1 Pointers

A **pointer** is a variable that holds the memory address of another
object. Dereferencing it (`*p`) reaches the object. `new` creates an object
on the heap and returns a pointer to it; `delete` frees it. A pointer that
points nowhere is `nullptr`. Using a freed pointer (a **dangling pointer**)
or forgetting to free (a **memory leak**) are the two classic bugs. Every
JavaScript object variable is really a pointer that you cannot do
arithmetic on and that never dangles, because the collector frees only
unreachable objects.

#### 1.5.2 Lvalues, rvalues and references

An **lvalue** is an expression that names a lasting object with an
address: a variable, an array element. An **rvalue** is a temporary that
will vanish at the end of the expression: the result of `x + y`, a literal,
a function's returned value. The names come from "can it appear on the left
of an assignment?".

A **reference** is another name for an existing object. An lvalue
reference (`T&`) binds to an lvalue and lets you change the original. An
rvalue reference (`T&&`) binds to a temporary, and it exists so that code
can recognise "this object is about to die, so I may steal its insides".

#### 1.5.3 Parameter passing

C++ gives four choices, and the book's rule of thumb picks among them:

- **Call by value** copies the argument. Fine for small types like `int`.
- **Call by constant reference** (`const T&`) passes a reference the
  function promises not to change. The default for large objects you only
  read, because it avoids the copy.
- **Call by lvalue reference** (`T&`) lets the function change the caller's
  object. Use it when changing it is the point.
- **Call by rvalue reference** (`T&&`) receives a temporary and may move
  from it.

JavaScript has one rule: everything is passed by value, but the value of an
object variable is a reference. So the function can *mutate* the caller's
object but cannot *rebind* the caller's variable.

#### 1.5.4 Return passing

Returning by value copies (or, since C++11, usually moves) the result.
Returning by constant reference avoids the copy, but only if the object
outlives the call; returning a reference to a local variable is a dangling
reference and a serious bug. The book shows a `findMax` that returns a
reference into the vector it was given, which is safe because the vector
belongs to the caller.

#### 1.5.5 std::swap and std::move

Swapping two big objects by copying through a temporary costs three
copies. `std::move` does not move anything by itself; it casts its argument
to an rvalue, telling the compiler "treat this as a temporary". The
receiving constructor or assignment can then steal the buffer instead of
copying it. Swap written with three moves costs three pointer swaps, not
three deep copies. After a move, the source object is still valid but its
contents are unspecified; it should only be assigned to or destroyed.

#### 1.5.6 The Big-Five

A class that owns a resource (heap memory, a file, a socket) must manage
five operations, which the book calls the Big-Five:

1. **Destructor**: frees the resource when the object dies.
2. **Copy constructor**: makes a new object as a deep copy of another.
3. **Move constructor**: makes a new object by stealing another's resource
   and leaving it empty.
4. **Copy assignment**: replaces an existing object's contents with a deep
   copy of another's.
5. **Move assignment**: replaces an existing object's contents by stealing.

The compiler writes default versions that copy each member. For a class
whose member is a plain pointer, the default copy copies the *address*, so
two objects own the same buffer, and both destructors free it: a **double
free**. That is why any class with a pointer member must write all five or
explicitly delete the ones it does not support. The modern advice, which
the book also reflects, is to hold resources in standard containers so the
defaults are correct and you write none of them.

A neat technique the book uses is **copy-and-swap**: implement copy
assignment by making a copy of the right-hand side and swapping it with
`*this`. It is correct even for self-assignment and gives the strong
exception guarantee (on failure, the old value is intact).

#### 1.5.7 C-style arrays and strings

A C-style array is a fixed block of elements with no size attached; passing
it to a function passes only a pointer to its first element, so the length
must travel separately. A C-style string is a char array ending in a zero
byte. Neither checks bounds. The book shows them so you can read older code
and so that `vector` and `string` make sense as the safer wrappers.

### Worked example

Simple: JavaScript's version of "pass by value of a reference".

```ts
const a = [1, 2, 3];
const b = a;          // b and a refer to the same array
b.push(4);
console.log(a);       // [ 1, 2, 3, 4 ]

function reassign(x: number[]) { x = [9]; } // rebinds the local name only
function mutate(x: number[]) { x.push(9); } // changes the shared object
const c = [1];
reassign(c); console.log(c); // [ 1 ]
mutate(c);   console.log(c); // [ 1, 9 ]
```

Simple: swap, and a real "move" in JavaScript. An `ArrayBuffer` can be
transferred: the new buffer takes the memory and the old one is left with
length zero, which is exactly the moved-from state.

```ts
let p = 1, q = 2;
[p, q] = [q, p];
console.log(p, q);                  // 2 1
const buf = new ArrayBuffer(8);
const moved = buf.transfer();       // Node 22: move, not copy
console.log(buf.byteLength, moved.byteLength); // 0 8
```

Realistic: the double free, in C++ (my own minimal class).

```cpp
class Buffer {
 public:
  explicit Buffer(size_t n) : n{n}, data{new int[n]} {}
  ~Buffer() { delete[] data; }
  // No copy constructor written: the compiler's default copies the pointer.
 private:
  size_t n;
  int* data;
};
void f() {
  Buffer a{100};
  Buffer b = a;   // b.data == a.data
}                 // b's destructor frees; a's destructor frees again: crash
```

The fix with the Big-Five written out, shown as the two copy operations
plus move (destructor as above):

```cpp
Buffer(const Buffer& rhs) : n{rhs.n}, data{new int[rhs.n]} {
  std::copy(rhs.data, rhs.data + n, data);   // deep copy
}
Buffer(Buffer&& rhs) noexcept : n{rhs.n}, data{rhs.data} {
  rhs.data = nullptr; rhs.n = 0;             // steal, leave empty
}
Buffer& operator=(Buffer rhs) noexcept {     // by value: copy or move made here
  std::swap(n, rhs.n); std::swap(data, rhs.data);
  return *this;                              // rhs's destructor frees the old buffer
}
```

The simpler fix is `std::vector<int> data;` and no hand-written special
members at all.

### Common mistake

In C++: returning a reference to a local variable. The local dies when the
function returns, so the caller holds a reference to nothing, and the
compiler may only warn. Return by value instead; moves make it cheap.

In TypeScript: the same aliasing bug in a garbage-collected costume.
`Array(3).fill([])` creates one inner array shared by all three slots, so
pushing into one pushes into all. `const copy = obj` is not a copy. The
fix is an explicit copy (`[...arr]`, `structuredClone(obj)`) wherever a
value crosses an ownership boundary, such as a cache returning stored
objects to callers who might mutate them.

### In real systems

Ownership bugs are the most expensive bugs in C and C++ systems: use after
free and double free are a large share of serious security problems in
browsers and operating systems, which is why Rust made ownership a compile
time rule. In TypeScript services, the equivalent is shared mutable state:
a module-level object mutated by two requests, a cached object handed out
and changed by a caller, a React state object mutated in place so the
framework does not see a change. The question is identical: who may change
this, and is what I hold a copy or a view?

1. Does `std::move(x)` move anything?

<summary>Answer</summary>

No. It only casts `x` to an rvalue reference so that a move constructor or
move assignment can be chosen. The stealing happens in those functions.

</details>

2. In JavaScript, can a function make the caller's variable point to a
   different array?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No. It can mutate the array the variable refers to, but assigning to its
parameter only rebinds its own local name.

</details>

3. Which of the Big-Five would a class holding only a `std::vector` and an

<details>

correct.

</details>

## 1.6 Templates

### In one sentence

A template writes an algorithm or container once for any type that
supports the operations it uses, which is how one `findMax` or one hash
table serves integers, strings and records.

### The idea

#### 1.6.1 Function templates

Without templates, finding the maximum of a vector of ints and of a vector
of strings needs two nearly identical functions. A **function template**
has a type parameter, written `template <typename Comparable>`, and the
compiler generates a version for each type it is called with. The
generated code is checked when it is generated: if the type lacks the
operation the template uses (say `<`), compilation fails at that call.

TypeScript's **generics** look the same but work differently. The type
parameter is checked once, at the definition, against a constraint, and then
erased; one JavaScript function runs for every type. So in TypeScript I must
pass in or constrain the operations I need.

#### 1.6.2 Class templates

A **class template** is a container parameterised by its element type:
`MemoryCell<int>`, `vector<string>`. Every structure in the book from
chapter 3 on is one. The TypeScript equivalent is `class Stack<T>`.

#### 1.6.3 Object, Comparable, and an example

The book names its type parameters by what they must support. **Object**
means any type that can be copied or moved and default-constructed.
**Comparable** means an Object that also supports `operator<`. When a
container says its element type is Comparable, it means "I will compare
elements and nothing else". The book's example finds the largest of a
vector of a small class by giving that class a less-than operator.

#### 1.6.4 Function objects

Sometimes the type's built-in order is not the one you want: strings
compared case-insensitively, rectangles compared by area. A **function
object** (a functor) is an object whose class defines `operator()`, so it
can be called like a function, and it is passed to the template as the
comparison. The standard library's `less<T>` is the default one. The
JavaScript equivalent is simply a comparator function, which is what
`Array.prototype.sort` takes.

#### 1.6.5 Separate compilation of class templates

Because C++ generates template code at each use, the template's full
definition normally has to be visible where it is used, which is why
templates usually live entirely in headers. The book's appendix A covers
the alternatives.

### Worked example

Simple: generic findMax with a comparator, and its edge case.

```ts
function findMax<T>(xs: readonly T[], less: (x: T, y: T) => boolean): T | undefined {
  if (xs.length === 0) return undefined;     // no maximum of nothing
  let best = xs[0];
  for (const x of xs) if (less(best, x)) best = x;
  return best;
}
console.log(findMax([3, 9, 2], (x, y) => x < y));                        // 9
console.log(findMax(['pear', 'Apple', 'fig'],
  (x, y) => x.toLowerCase() < y.toLowerCase()));                          // pear
console.log(findMax([], (x: number, y: number) => x < y));                // undefined
```

Realistic: JavaScript's own sort shows why the comparator matters. With no
comparator, `sort` converts elements to strings.

```ts
console.log(['b', 'A', 'c'].sort());                              // [ 'A', 'b', 'c' ]
console.log(['b', 'A', 'c'].sort((x, y) => x.localeCompare(y)));  // [ 'A', 'b', 'c' ]
console.log([10, 9, 1].sort());                                   // [ 1, 10, 9 ]
```

The last line is the classic bug: the default order is by string, so 10
comes before 9. Passing `(x, y) => x - y` is passing a function object.

### Common mistake

The mistake is assuming a type's default order is the one you want. In
C++, a Comparable whose `operator<` is not a strict weak ordering (for
example "a.x < b.x or a.y < b.y") breaks sorting and sets in ways that look
random. In JavaScript, sorting numbers without a comparator sorts them as
strings, and a comparator that returns a boolean (`(a, b) => a > b`)
instead of a number gives unspecified results. The fix is a comparator
that returns negative, zero or positive, and is consistent: if a is less
than b and b less than c, then a is less than c.

### In real systems

Every typed collection library is generics: `Map<K, V>`, Java's
`TreeMap<K, V>` with a `Comparator`, Rust's `BTreeMap` requiring `Ord`. In
TypeScript services, a generic `Repository<T>` or `Cache<K, V>` with an
injected key function is the same pattern. Sorting by locale in a UI,
ordering by a composite database key, and ranking search results all come
down to choosing the right function object.

1. What does the name Comparable promise about a type?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That it can be copied or moved like any Object and also supports a
less-than comparison, which is the only comparison the template uses.

</details>

2. Why does `[10, 9, 1].sort()` return `[1, 10, 9]`?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The default comparison converts elements to strings, and "10" sorts before
"9" because "1" comes before "9".

</details>

## 1.7 Using matrices

### In one sentence

A matrix class, a vector of row vectors, shows every idea in the chapter
working together in one small type: a class, a template, an indexing
operator and the Big-Five for free.

### The idea

C++ has no built-in two-dimensional growable array, so the book builds one.

#### 1.7.1 The data members, constructor and basic accessors

The matrix stores a vector of rows, each row a vector of the element type.
The constructor takes the number of rows and columns and sizes every row.
The accessors return the number of rows (the outer vector's size) and the
number of columns (the first row's size).

#### 1.7.2 operator[]

Indexing `m[i][j]` works by having `operator[]` return row i, which is a
vector, and then the vector's own `operator[]` picks column j. Two versions
are needed: one returning a reference for writing, and one returning a
constant reference for reading through a constant matrix. Getting the
return type wrong (returning a copy of the row) makes `m[i][j] = 5` change
a temporary and silently do nothing.

#### 1.7.3 Big-Five

Because every member is a vector, the compiler's default Big-Five are
correct: copying a matrix copies the vector of vectors deeply. The matrix
needs no hand-written special members, which is the "hold resources in
containers" advice proved on a real class.

### Worked example

Simple, in TypeScript: a matrix whose rows are separate arrays, with a
deep clone.

```ts
class Matrix {
  private rows: number[][];
  constructor(r: number, c: number) {
    this.rows = Array.from({ length: r }, () => new Array(c).fill(0));
  }
  get numRows() { return this.rows.length; }
  get numCols() { return this.rows[0]?.length ?? 0; }
  row(i: number): number[] { return this.rows[i]; }    // a view, like T&
  clone(): Matrix { const m = new Matrix(0, 0); m.rows = this.rows.map((r) => [...r]); return m; }
}
const m = new Matrix(2, 3);
m.row(1)[2] = 7;
const m2 = m.clone();
m2.row(1)[2] = 8;
console.log(m.row(1), m2.row(1), m.numRows, m.numCols); // [ 0, 0, 7 ] [ 0, 0, 8 ] 2 3
```

Realistic: the bug this design avoids.

```ts
const wrong = Array(2).fill(Array(2).fill(0)); // one row object, shared twice
wrong[0][0] = 5;
console.log(JSON.stringify(wrong));            // [[5,0],[5,0]]
```

Both rows are the same array, so writing one cell writes the whole column.
`Array.from` with a function creates a fresh row per index, which is what
the C++ constructor's loop does.

### Step by step

<Figure caption="The matrix as a vector of rows. m[1] returns the second row by reference, then [2] indexes into it. Look at the arrows: each row is its own block, so a deep copy copies three blocks." art={`
  m.rows ──▶ +---------+---------+

1. `new Matrix(2, 3)` builds an outer array of two slots and fills each
   with a fresh array of three zeros.
2. `m.row(1)` returns the second row itself, not a copy.
3. `[2] = 7` writes into that row, so the matrix changes.
4. `clone()` maps each row to a spread copy: new outer array, new rows.
5. Writing into the clone leaves the original untouched.

### Common mistake

Returning a copy of a row from the indexing method in C++
(`vector<Object> operator[](int)`) makes writes vanish, because they go to
the temporary. In TypeScript the opposite mistake is common: returning the
internal row when callers should not be able to change it. Decide per
method whether you are handing out a view or a copy, and name it.

### In real systems

Images, game boards, spreadsheets, dynamic programming tables (chapter 10)
and adjacency matrices (chapter 9) are matrices. Production numeric code
stores them as one flat typed array indexed by `i * cols + j`, because one
contiguous block is far friendlier to the cache than an array of separate
rows; `Float64Array` in JavaScript and every numerical library do this. The
row-of-rows version is clearer and is the right first version.

### Check yourself

1. Why does the matrix class need no hand-written copy constructor?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Its only resource is held in vectors, whose own copy constructors copy
deeply, so the default memberwise copy is a deep copy.

</details>

2. What does `Array(3).fill([])` give, and why is it a bug for a matrix?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Three slots all pointing at one empty array. Writing a cell of one row
writes the same cell of every row.

</details>

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 1, Programming: A General Overview">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level the chapter gives three thinking tools. A logarithm
counts halvings: log₂ of a million is about 20, so anything that halves its
work each step finishes a million-item problem in about 20 steps. A
geometric series is bounded by its last term times a constant:
1 + 2 + 4 + ... + n is less than 2n. And recursion solves a problem by
trusting a smaller copy of itself, as long as there is a base case.

A worked example that uses all three: binary search on a sorted array of
1,000 names. Each comparison halves the range, so at most about 10
comparisons are needed (log₂ 1000 is just under 10). Written recursively,
the base case is an empty range (not found) and every call is on half the
range, so rule 2 holds.

The beginner mistake is writing the recursive version with the range
`[lo, mid]` instead of `[lo, mid - 1]`, so a range of one element never
shrinks and the recursion never ends. The fix is checking that every call
makes the range strictly smaller.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level the pieces combine into a small, correct
program: a generic class with a private representation and a comparator.
Here is a tiny "keep the best k" structure, which is the selection idea of
section 1.1 written with the habits of sections 1.4 and 1.6.

```ts
class TopK<T> {
  #items: T[] = [];
  constructor(private k: number, private less: (a: T, b: T) => boolean) {}
  offer(x: T): void {
    const xs = this.#items;
    if (xs.length === this.k && !this.less(xs[xs.length - 1], x)) return;
    let i = xs.length < this.k ? xs.length : xs.length - 1;
    while (i > 0 && this.less(xs[i - 1], x)) { xs[i] = xs[i - 1]; i--; }
    xs[i] = x;
  }
  values(): readonly T[] { return [...this.#items]; } // a copy, not a view
}
const t = new TopK<number>(3, (a, b) => a < b);
for (const x of [8, 2, 9, 4, 7, 1]) t.offer(x);
console.log(t.values()); // [ 9, 8, 7 ]
```

It hides its array, takes the ordering as a function object, and returns a
copy so callers cannot break the sorted invariant. Its cost is up to k
shifts per offer, which chapter 6 improves with a heap. The intermediate
mistake is returning `this.#items` directly from `values()`; a caller that
sorts or pushes into it silently corrupts the structure.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

The advanced reading is about exact rules. In C++, the five special members
interact by precise rules: declaring a destructor or a copy operation stops
the compiler generating the move operations, so a class with a
hand-written destructor and nothing else silently copies where it could
move. Marking the move constructor `noexcept` matters because `vector`
only moves elements during a resize if the move cannot throw; otherwise it
copies to keep its strong guarantee.

On the maths side, the exact statement behind "log n is cheap" is that for
any constant greater than zero, log n divided by n to that constant tends
to zero. Behind "the harmonic number is about ln n" is the bound
ln(n + 1) at most Hₙ at most 1 + ln n, which comes from comparing the sum
with the area under the curve 1/x.

A worked check: with n equal to 1000, ln 1001 is about 6.909, H is about
7.486 and 1 + ln 1000 is about 7.908. The bounds hold. The advanced
mistake is treating these approximations as equalities in a precise
calculation; they are bounds, and the gap is a constant.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Each tool has specific boundaries.

- **log of 1 is 0, log of 0 is undefined.** `Math.log2(0)` is `-Infinity`
  and `Math.log2(-1)` is `NaN`. Analysis assumes n at least 1; code that
  computes a tree height from a log must handle the empty case.
- **The halving count of 1 is 0.** A loop `while (n > 1)` does nothing for
  n equal to 1, which is right, but `while (n >= 1)` runs forever for
  `n = n / 2` with real division.
- **`%` with a zero divisor.** `5 % 0` is `NaN` in JavaScript, not an
  exception, so a hash table sized 0 fails quietly.
- **Recursion depth.** `sumTo(10000)` works in Node; `sumTo(1e6)` throws
  `RangeError`. The base case is fine; the stack is the limit.
- **`findMax` of an empty array.** There is no maximum. My version returns
  `undefined`; the C++ version in the book would read past the end. Decide
  and document.
- **Self-assignment `a = a`.** A copy assignment that frees its buffer
  before copying from the right-hand side destroys the data it is about to
  copy. Copy-and-swap handles it.

The mistake common to all of them is assuming the smallest input behaves
like a typical one. Test n equal to 0, 1 and 2 on purpose.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases are two boundaries at once.

- **A negative number and a ring buffer of size 1.** `((i - 1) % 1 + 1) % 1`
  is 0, correct, but `(i - 1) % 1` alone is `-0` for i equal to 0, and
  `arr[-0]` is `arr[0]`, so it works by luck. Change the size and it breaks.
- **A moved-from object that is then copied.** In C++, `Buffer b =
  std::move(a); Buffer c = a;` compiles and copies an empty buffer; any code
  that assumed `a` still held data reads nothing.
- **Large integers and modular arithmetic.** Computing a hash as
  `h = h * 31 + code` in JavaScript without reducing loses precision after
  2⁵³, because `2 ** 53 + 1 === 2 ** 53` is `true`. Reducing at each step
  (`>>> 0` or `% size`) keeps it exact, using the congruence rules of
  section 1.2.4.
- **Memoisation with an object key.** `memo.get([1, 2])` never hits,
  because each array literal is a new object. The key must be a primitive
  such as `'1,2'`.
- **Generic code plus `NaN`.** `findMax([1, NaN, 3], (a, b) => a < b)`
  returns 3 or 1 depending on where `NaN` sits, because every comparison
  with `NaN` is false.

The mistake is testing each boundary alone. Property tests that generate
random combinations catch these pairs.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, this chapter's ideas show up as three kinds of incident.

The first is a **stack overflow from recursion on user input**. A service
that recursively validates JSON accepts a deeply nested body and crashes.
The fix is a depth limit, or an iterative walk with an explicit stack.

The second is **shared mutable state**, the garbage-collected version of
the double free. A cache returns the stored object; one request mutates it;
the next request sees the change. The fix is to return copies or freeze
cached values (`Object.freeze`, or immutable data structures).

The third is **an estimate that ignored growth**. A job that does a
quadratic amount of work per tenant runs fine until the largest tenant
signs up. The chapter's habit, estimating with logs and series before
writing, is the cheap fix.

Observability turns these into numbers: record the recursion depth or
input size alongside latency, and the growth curve shows up in the
dashboard before it becomes an outage.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset the chapter teaches is "know the answer before you measure".
Weiss wants an estimate from maths first, then code, then a measurement to
confirm it. If the measurement disagrees, one of the two is wrong, and
finding which is how you learn.

An example: before running the naive Fibonacci, predict the call count for
n equal to 30. The recurrence says it is about 2F(31), roughly 2.7 million.
The run printed 2,692,537. The prediction was right, so I trust the model;
if it had printed 60, I would know my mental model of the code was wrong.

The second habit is ownership thinking. For every value I hand out, I ask:
is this a copy or a view, and who may change it? That question applies to a
C++ buffer, a TypeScript cache entry, a database row and an API response
alike. The mistake is thinking memory questions disappear in a garbage
collected language; only the freeing does.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

At system scale, ownership becomes data ownership. A concrete example: an
orders service and a billing service both need order records. If both write
the same table, a schema change in one breaks the other, and neither knows
who is allowed to delete a row. That is two objects sharing one buffer. The
fix is a single owner (the orders service) with a published interface
(an API or an event stream), and billing keeping its own copy that it is
free to shape. Copy versus view is the design decision.

Recursion and the four rules appear in system design as fan-out. A request
that calls three services, each of which calls three more, does work that
grows exponentially with depth. Rule 4 is caching or request
de-duplication so that the same sub-request is not made twice.

Logarithms size the system: a B-tree index over a billion rows with a
fan-out of a few hundred is four levels deep, so a lookup is about four
page reads. The mistake is designing without these estimates and finding
the cost in the first load test.

</Level>

## Questions people ask

**Do I need to know C++ to read this book?** No. The analysis, which is the
point, does not depend on the language. Reading the C++ code helps, and
sections 1.4 to 1.6 give enough to do that. I write everything in
TypeScript.

**Why base 2 for logarithms?** Because the algorithms halve things. Any
other base changes the answer only by a constant factor, which big-O
ignores, so base 2 is chosen for convenience.

**Is recursion slower than a loop?** Often a little, because of the frame
per call, and it risks the stack limit. A recursion that obeys rule 4 has
the same big-O as the equivalent loop. Use recursion where it makes the
code clearer, such as trees, and loops for long linear walks.

**What is the difference between memoisation and dynamic programming?**
Memoisation caches the results of a top-down recursion. Dynamic
programming usually fills a table bottom-up in an order that guarantees
sub-answers exist. Both obey rule 4; chapter 10 covers the second.

**Why does JavaScript's `%` give negative results?** It computes the
remainder of truncating division, whose sign follows the dividend. Many
languages do the same. Python's `%` is a true modulus. Know which your
language has.

**Is the Big-Five the same as the "rule of three"?** It is the C++11
extension of it. The rule of three named the destructor and the two copy
operations; moves added two more, making five. The "rule of zero" says
write none by holding resources in standard types.

**Do TypeScript generics cost anything at run time?** No. They are erased.
The cost is that you cannot ask a type parameter at run time what type it
is, so operations must be passed in or constrained.

## Key takeaways

- Every structure in the book comes with its running time, and so should
  every structure I build.
- log₂ n is the number of halvings from n to 1; about 20 for a million.
- The sum of powers of two up to 2ⁿ is 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1, which is why doubling
  costs only constant work per item on average.
- The arithmetic series is about n²/2 and the harmonic series about ln n.
- JavaScript's `%` is a remainder; use `((a % n) + n) % n` for a modulus.
- Recursion needs a base case, progress, trust in smaller calls, and no
  repeated subproblems.
- A class with a raw owning pointer needs the Big-Five; a class holding
  containers needs none.
- Copy or view is the question behind most aliasing bugs, in any language.
- Generic code takes its comparison as a function object, and the default
  comparison is often not the one you want.

## Glossary

- **Algorithm**: a finite, precise sequence of steps that solves a problem.
- **Data structure**: a way of organising data so some operations are cheap.
- **Logarithm**: the exponent that produces a number; log₂ n counts halvings.
- **Geometric series**: a sum of powers of a fixed ratio.
- **Arithmetic series**: the sum 1 + 2 + ... + n, equal to n(n + 1)/2.
- **Harmonic number**: Hₙ = 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n, about ln n.
- **Congruent modulo n**: leaving the same remainder when divided by n.
- **Proof by induction**: base case plus "if true up to k, true for k + 1".
- **Counterexample**: one case that disproves a general claim.
- **Proof by contradiction**: assume the opposite, derive the impossible.
- **Recursion**: a function defined in terms of itself on smaller input.
- **Base case**: an input answered without recursion.
- **Stack frame**: the memory for one function call's parameters and locals.
- **Memoisation**: caching a function's results by its input.
- **Encapsulation**: hiding a class's data behind its methods.
- **Constructor**: the function that makes a new object valid.
- **Accessor and mutator**: methods that read state and change state.
- **Pointer**: a variable holding a memory address.
- **Dangling pointer**: a pointer to memory that has been freed.
- **Lvalue and rvalue**: a lasting named object, and a temporary.
- **Reference**: another name for an existing object.
- **Move**: transferring a resource from one object to another without copying.
- **Big-Five**: destructor, copy and move constructors, copy and move assignment.
- **Double free**: freeing the same memory twice, usually from a shallow copy.
- **Template / generic**: code parameterised by a type.
- **Comparable**: a type that supports less-than.
- **Function object**: a callable object passed in to customise behaviour.

Self-check:

<details>
<summary>Why does doubling a buffer cost only about one copy per push on average?</summary>

The copies are 1 + 2 + 4 + ... up to the final size, a geometric series
bounded by twice the final size. Spread over all the pushes, that is a
constant per push.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the fourth rule of recursion, and what breaks it?</summary>

Never solve the same instance twice in separate calls. Naive Fibonacci
breaks it and becomes exponential.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does `mod(-7, 3)` return, and what does `-7 % 3` return?</summary>

2 and −1.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What happens when a class with a raw pointer member is copied by default?</summary>

Both objects hold the same address; both destructors free it; the second
free is undefined behaviour.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does `[10, 9, 1].sort()` give `[1, 10, 9]`?</summary>

Without a comparator, elements are compared as strings.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is the harmonic number about ln n and not about n?</summary>

The terms shrink like 1/i, and the area under 1/x from 1 to n is ln n. The
sum tracks that area within a constant.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between `private` and `#` fields in TypeScript?</summary>

`private` is checked only at compile time; `#` fields are hidden by the
JavaScript engine at run time.

</details>
