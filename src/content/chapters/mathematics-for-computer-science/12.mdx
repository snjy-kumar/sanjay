Chapter 11 treated a graph as pure structure: who is joined to whom. This
chapter asks a geometric question about that structure. Can you draw the
graph on a flat sheet so that no two edges cross? Circuit boards care,
because every crossing costs an extra layer or a via. People reading a
diagram care, because crossings make it hard to follow. And mathematicians
care, because the answer turns out to be governed by a single counting
law, Euler's formula, that links vertices, edges and the regions the
drawing cuts the plane into. The chapter's real lesson is a method: it
replaces pictures, which are easy to get wrong, with a recursive data type
of "faces", and then proves everything by structural induction. That move,
from a continuous idea to a discrete definition you can compute with, is
exactly what this page does in TypeScript.

## 12.1 Drawing Graphs in the Plane

### In one sentence

Some graphs can be drawn on paper with no crossing edges and some cannot,
and the two smallest troublemakers are K3,3 (three things each wired to
three others) and K5 (five things all wired to each other).

### The idea

#### Two puzzles

Picture three services, each of which must be wired to each of three data
centres, on a single-layer board where wires may not cross. That is the
complete bipartite graph **K3,3**: two sides of three vertices, every
left vertex joined to every right vertex, nine edges. Or picture five
offices that each want a direct private line to every other, again on one
layer. That is the complete graph **K5**: five vertices, ten edges.

Both puzzles ask whether a graph has a **planar drawing**: vertices as
points, edges as curves between their endpoints, and no two curves meeting
except at shared endpoints. Try it on paper and you will get stuck on the
last edge every time. The answer for both is "no, but nearly": delete any
one edge and the rest can be drawn without crossings.

#### Why it matters

- **Circuit layout.** On a printed circuit board or a chip, wires on one layer cannot cross. Every unavoidable crossing needs a via to another layer, which adds cost, area and points of failure. Designers fight hard for nearly planar layouts.
- **Readable diagrams.** Flow charts, org charts, dependency graphs and conflict graphs are easier to read with fewer crossings. Graph drawing software tries to minimise them, a problem that is hard in general.
- **Maps.** Countries sharing borders form a planar graph, which is where the famous four colour question comes from (12.6).

The chapter will prove both puzzles impossible, but first it needs a
definition that a proof can stand on.

### Worked example

**Simple: how many crossings does a straight-line drawing have?** Place
the points, draw every edge as a straight segment, and count pairs of
segments that cross at an interior point:

```ts
type P = [number, number];
const cross = (o: P, a: P, b: P) => (a[0] - o[0]) * (b[1] - o[1]) - (a[1] - o[1]) * (b[0] - o[0]);
const proper = (p: P, q: P, r: P, s: P) => cross(p, q, r) * cross(p, q, s) < 0 && cross(r, s, p) * cross(r, s, q) < 0;
function crossings(pts: P[]) {
  const E: [number, number][] = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < pts.length; i++) for (let j = i + 1; j < pts.length; j++) E.push([i, j]);
  let c = 0;
  for (let x = 0; x < E.length; x++) for (let y = x + 1; y < E.length; y++) {
    const [a, b] = E[x], [d, e] = E[y];
    if (new Set([a, b, d, e]).size < 4) continue; // edges sharing an endpoint do not cross
    if (proper(pts[a], pts[b], pts[d], pts[e])) c++;
  }
  return c;
}
const pentagon: P[] = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4].map((k) => [Math.round(100 * Math.cos((2 * Math.PI * k) / 5)), Math.round(100 * Math.sin((2 * Math.PI * k) / 5))]);
// K3,3: only left-right edges exist; count crossing pairs among them
const k33 = (L: P[], R: P[]) => { let c = 0; for (const a of L) for (const b of R) for (const d of L) for (const e of R) if (a !== d && b !== e && proper(a, b, d, e)) c++; return c / 2; };
console.log('K5 on a convex pentagon:', crossings(pentagon), 'crossings');
console.log('K5 on a big triangle with two points inside:', crossings([[0, 0], [100, 0], [50, 90], [45, 30], [55, 25]]), 'crossing(s)');
console.log('K3,3 with sides on two parallel lines:', k33([[0, 0], [50, 0], [100, 0]], [[0, 50], [50, 50], [100, 50]]), 'crossings');
console.log('K3,3 on a hexagon, sides alternating:', k33([[100, 0], [-50, 87], [-50, -87]], [[50, 87], [-100, 0], [50, -87]]), 'crossings');
```

```text
K5 on a convex pentagon: 5 crossings
K5 on a big triangle with two points inside: 1 crossing(s)
K3,3 with sides on two parallel lines: 9 crossings
K3,3 on a hexagon, sides alternating: 3 crossings
```

On a convex pentagon, every choice of four points gives one crossing (the
two diagonals of that quadrilateral), and there are five ways to choose
four of five points. Moving two points inside a triangle drops it to one.
For K3,3, two parallel rows give one crossing for every pair of left
vertices and pair of right vertices, 3 × 3 = 9; alternating the sides
around a hexagon leaves only the three long diagonals, which cross in the
middle. One crossing is the best possible for both graphs (each has
crossing number 1), but no placement reaches zero. Section 12.5 proves it.

**Realistic: the layout question in a design review.** A board has three
power rails and three components, each needing every rail. Before anyone
tries layouts for an afternoon, the question "is K3,3 planar?" settles it:
at least one via is required. Recognising the graph saves the search.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** "I tried for ten minutes and could not draw it without
crossings, so it is not planar."

**Why it fails:** failing to find a drawing proves nothing. The cube and
the octahedron have tangled-looking drawings that untangle completely.

**The right way:** prove non-planarity with an invariant that every
planar graph satisfies (the edge bounds of 12.4 and 12.5) or by finding a
K5 or K3,3 minor (12.8). Prove planarity by exhibiting a drawing.

### In real systems

- **PCB and chip routing:** layer assignment and via minimisation are planarity questions at heart.
- **Graph drawing tools** run layout heuristics that reduce crossings for dependency graphs, call graphs and state machines.
- **Utility networks:** pipes and cables on one level behave like a planar drawing; crossings mean bridges, tunnels or extra depth.

## 12.2 Definitions of Planar Graphs

### In one sentence

A planar drawing is easy to picture but hard to reason about rigorously,
so the book defines a **planar embedding** as a set of closed walks called
faces, built recursively from one vertex by two constructors, "split a
face" and "add a bridge".

### The idea

A **drawing** assigns each vertex a distinct point and each edge a smooth
curve between its endpoints' points. It is **planar** if no curve crosses
itself or another curve, meaning the only points on more than one curve
are vertex points. A graph is **planar** if it has a planar drawing.

That definition leans on "smooth curve" and "crossing", which belong to
continuous mathematics and topology. Proofs built on pictures have a long
history of going wrong. The book's way out is to find a purely discrete
description of what a planar drawing does, in terms of faces.

#### 12.2.1 Faces

A planar drawing cuts the plane into connected regions, the **continuous
faces**. One of them, the **outer face**, extends to infinity. For K4
drawn as a triangle with a centre vertex, there are four faces: three
small triangles and the outside.

Walking around the boundary of a face traces a sequence of vertices. For
nice drawings this is a cycle, and the cycles identify the faces. For K4
with vertices a, b, c on the triangle and d in the middle, the boundaries
are abd, bcd, cad and the outer abc. These cycles are the **discrete
faces**.

Two complications stop every face boundary from being a cycle:

- **Bridges.** A bridge is a cut edge. Walking around the face on both sides of a bridge, you traverse it twice, once in each direction, so the boundary is a closed walk that repeats vertices and the edge.
- **Dongles.** A tree hanging into a face (the book's name for a tree made of bridges) is walked around completely: down each branch and back. Every edge of the dongle appears twice in that face's boundary.

For connected graphs these are the only complications: every continuous
face corresponds to a **closed walk**, and those closed walks are the
discrete faces.

#### 12.2.2 A Recursive Definition for Planar Embeddings

Think about drawing a planar graph step by step: either put down a new
point, or draw a new curve between existing points without crossing
anything. A new curve avoids crossings exactly when it stays inside one
face, or when it joins the outer faces of two separate drawings. That
suggests the definition.

A **planar embedding** of a connected graph is a nonempty set of closed
walks of the graph, its **discrete faces**, defined recursively:

- **Base case.** A single vertex v has an embedding with one face, the length-0 closed walk v.
- **Constructor 1, split a face.** Suppose G has an embedding, and a and b are distinct, nonadjacent vertices on the same face γ. Write γ as α then β, where α is a walk from a to b and β is a walk from b back to a. Adding the edge a—b gives an embedding of the bigger graph whose faces are G's faces with γ replaced by two faces: α followed by the new edge back to a, and the new edge from a to b followed by β. (One special case: if G is a path from a to b, both new faces are the same cycle, and the definition keeps two copies, one for the inside and one for the outside.)
- **Constructor 2, add a bridge.** Suppose G and H are connected, with embeddings and disjoint vertex sets. Pick a face γ of G that starts and ends at a, and a face δ of H that starts and ends at b. Joining G and H by the new edge a—b gives an embedding whose faces are all the faces of both, except that γ and δ are replaced by one combined face: go around γ back to a, cross to b, go around δ back to b, and cross back to a.

In an embedding, the **bridges are exactly the edges that occur twice in
one face**; every other edge occurs once in each of two different faces.

#### 12.2.3 Does It Work?

The book asserts, without proof, that a graph has a planar drawing exactly
when each connected component has a planar embedding in this recursive
sense. Proving that equivalence requires the continuous mathematics the
definition was designed to avoid. Once accepted, every later proof uses
only the discrete definition.

The price is that the definition is more technical than "no crossings".
Some facts that are obvious for drawings (deleting an edge keeps a graph
planar) take real work to prove for embeddings. The book suggests that
pictures are fine for intuition and for experts, but precise definitions
are safer for proofs. And there is a second lesson: a continuous idea
(regions of the plane) can be modelled faithfully by a discrete data type
(sets of closed walks), which is exactly what software has to do.

#### 12.2.4 Where Did the Outer Face Go?

A drawing has an obvious outer face; an embedding has none. All faces
are treated alike. The reason is that a planar drawing is really a
drawing on a sphere: puncture the sphere inside any face, stretch the hole
wide and flatten, and that face becomes the outside. So drawings with
different outer boundaries can be pictures of the same embedding, if
their sets of face boundaries agree. This is also why the bridge
constructor may use any face of each graph: treat the chosen faces as the
outer ones and the bridge can be drawn between them without crossings.

### Worked example

**Simple: the embedding data type in code.** Faces are arrays of vertices
read cyclically (consecutive entries are joined by an edge, and the last
joins the first). A single vertex's face is the one-element array:

```ts
type Emb = { V: Set<string>; E: Set<string>; F: string[][] };
const key = (a: string, b: string) => (a < b ? `${a}-${b}` : `${b}-${a}`);
const single = (v: string): Emb => ({ V: new Set([v]), E: new Set(), F: [[v]] });

function split(G: Emb, fi: number, a: string, b: string): Emb {
  const f = G.F[fi]; const i = f.indexOf(a);
  const rot = [...f.slice(i), ...f.slice(0, i)];          // the face read from a
  const j = rot.indexOf(b);
  if (j < 0 || G.E.has(key(a, b)) || a === b) throw new Error('bad split');
  const f1 = rot.slice(0, j + 1);                          // a ... b, closed by the new edge b—a
  const f2 = [...rot.slice(j), a];                         // b ... a, closed by the new edge a—b
  return { V: new Set(G.V), E: new Set([...G.E, key(a, b)]), F: G.F.filter((_, k) => k !== fi).concat([f1, f2]) };
}

function bridge(G: Emb, fg: number, a: string, H: Emb, fh: number, b: string): Emb {
  const rotTo = (f: string[], x: string) => { const i = f.indexOf(x); return [...f.slice(i), ...f.slice(0, i)]; };
  const g = rotTo(G.F[fg], a), h = rotTo(H.F[fh], b);
  const face = [...(g.length === 1 ? [a] : [...g, a]), ...(h.length === 1 ? [b] : [...h, b])];
  return { V: new Set([...G.V, ...H.V]), E: new Set([...G.E, ...H.E, key(a, b)]),
           F: [...G.F.filter((_, k) => k !== fg), ...H.F.filter((_, k) => k !== fh), face] };
}
```

**Realistic: building K4 one constructor at a time.** Start from a single
vertex, add bridges to make a path, then split faces to add the chords,
printing the faces and v − e + f after each step:

```text
a—b         faces: ab  v-e+f = 2
+ b—c       faces: babc  v-e+f = 2
+ a—c split faces: abc cba  v-e+f = 2
+ a—d       faces: cba abcad  v-e+f = 2
+ d—b split faces: cba dab bcad  v-e+f = 2
+ d—c split faces: cba dab dbc cad  v-e+f = 2  (K4: v=4 e=6 f=4)
```

Read the faces carefully. After the first bridge, the only face is the
walk a, b, back to a: the edge a—b appears twice, as a bridge should.
After adding c, the face b, a, b, c (then back to b) walks both bridges
twice. Splitting at a and c produces the triangle twice, abc and cba,
which is the special "line graph" case: inside and outside of the same
cycle. Adding d as a bridge hangs a dongle into one face, which then
reads a, b, c, a, d (a appears twice). Two more splits turn K4's last
edges into faces, ending with four triangles. The fourth, cba, is the
"outer" triangle, though the embedding does not know that.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** assuming every face of a planar drawing is bounded by a
cycle, and so "a face is a cycle of the graph".

**Why it fails:** bridges and dongles appear twice in a face boundary. A
tree has exactly one face, whose boundary walks every edge twice.

**The right way:** faces are closed walks. Count edge occurrences, not
edges: across all faces, every edge occurs exactly twice, either once in
each of two faces or twice in one.

### In real systems

- **Computational geometry** stores planar subdivisions as half-edge (doubly connected edge list) structures, which are exactly faces as closed walks with each edge used once in each direction.
- **Map and GIS software** represents regions, borders and adjacency with the same face-based structure.
- **Mesh processing** for 3D graphics stores surfaces as faces around vertices; Euler's formula is a standard sanity check on meshes.

## 12.3 Euler's Formula

### In one sentence

For every planar embedding of a connected graph, vertices minus edges plus
faces equals 2, so the number of faces is fixed by the graph alone, not by
how you draw it.

### The idea

**Theorem (Euler's formula).** If a connected graph has a planar
embedding with v vertices, e edges and f faces, then v − e + f = 2.

For K4 drawn as a triangle with a centre vertex: 4 − 6 + 4 = 2. For a
tree with n vertices: n − (n − 1) + 1 = 2, since a tree has one face. For
a cycle C_n: n − n + 2 = 2, inside and outside.

**Proof by structural induction on the recursive definition.** Let P(E) be
"v − e + f = 2 for the embedding E". Structural induction needs the base
case and one argument per constructor.

- **Base case.** One vertex, no edges, one face (the length-0 walk): 1 − 0 + 1 = 2.
- **Split a face.** The new embedding has the same vertices, one more edge and one more face (one face replaced by two). The changes cancel: v − (e + 1) + (f + 1) = v − e + f, which is 2 by the hypothesis for G.
- **Add a bridge.** Joining G and H gives v_G + v_H vertices, e_G + e_H + 1 edges, and f_G + f_H − 1 faces (two faces merge into one). Then the total is (v_G − e_G + f_G) + (v_H − e_H + f_H) − 1 − 1, which is 2 + 2 − 2 = 2 by the hypotheses for G and H.

Both constructors preserve the formula, so it holds for every embedding.

Two things are worth noticing about the shape of this proof. First, it is
the reward for the technical definition: with drawings, you would have to
argue about regions of the plane; with the recursive type, each case is
one line of arithmetic. Second, the bridge case uses **two** hypotheses,
one for each part, which is natural for structural induction on a
constructor with two inputs and awkward for ordinary induction on n.

The formula needs **connectedness**. A graph with c components drawn in
the plane has v − e + f = 1 + c, because the components share one outer
face. Two disjoint triangles: 6 − 6 + 3 = 3 = 1 + 2.

### Worked example

**Simple: Euler on familiar shapes.** Cube: 8 corners, 12 edges, 6 faces:
8 − 12 + 6 = 2. Octahedron: 6 − 12 + 8 = 2. A square grid of 3 × 3 cells
(16 points, 24 unit edges, 9 cells plus the outside): 16 − 24 + 10 = 2. A
star with one centre and five leaves: 6 − 5 + 1 = 2.

**Realistic: testing the formula on 500 random embeddings.** Starting from
one vertex, apply 120 random constructor steps (about one in five adds a
bridge to a new vertex; the rest split a random face at two random
nonadjacent vertices on it), then check three things: Euler's formula,
that every edge occurs exactly twice across the face boundaries, and that
the face lengths sum to 2e:

```ts
let ok = 0, edgeBound = 0, n = 0;
for (let t = 0; t < 500; t++) {
  let G = single('v0'); let next = 1;
  for (let s = 0; s < 120; s++) {
    if (rnd() < 0.2) { const fgi = Math.floor(rnd() * G.F.length); const a = pick(G.F[fgi]); G = bridge(G, fgi, a, single(`v${next}`), 0, `v${next}`); next++; }
    else { const fi = Math.floor(rnd() * G.F.length); const f = G.F[fi]; const a = pick(f), b = pick(f); if (a !== b && !G.E.has(key(a, b))) G = split(G, fi, a, b); }
  }
  const occ = new Map<string, number>();
  for (const f of G.F) if (f.length > 1) for (let i = 0; i < f.length; i++) { const k = key(f[i], f[(i + 1) % f.length]); occ.set(k, (occ.get(k) ?? 0) + 1); }
  const twice = [...G.E].every((e) => occ.get(e) === 2);
  const lenSum = G.F.reduce((s, f) => s + (f.length === 1 ? 0 : f.length), 0);
  if (G.V.size - G.E.size + G.F.length === 2 && twice && lenSum === 2 * G.E.size) ok++;
  if (G.V.size >= 3) { n++; if (G.E.size <= 3 * G.V.size - 6) edgeBound++; }
}
```

```text
500 random embeddings: Euler, edge-twice and length-sum 2e held in 500; e <= 3v-6 held in 500/500; largest v=36 e=57
```

All 500 pass, as the proof says they must. The same run also checks the
bound of the next section. The code is a useful model of what the proof
does: each constructor call is one inductive step, and the checks are the
invariant.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** applying v − e + f = 2 to a disconnected graph, or to a
drawing that has crossings.

**Why it fails:** with c components the sum is 1 + c; with crossings the
"faces" are not faces of an embedding at all.

**The right way:** check the hypotheses: a connected graph, and an actual
planar embedding. For c components, use v − e + f = 1 + c.

### In real systems

- **Mesh validation:** a closed triangle mesh of a ball-like object must satisfy v − e + f = 2; a different value means holes, handles or broken topology. (For a surface with g handles, the value is 2 − 2g.)
- **Map data:** checking v − e + f on a planar map's vertices, borders and regions catches missing or duplicated borders.
- **Algorithm analysis:** Euler's formula is why planar graphs have O(v) edges and faces, which keeps many planar algorithms linear.

## 12.4 Bounding the Number of Edges in a Planar Graph

### In one sentence

Because every face has length at least 3 and every edge is counted twice
around the faces, a connected planar graph with at least 3 vertices has at
most 3v − 6 edges.

### The idea

Two lemmas follow from the recursive definition by structural induction:

- **Lemma A.** In a planar embedding of a connected graph, each edge occurs once in each of two different faces, or exactly twice in one face.
- **Lemma B.** In a planar embedding of a connected graph with at least 3 vertices, every face has length at least 3.

**Theorem.** A connected planar graph with v at least 3 vertices and e
edges has e at most 3v − 6.

**Proof.** Take a planar embedding with f faces. Add up the lengths of all
the face boundaries. By Lemma A, every edge contributes exactly 2, so the
total is exactly 2e. By Lemma B, each face contributes at least 3, so the
total is at least 3f. Hence 3f is at most 2e. Euler gives f = e − v + 2,
so 3(e − v + 2) is at most 2e, that is, e − 3v + 6 is at most 0, which
rearranges to e at most 3v − 6.

The proof is a **double count**: one quantity (total face length) counted
by edges and by faces. That technique, with the handshaking lemma
alongside it, does most of the work in this chapter.

**Consequences worth remembering:**

- Planar graphs are **sparse**: fewer than 3v edges, so average degree below 6.
- **Equality** e = 3v − 6 holds exactly when every face is a triangle; such graphs are **triangulations** (maximal planar graphs). You cannot add any edge to them and stay planar.
- The number of faces is also linear: f at most 2v − 4.

### Worked example

**Simple: how many edges can a planar graph have?** With 10 vertices, at
most 24. With 100 vertices, at most 294. Compare the complete graph,
which has v(v − 1)/2 edges: 45 and 4,950. Planar graphs use a vanishing
fraction of the possible edges.

**Realistic: random triangulations hit the bound exactly.** Generate
planar triangulations by repeatedly inserting a new vertex into a random
triangular face (joining it to the face's three corners), then perform
many random edge flips (replace the shared edge of two adjacent triangles
by the other diagonal, when that edge is not already present). Every
result should have exactly 3v − 6 edges:

```ts
function triangulation(n: number, flips: number) {
  let F: number[][] = [[0, 1, 2], [0, 2, 1]]; // a triangle: its inside and its outside
  for (let v = 3; v < n; v++) {
    const i = Math.floor(rnd() * F.length); const [a, b, c] = F[i];
    F.splice(i, 1, [a, b, v], [b, c, v], [c, a, v]);
  }
  // ... random flips of the edge shared by faces (a,b,c) and (b,a,d) into c—d ...
  return edgesOf(F);
}
```

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** "the graph has at most 3v − 6 edges, so it is planar."

**Why it fails:** the bound is necessary, not sufficient. K3,3 has 9 edges
and 3 · 6 − 6 = 12, so it passes, yet it is not planar.

**The right way:** use the bound only to rule graphs **out**. To show a
graph is planar, give an embedding (or run a planarity algorithm).

### In real systems

- **Data structure sizing:** a planar map, road network (approximately planar) or mesh with v vertices has at most 3v − 6 edges, so adjacency lists use linear memory.
- **Algorithm design:** many planar graph algorithms rely on sparsity and on the existence of low-degree vertices.
- **Mesh statistics:** a closed triangle mesh of a ball-like surface has e = 3v − 6 and f = 2v − 4 exactly, so triangle count is about twice the vertex count, a rule of thumb graphics programmers use for memory budgets.

## 12.5 Returning to K5 and K3,3

### In one sentence

K5 breaks the 3v − 6 bound, and K3,3, which has no triangles, breaks the
sharper bound 2v − 4 that holds for bipartite planar graphs, so neither
graph is planar.

### The idea

**Corollary: K5 is not planar.** It is connected with 5 vertices and 10
edges, and 10 is more than 3 · 5 − 6 = 9.

K3,3 has 9 edges and 6 vertices, well within 3 · 6 − 6 = 12, so the
general bound is silent. The extra fact needed is that K3,3 is bipartite.

**Lemma.** In a planar embedding of a connected bipartite graph with at
least 3 vertices, every face has length at least 4.

**Proof.** Every face has length at least 3 (Lemma B). A face is a closed
walk, and a bipartite graph has no closed walks of odd length (chapter
11: bipartite, 2-colourable and odd-cycle-free are the same). So no face
has length 3, and every face has length at least 4.

**Theorem.** A connected bipartite planar graph with v at least 3 vertices
has e at most 2v − 4.

**Proof.** The same double count: total face length is exactly 2e and at
least 4f, so 4f is at most 2e. With f = e − v + 2, that gives
4(e − v + 2) at most 2e, so e at most 2v − 4.

**Corollary: K3,3 is not planar.** It is connected and bipartite with 6
vertices and 9 edges, and 9 is more than 2 · 6 − 4 = 8.

#### Generalising: girth

The **girth** of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle. If every
face has length at least g (true when the girth is g and the graph is not
a tree), the double count gives g · f at most 2e, and then e at most
g(v − 2)/(g − 2). For g = 3 that is 3v − 6, for g = 4 it is 2v − 4, and
for g = 5 it is 5(v − 2)/3, which is enough to rule out the Petersen
graph (10 vertices, 15 edges, girth 5: the bound is 13⅓).

### Worked example

**Simple: the counting tests, and where they go quiet.**

cheap and decisive when they fire, and silent on many non-planar graphs.

**Realistic: a brute-force planarity test that always answers.** Every
embedding of a connected graph is determined by a **rotation system**: at
each vertex, the cyclic order of its neighbours (which way round the
edges leave it). Faces can be traced from a rotation: arrive at v from u,
leave along the next neighbour after u in v's order, and repeat until the
walk closes. Each rotation system describes an embedding on some surface,
and it is planar exactly when the traced faces satisfy Euler's formula,
f = 2 − v + e. For small graphs, try them all:

```ts

  for (const [u, ns] of g) for (const v of ns) {
    if (seen.has(`${u},${v}`)) continue; f++;

      seen.add(`${a},${b}`);
      const r = rot.get(b)!; const c = r[(r.indexOf(a) + 1) % r.length];
      a = b; b = c;
    }
  }
  return f;
}
```

- **Surfaces with handles:** graphs that do not fit on the plane may fit on a torus, which matters for some chip and network layouts that wrap around.

## 12.6 Coloring Planar Graphs

### In one sentence

Every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5, and with that fact,
plus the facts that deleting vertices and merging adjacent vertices keep
a graph planar, strong induction shows that every planar graph can be
coloured with five colours.

### The idea

The famous **four colour theorem** says every planar graph is
4-colourable. Its proof is far beyond this chapter. But five colours are
within reach, with three lemmas.

- **Lemma 1. Any subgraph of a planar graph is planar.** Erase edges and vertices from a crossing-free drawing and it stays crossing-free. (With the recursive definition, this takes a structural induction.)
- **Lemma 2. Merging two adjacent vertices of a planar graph leaves a planar graph.** To **merge** adjacent n1 and n2, delete both and add a new vertex m joined to every vertex that was adjacent to either. In a drawing, shrink the edge n1—n2 to a point.
- **Lemma 3. Every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5.** If every degree were at least 6, the degree sum would be at least 6v. By the handshaking lemma it equals 2e, so e would be at least 3v. But e is at most 3v − 6, less than 3v. (Graphs with fewer than 3 vertices trivially have small degrees.)

**A warm-up: six colours are easy.** Induct on v. Remove a vertex g of
degree at most 5; the rest is planar (Lemma 1) and 6-colourable by
hypothesis; g has at most 5 neighbours, so one of 6 colours is free for
it. This is the max-degree argument from chapter 11, applied one vertex
at a time.

**Theorem: every planar graph is five-colourable.** Strong induction on
the number of vertices v.

- **Base cases, v at most 5:** give every vertex its own colour.
- **Inductive step:** let G be planar with v + 1 vertices, and choose a vertex g of degree at most 5 (Lemma 3).
- **Case 1, degree of g below 5.** Delete g. The rest is planar (Lemma 1) with v vertices, so it is 5-colourable. g has at most 4 neighbours, so a fifth colour is free for it.
- **Case 2, degree of g exactly 5.** If all five neighbours were adjacent to each other, they would form a K5 inside G, which is impossible by Lemma 1 and the non-planarity of K5. So two neighbours, n1 and n2, are **not** adjacent. Merge n1 into g, giving a vertex m, which is adjacent to n2 (since g was). Merge m with n2, giving m′. Two merges keep the graph planar (Lemma 2), and it now has v − 1 vertices, so it is 5-colourable by the strong hypothesis. Now un-merge: every vertex other than g, n1 and n2 keeps its colour; n1 and n2 both get m′'s colour. That is proper, because n1 and n2 are not adjacent, and every neighbour of either was a neighbour of m′ and so has a different colour. Finally, g's five neighbours use at most four colours between them (n1 and n2 share one), so a fifth colour is free for g.

The trick in Case 2 is to force two neighbours to share a colour, which
frees a colour for g. Kempe's 19th-century "proof" of four colours tried
a cleverer version of this recolouring and had a gap that survived for
eleven years.

### Worked example

**Simple: the book's induction as a recursive function.** Each call finds
a vertex of degree at most 5, recurses on a smaller planar graph, and
colours the removed vertices on the way back:

```ts
function five(g: G): Map<number, number> {
  if (g.size <= 5) { const c = new Map<number, number>(); [...g.keys()].forEach((v, i) => c.set(v, i)); return c; }
  const v = [...g.keys()].find((x) => g.get(x)!.size <= 5)!;      // exists by Lemma 3
  const nb = [...g.get(v)!];
  const copy = (): G => new Map([...g].map(([k, s]) => [k, new Set(s)]));
  if (nb.length < 5) {                                              // Case 1: delete v
    const h = copy(); for (const u of nb) h.get(u)!.delete(v); h.delete(v);
    const c = five(h); const used = new Set(nb.map((u) => c.get(u)));
    let k = 0; while (used.has(k)) k++; c.set(v, k); return c;
  }
  let n1 = -1, n2 = -1;                                             // Case 2: two nonadjacent neighbours
  for (const a of nb) for (const b of nb) if (a < b && !g.get(a)!.has(b) && n1 < 0) { n1 = a; n2 = b; }
  const h = copy();                                                 // merge n1, v, n2 into one vertex named n1
  const merged = new Set([...g.get(n1)!, ...g.get(v)!, ...g.get(n2)!]); merged.delete(n1); merged.delete(n2); merged.delete(v);
  for (const x of [v, n2]) { for (const u of g.get(x)!) h.get(u)?.delete(x); h.delete(x); }
  h.set(n1, merged); for (const u of merged) h.get(u)!.add(n1);
  const c = five(h); c.set(n2, c.get(n1)!);                         // n1 and n2 share the merged colour
  const used = new Set(nb.map((u) => c.get(u)));
  let k = 0; while (used.has(k)) k++; c.set(v, k); return c;
}
```

- **Register allocation and scheduling** reuse the smallest-last (degeneracy) ordering, which is the six-colour proof turned into an algorithm.
- **Machine-checked proofs:** the four colour theorem was one of the first major results whose acceptance rested on computer verification, a precedent for verified software today.

## 12.7 Classifying Polyhedra

### In one sentence

Applying Euler's formula to the graph of a polyhedron, together with
two handshake-style counts, shows that only five regular polyhedra are
possible.

### The idea

A **polyhedron** here is a convex, three-dimensional solid bounded by
finitely many flat polygonal faces. It is **regular** if all faces are the
same regular polygon and the same number of faces meet at every corner.
The tetrahedron, cube and octahedron are examples.

**From solid to planar graph.** Put a light inside a polyhedron and
project its edges onto a surrounding sphere: that is a crossing-free
drawing on the sphere, and drawings on a sphere are the same as drawings
in the plane (12.2.4). So corners, edges and faces of the solid satisfy
v − e + f = 2.

Let **m** be the number of faces meeting at each corner and **n** the
number of edges on each face. Then:

- Each corner has m edges, and each edge has two ends: **mv = 2e**.
- Each face has n edges, and each edge borders two faces: **nf = 2e**.

Substitute v = 2e/m and f = 2e/n into Euler's formula and divide by 2e:

**1/m + 1/n = 1/e + 1/2.**

The right side is more than 1/2. A polygon has at least 3 sides, so n is
at least 3, and at least 3 faces meet at a corner, so m is at least 3. If
either were 6 or more, the left side would be at most 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2,
which is too small. That leaves finitely many cases to check, and exactly
five work.

### Worked example

**Simple: solving the equation.**

```ts

  const e = Math.round(1 / lhs), v = (2 * e) / m, f = (2 * e) / n;
```

**Realistic: why every football-style ball has exactly 12 pentagons.**
Suppose a solid has only pentagons and hexagons, with three faces at each
corner. Let p and h be their counts. Then 2e = 5p + 6h and 3v = 2e. Put
these into Euler: v − e + f = 2e/3 − e + p + h = −(5p + 6h)/6 + p + h =
p/6, so **p = 12, whatever h is**. The hexagons cancel out completely:

```text
12 pentagons + 20 hexagons, 3 per corner: v=60 e=90 f=32 v-e+f=2
  f6=0   f5=12: v=20 e=30 f=12 v-e+f=2
  f6=1   f5=12: v=22 e=33 f=13 v-e+f=2
  f6=2   f5=12: v=24 e=36 f=14 v-e+f=2
  f6=5   f5=12: v=30 e=45 f=17 v-e+f=2
  f6=20  f5=12: v=60 e=90 f=32 v-e+f=2
  f6=110 f5=12: v=240 e=360 f=122 v-e+f=2
```

The row with 20 hexagons is the classic stitched football and the shape of
the carbon-60 molecule. Euler's formula fixes the pentagons; it does not
promise that every number of hexagons is achievable (a closed cage with
exactly one hexagon, for example, cannot be built), which is the same
"necessary, not sufficient" lesson as the edge bound.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** using v − e + f = 2 for any polyhedron-like object, such
as a picture frame or a mesh with a hole through it.

**Why it fails:** the formula is for surfaces like a sphere. A solid with
a tunnel through it is like a torus, where v − e + f = 0.

**The right way:** check that the surface is sphere-like (convex solids
always are). In mesh software, a value of 2 − 2g reveals g handles.

### In real systems

- **3D graphics and CAD:** mesh validators check Euler characteristic to catch holes and non-manifold geometry.
- **Geodesic domes and sensor or satellite layouts** start from the icosahedron and subdivide it, because it is the most uniform regular solid; no regular solid has more than 20 faces, so finer uniform layouts use near-regular subdivisions.
- **Chemistry:** carbon cages made of pentagons and hexagons always have exactly 12 pentagons, by the calculation above.

## 12.8 Another Characterization for Planar Graphs

### In one sentence

A graph is non-planar exactly when K5 or K3,3 can be obtained from it by
deleting vertices, deleting edges and merging adjacent vertices, so the
two opening puzzles are the only obstacles.

### The idea

A **minor** of G is any graph obtained from G by repeatedly deleting
vertices, deleting edges, and merging adjacent vertices.

**Theorem (Kuratowski).** A graph is not planar if and only if it has K5
or K3,3 as a minor.

One direction is what this chapter has proved: deleting and merging keep
a planar graph planar (Lemmas 1 and 2 of 12.6), so a planar graph cannot
have a non-planar minor, and K5 and K3,3 are non-planar (12.5). The other
direction, that every non-planar graph contains one of them, is the deep
part. The book does not prove it, because the known proofs are too long
for an introductory text.

A small example of minors: the triangle C3 is a minor of a connected graph
exactly when the graph is not a tree. If there is a cycle, delete
everything off it and merge along it until three vertices remain; a tree
has no cycle, and merging and deleting cannot create one.

**A note on names.** Kuratowski's original 1930 theorem is about
**subdivisions** (copies of K5 or K3,3 with edges replaced by paths); the
equivalent statement with minors is usually credited to Wagner. The two
are equivalent for planarity, and the minor version is the one that
generalises: the Robertson–Seymour theorem says every property closed
under taking minors is characterised by a finite list of forbidden minors.

### Worked example

**Simple: the Petersen graph has a K5 minor.** It has an outer 5-cycle,
an inner pentagram and five spokes joining them. Merge the two ends of
each spoke:

```ts
function merge(g: G, a: string, b: string, name: string): G {
  if (!g.get(a)!.has(b)) throw new Error('only adjacent vertices may be merged');
  const h: G = new Map([...g].filter(([k]) => k !== a && k !== b)
    .map(([k, s]) => [k, new Set([...s].map((x) => (x === a || x === b ? name : x)))]));
  h.set(name, new Set([...g.get(a)!, ...g.get(b)!].filter((x) => x !== a && x !== b)));
  return h;
}
const pet: string[] = [];
for (let i = 0; i < 5; i++) pet.push(`o${i}-o${(i + 1) % 5}`, `o${i}-i${i}`, `i${i}-i${(i + 2) % 5}`);
let g = mk(pet);
for (let i = 0; i < 5; i++) g = merge(g, `o${i}`, `i${i}`, `m${i}`);
```

```text
Petersen: 10 vertices, 15 edges
after merging each spoke o_k—i_k: 5 vertices, 10 edges, complete? true
m0:m1,m2,m3,m4  m1:m0,m2,m3,m4  m2:m0,m1,m3,m4  m3:m0,m1,m2,m4  m4:m0,m1,m2,m3
```

Each merged vertex m_k inherits outer neighbours k ± 1 and inner
neighbours k ± 2, which together are all four others: K5. By Kuratowski,
that is a certificate of non-planarity, agreeing with the girth bound and
the rotation search of 12.5.

**Realistic: finding a triangle minor in a graph with a cycle.**

```text
5-cycle with a tail, after deleting f and merging a,b,c: d:abc,e  e:abc,d  abc:d,e
```

A 5-cycle a, b, c, d, e with a pendant vertex f: delete f, merge a with
b, then with c, and a triangle is left. This is the "not a tree" direction
of the C3 example, done by hand in code.

**Why it fails:** the Petersen graph is non-planar but has no vertex of
degree 4, so it has no K5 subgraph, and it has no K3,3 subgraph either.
The obstacles appear only after merging (or as subdivisions).

**The right way:** look for minors or subdivisions. In code, planarity
testers that return a witness return a Kuratowski subdivision.

### In real systems

- **Planarity testing libraries** return either an embedding or a Kuratowski subgraph as a certificate, so the answer can be checked independently.
- **Graph minor theory** underlies a family of algorithms for graphs that exclude a fixed minor (such as planar graphs), which often admit faster algorithms than general graphs.
- **Certifying algorithms:** returning a witness for both yes and no answers, as planarity allows, is a pattern worth copying in any decision procedure.

## Questions people ask

**Why not just define planar graphs by drawings?** That definition is
fine, but proving things from it needs topology (for example, that a
closed curve splits the plane into an inside and an outside). The
recursive embedding definition gives the same class of graphs and lets
every proof be discrete and checkable.

**Is the four colour theorem really proved if a computer did part of it?**
The mathematical community accepts it. The case analysis has been
re-checked independently and the whole proof has been verified in a
proof assistant. What it lacks is a short human-readable proof.

**Can a planar graph always be drawn with straight edges?** Yes. Fáry's
theorem says every planar graph has a crossing-free drawing in which
every edge is a straight segment, which matters for layout software.

**Does the number of faces depend on how I draw the graph?** No, for a
connected graph it is always e − v + 2. The shapes and sizes of the faces
can change between drawings, but not their number.

**How do real tools test planarity?** With linear-time algorithms that
build an embedding incrementally and either finish or return a Kuratowski
subdivision. Brute force over rotation systems, as on this page, is only
for small graphs and for understanding.

**What is the crossing number of a graph?** The fewest crossings in any
drawing. It is 1 for both K5 and K3,3, and computing it exactly is hard in
general. Planar graphs are exactly the graphs with crossing number 0.

## Key takeaways

- A planar drawing has no crossings; a planar embedding is a set of closed walks (faces) built recursively by splitting a face or adding a bridge.
- Faces can repeat edges: bridges and dongles appear twice in one face; every other edge appears once in each of two faces.
- Euler's formula v − e + f = 2 holds for every planar embedding of a connected graph, by structural induction.
- The double count of face lengths gives e at most 3v − 6 (v at least 3), and e at most 2v − 4 for bipartite graphs; generally g(v − 2)/(g − 2) for girth g.
- K5 and K3,3 are not planar; removing any one edge from either makes it planar.
- Every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5, so it is 6-colourable easily and 5-colourable with the merge trick; 4 colours suffice, by a computer-assisted proof.
- The only regular polyhedra are the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron.
- Kuratowski: a graph is non-planar exactly when it has a K5 or K3,3 minor.

## Glossary

- **Drawing:** points for vertices and curves for edges in the plane.
- **Planar drawing:** a drawing with no crossings; **planar graph:** one that has a planar drawing.
- **Continuous face:** a connected region of the plane left by a planar drawing; the **outer face** is the unbounded one.
- **Discrete face:** the closed walk around a face's boundary.
- **Planar embedding:** a set of closed walks built from one vertex by split-a-face and add-a-bridge.
- **Bridge:** a cut edge; in an embedding, an edge that occurs twice in one face.
- **Dongle:** a tree of bridges hanging into a face.
- **Euler's formula:** v − e + f = 2 for a connected planar embedding.
- **Triangulation (maximal planar graph):** a planar graph whose faces are all triangles; it has exactly 3v − 6 edges.
- **Girth:** the length of the shortest cycle.
- **Merging adjacent vertices (contraction):** replacing two adjacent vertices by one joined to all their other neighbours.
- **Minor:** a graph obtained by deleting vertices, deleting edges and merging adjacent vertices.
- **Subdivision:** a graph obtained by replacing edges with paths.
- **Rotation system:** a cyclic order of neighbours at every vertex; it determines the faces.
- **Regular polyhedron:** a convex solid whose faces are identical regular polygons, with the same number meeting at every corner.
- **Crossing number:** the fewest crossings in any drawing of a graph.
