English is too loose for proofs: "or" sometimes means "one or the other but
not both", and "if" sometimes means "if and only if". This chapter replaces
English connectives with exact ones, gives you a mechanical way (the truth
table) to decide what a formula means, and shows the algebra for rewriting
one formula into another. It ends at the SAT problem, the question at the
centre of computational complexity, and at quantified formulas, the
language every later chapter states its theorems in.

**After this chapter you will be able to:**

- Tell valid, satisfiable and unsatisfiable formulas apart, and convert between the questions.
- Put any formula into disjunctive or conjunctive normal form, and prove equivalences with algebraic rules.
- Explain what SAT is, why a fast general algorithm would matter so much, and how practical solvers search.
- Read and write predicate formulas with mixed quantifiers, and negate them correctly.

## 3.1 Propositions from Propositions

### In one sentence

Connectives build compound propositions from simpler ones, and each
connective is defined exactly by a truth table.

### The idea

Start with **propositional variables**: letters such as P and Q that each
stand for a proposition, true (T) or false (F). Connectives combine them.
The meaning of a connective is nothing more than its **truth table**: the
output for each combination of inputs. There is no hidden nuance, which is
the whole point.

#### NOT, AND, and OR

**NOT P** (written ¬P) is true exactly when P is false. **P AND Q** (P ∧ Q)
is true exactly when both are true. **P OR Q** (P ∨ Q) is true when at least
one is true, including when both are. That last part differs from casual
English, where "soup or salad" means one but not both. The mathematical OR
is **inclusive**.

The one-but-not-both version is its own connective, **exclusive or**,
written P XOR Q (or P ⊕ Q). It is true when exactly one of the two is true.
In code, XOR on booleans is `a !== b`.

#### IMPLIES

**P IMPLIES Q** (P → Q) is false only when P is true and Q is false. Chapter
1 explained why: it is a promise, and a promise is only broken when its
condition happens and its result does not. P is the **hypothesis** and Q the
**conclusion**. P → Q is equivalent to (¬P) ∨ Q, which is how you compute it.

The false-hypothesis rows cause endless confusion in English, where "if"
suggests a causal link. Mathematical implication has no causation. "If the
moon is cheese then 2 + 2 = 4" is true (the conclusion is true), and so is
"if 2 + 2 = 5 then the moon is cheese" (the hypothesis is false).

#### If and Only If

**P IFF Q** (P ↔ Q) is true when P and Q have the same truth value. It is
the negation of XOR. In code it is `a === b` on booleans.

### Worked example

**Simple: evaluate a compound.** Let P be "the build passed" (T) and Q be
"the tests passed" (F). Then P ∧ Q is F, P ∨ Q is T, P → Q is F (the build
passed and the tests did not: the promise "if it builds, the tests pass" is
broken), and ¬Q → ¬P is also F, as the contrapositive should be.

**Realistic: a deploy gate.** A team writes the rule "deploy if the change
is approved or it is a hotfix, but not if the freeze is on". As a formula:
(Approved ∨ Hotfix) ∧ ¬Freeze. Writing it this way surfaces the question the
English hides: does a hotfix override the freeze? In the formula as written,
no. If the team meant yes, the formula is Hotfix ∨ (Approved ∧ ¬Freeze),
which differs on the row Hotfix = T, Freeze = T. The truth table is how the
team settles which one they meant.

```ts
const gateA = (ap: boolean, hf: boolean, fz: boolean) => (ap || hf) && !fz;
const gateB = (ap: boolean, hf: boolean, fz: boolean) => hf || (ap && !fz);
for (const ap of [true, false]) for (const hf of [true, false]) for (const fz of [true, false])
  if (gateA(ap, hf, fz) !== gateB(ap, hf, fz)) console.log({ ap, hf, fz });
```

```text
{ ap: true, hf: true, fz: true }
{ ap: false, hf: true, fz: true }
```

The two readings disagree exactly when a hotfix arrives during a freeze,
which is the case the English left open.

### Step by step

Computing (P → Q) ∧ (Q → P) column by column, which should reproduce IFF.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** reading OR as exclusive. Someone proves "x is at least 0
or x is at most 0" is false at x = 0 because "it is both". Under inclusive
OR, being both makes the OR true, so the statement holds for every real x.

**The fix:** in mathematics and code, OR is inclusive unless it says XOR.
When an English requirement says "or", ask whether "both" is allowed, and
write XOR if it is not.

### In real systems

Feature flags, permission rules and alerting conditions are all compound
propositions. Bugs come from the same places as in English: unclear OR,
implied IFF, and forgotten false-hypothesis rows. Policy languages such as
AWS IAM, Open Policy Agent's Rego and Google's Zanzibar-style permission
systems make these connectives explicit for exactly that reason, and most
of them have a "why was this allowed?" tool that is a truth-table
evaluation of one row.

## 3.2 Propositional Logic in Computer Programs

### In one sentence

Every `if` condition is a propositional formula, and truth tables let you
replace a complicated condition with a simpler equivalent one safely.

### The idea

Program conditions combine simple tests with `&&`, `||` and `!`. Each
simple test, such as `user.isAdmin` or `retries > 3`, plays the role of a
propositional variable. The whole condition is then a formula, and two
conditions are interchangeable exactly when their formulas are
**equivalent**: they have the same truth value for every assignment of their
variables.

Conditions grow by accretion: someone adds a special case, then a guard, and
the condition ends up saying something simple in a complicated way.
Rewriting it is risky without a check. The truth table is that check: it
lists every combination of the simple tests and compares the two
conditions on each.

There is one catch for real code: the simple tests are not always
independent. `x > 0` and `x <= 0` cannot both be true. A truth table treats
them as two separate variables and includes the impossible rows; that is
safe (it only demands agreement on extra rows) but it can stop you from
spotting a simplification that relies on the link. The book's approach is to
name one variable A for `x > 0` and write `x <= 0` as ¬A.

#### Truth table calculation

Here is a condition of the kind that accumulates in real code:
`user.isAdmin || (!user.isAdmin && user.ownsDoc)`. Let A be "is admin" and B
be "owns the document". The condition is A ∨ (¬A ∧ B). Is it the same as A ∨
B?

<Trace columns={["A", "B", "¬A", "¬A ∧ B", "A ∨ (¬A ∧ B)", "A ∨ B"]} rows={[
  ["T", "T", "F", "F", "T", "T"],
  ["T", "F", "F", "F", "T", "T"],
  ["F", "T", "T", "T", "T", "T"],
  ["F", "F", "T", "F", "F", "F"],
]} caption="Compute the inner columns first. The two final columns agree on all four rows, so the conditions are equivalent and the simpler one can replace the longer one." />

The same result by cases: if A is true, both sides are "true OR something",
which is true. If A is false, ¬A is true, so ¬A ∧ B is just B, and both
sides reduce to B. Two cases, both agree, done.

#### Cryptic notation

Different communities write the same connectives differently. Logicians use
¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔ and ⊕. Programmers use `!`, `&&`, `||` and `!==`. Hardware
people write NOT P as P with a bar over it, AND as multiplication (PQ) and
OR as addition (P + Q). The book mostly writes the connectives as words in
capitals (AND, OR, NOT, IMPLIES, IFF) to avoid confusion while you learn.

### Worked example

**Simple:** the book-style check, automated. A tiny evaluator over a formula
tree lets you test any two formulas for equivalence:

```ts
type F = { t: 'var'; name: string } | { t: 'not'; a: F }
       | { t: 'and' | 'or' | 'imp'; a: F; b: F };
type Env = Record<string, boolean>;
const ev = (f: F, e: Env): boolean =>
  f.t === 'var' ? e[f.name] : f.t === 'not' ? !ev(f.a, e)
  : f.t === 'and' ? ev(f.a, e) && ev(f.b, e)
  : f.t === 'or' ? ev(f.a, e) || ev(f.b, e) : !ev(f.a, e) || ev(f.b, e);
function* envs(names: string[]): Generator<Env> {
  for (let m = 0; m < 1 << names.length; m++)
    yield Object.fromEntries(names.map((n, i) => [n, ((m >> i) & 1) === 1]));
}
const equivalent = (f: F, g: F, names: string[]) =>
  [...envs(names)].every((e) => ev(f, e) === ev(g, e));
```

With helpers `V`, `not`, `and`, `or`, `imp` that build nodes, the checks
read like the math:

```ts
console.log(equivalent(or(A, and(not(A), B)), or(A, B), ['A', 'B']));    // the simplification
console.log(equivalent(not(and(A, B)), or(not(A), not(B)), ['A', 'B'])); // De Morgan
console.log(equivalent(imp(A, B), imp(B, A), ['A', 'B']));               // converse
```

```text
true
true
false
```

**Realistic: simplifying a retry condition.** A service retries when
`(status >= 500 || timedOut) && !(status >= 500 && attempts > 3) && !(timedOut && attempts > 3)`.
Name S for a server error, T for a timeout, M for "more than 3 attempts".
The condition is (S ∨ T) ∧ ¬(S ∧ M) ∧ ¬(T ∧ M). Distribute and simplify
(section 3.4 has the rules) and it becomes (S ∨ T) ∧ ¬M: retry on a server
error or timeout, unless the attempts are used up. The truth table over 8
rows confirms it, and the code reviewer now reads one idea instead of three.

### Step by step

Proving A ∨ (¬A ∧ B) ≡ A ∨ B algebraically, the way 3.4 will formalise.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** simplifying a condition with side effects or
short-circuit dependence. `user && user.isAdmin` cannot be reordered to
`user.isAdmin && user`, because when `user` is null the first is safe and
the second throws. Propositional equivalence assumes every variable has a
value; code may not.

**The fix:** before applying logic rewrites to code, check that every test
is pure and always defined. Guards that protect later tests (null checks,
bounds checks) must stay first.

### In real systems

Compilers apply exactly these rewrites when they optimise boolean
expressions, but only when they can prove the operands are side-effect free.
Linters such as ESLint's `no-constant-condition` and TypeScript's
"this condition will always return true" warnings are truth-table checks on
small formulas. In hardware, minimising a formula means fewer logic gates on
a chip, which is what synthesis tools spend most of their time on.

### Check yourself

1. Is `!(a && b)` the same as `!a || !b` in TypeScript, for booleans a and b?
2. Why can a truth table with impossible rows still be a valid equivalence check?

1. Yes. It is De Morgan's law, and for pure boolean operands it holds on all four rows.
2. Because it demands agreement on more rows than necessary. If two conditions agree on every row, they agree on the possible ones. It can only make you miss a simplification, never accept a wrong one.

## 3.3 Equivalence and Validity

### In one sentence

Two formulas are equivalent when they agree on every assignment; a formula
is valid when it is true on every assignment and satisfiable when it is
true on at least one.

### The idea

An **assignment** (also called an environment) gives each variable a truth
value. With n variables there are 2ⁿ assignments, one per row of the truth
table. Three questions about formulas come up again and again, and each is
a statement about those rows.

**Equivalence.** F and G are equivalent, written F ≡ G, if they have the
same value on every row. **Validity.** F is **valid** (a **tautology**) if it
is true on every row: it is true no matter what. **Satisfiability.** F is
**satisfiable** if it is true on at least one row. A formula true on no row
is **unsatisfiable** (a contradiction).

The three questions reduce to one another. F ≡ G exactly when F ↔ G is
valid. F is valid exactly when ¬F is unsatisfiable. So a single tool, a
procedure for satisfiability, answers all three: to check validity of F,
ask whether ¬F is satisfiable and flip the answer.

#### Implications and contrapositives

The truth-table view settles chapter 1's claims mechanically. P → Q is
equivalent to its **contrapositive** ¬Q → ¬P: both are false only on the
row P = T, Q = F. The **converse** Q → P is false on a different row (P = F,
Q = T), so it is not equivalent. The **inverse** ¬P → ¬Q is equivalent to
the converse. That table is the reason the contrapositive is a legal proof
technique and the converse is not.

  ["F", "T", "T", "T", "F", "F"],

#### Validity and satisfiability

Valid formulas are the laws of logic. P ∨ ¬P (the excluded middle) is valid.
So is ((P → Q) ∧ (Q → R)) → (P → R), the transitivity used in every chained
proof. Satisfiable formulas are the ones that *can* be made true, and the
satisfying assignment is often the thing you actually want: a schedule, a
configuration, a test input.

```ts
const valid = (f: F, names: string[]) => [...envs(names)].every((e) => ev(f, e));
const satisfiable = (f: F, names: string[]) => [...envs(names)].find((e) => ev(f, e)) ?? null;
console.log(valid(or(A, not(A)), ['A']));
console.log(valid(imp(and(imp(A, B), imp(B, C)), imp(A, C)), ['A', 'B', 'C']));
```

```text
true
true
```

### Worked example

**Simple:** Is (P → Q) ∨ (Q → P) valid? Check the rows: it can only be false
if both implications are false, which needs P = T, Q = F and Q = T, P = F at
once. Impossible, so it is valid. This surprises people: for any two
statements, one implies the other.

**Realistic: a configuration that cannot be satisfied.** A product has three
toggles with rules: "dark mode needs the new renderer", "the new renderer
conflicts with the legacy plugin", and "the legacy plugin is required when
enterprise mode is on". Can a user have dark mode with enterprise mode? As a
formula: (D → N) ∧ (N → ¬L) ∧ (E → L) ∧ D ∧ E. From D, N; from N, ¬L; from
E, L. L and ¬L: unsatisfiable. A satisfiability check over the 16 rows finds
no satisfying assignment, and support now knows that combination is
impossible by design rather than a bug.

Turning "is F equivalent to G?" into one satisfiability question.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** confusing "satisfiable" with "valid". A formula that is
true in your test case is satisfiable; it is not therefore always true. "The
condition evaluated to true in the demo" says nothing about other inputs.

**The fix:** to claim validity, check every row or give a proof. To show a
formula is *not* valid, one falsifying row is enough, and that row is a
ready-made failing test.

### In real systems

Equivalence checking is a production tool. Chip designers check that an
optimised circuit is equivalent to the reference by asking a SAT solver for
an input on which they differ. Compiler teams use translation validation
the same way. And "is this firewall rule set equivalent to the old one?" is
answered by tools like AWS's Zelkova, which turns policies into formulas and
asks an SMT solver whether any request is treated differently.

## 3.4 The Algebra of Propositions

### In one sentence

Every formula can be rewritten into a standard normal form, and equivalences
can be proved by algebra with a small set of rules instead of truth tables.

### The idea

Truth tables always work, but they double in size with every variable: 30
variables means over a billion rows. Algebra scales better. You rewrite a
formula step by step, each step replacing a piece by an equivalent piece,
until it becomes the formula you want. Since every step preserves
equivalence, the start and end are equivalent.

#### Propositions in normal form

A **literal** is a variable or its negation (P or ¬P). An **AND-of-literals**
such as P ∧ ¬Q ∧ R describes one row of a truth table exactly. A formula is
in **disjunctive normal form** (DNF) if it is an OR of AND-of-literals
terms. It is in **conjunctive normal form** (CNF) if it is an AND of
OR-of-literals clauses.

Every formula has a DNF, and you can read it off the truth table: for each
row where the formula is true, write the AND-term that describes that row,
then OR the terms together. The result is true exactly on those rows. A
**full** DNF uses every variable in every term. CNF works the same way with
the false rows: for each false row, write a clause that rules out exactly
that row, and AND the clauses.

Here is DNF from a truth table in code, for the "majority of three" function
(true when at least two of A, B, C are true):

```ts
const names = ['A', 'B', 'C'];
const majority = (e: Record<string, boolean>) => [e.A, e.B, e.C].filter(Boolean).length >= 2;
const terms: string[] = [];
for (let m = 0; m < 8; m++) {
  const e = Object.fromEntries(names.map((n, i) => [n, ((m >> (2 - i)) & 1) === 1]));
  if (majority(e)) terms.push('(' + names.map((n) => (e[n] ? n : `¬${n}`)).join(' ∧ ') + ')');
}
console.log(terms.join(' ∨ '));
```

```text
(¬A ∧ B ∧ C) ∨ (A ∧ ¬B ∧ C) ∨ (A ∧ B ∧ ¬C) ∨ (A ∧ B ∧ C)
```

This proves something big: **AND, OR and NOT are enough to express any
truth table**. A set of connectives with that property is called
**complete**. (NAND alone is complete too, which is why chips can be built
from one kind of gate.)

#### Proving equivalences

The algebraic rules mirror ordinary algebra, with a few twists. The ones
used most:

<Compare caption="The working set of equivalence rules. Each line is valid, so you can replace the left side with the right anywhere inside a formula." left="Rule" right="Equivalence" rows={[
  ["commutative", "A ∧ B ≡ B ∧ A,  A ∨ B ≡ B ∨ A"],
  ["associative", "(A ∧ B) ∧ C ≡ A ∧ (B ∧ C), same for ∨"],
  ["distributive", "A ∧ (B ∨ C) ≡ (A ∧ B) ∨ (A ∧ C), and A ∨ (B ∧ C) ≡ (A ∨ B) ∧ (A ∨ C)"],
  ["identity", "A ∧ T ≡ A,  A ∨ F ≡ A"],
  ["zero", "A ∧ F ≡ F,  A ∨ T ≡ T"],
  ["idempotence", "A ∧ A ≡ A,  A ∨ A ≡ A"],
  ["contradiction, validity", "A ∧ ¬A ≡ F,  A ∨ ¬A ≡ T"],
  ["double negation", "¬¬A ≡ A"],
  ["De Morgan", "¬(A ∧ B) ≡ ¬A ∨ ¬B,  ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ ¬A ∧ ¬B"],
  ["implication", "A → B ≡ ¬A ∨ B"],
]} />

The twist compared with numbers is that **OR distributes over AND** as well
as AND over OR. In arithmetic, a + (b × c) is not (a + b) × (a + c), but in
logic the matching law holds. That second distributive law is what turns DNF
into CNF.

With these rules, any formula can be driven to a normal form: eliminate →
and ↔ using their definitions, push ¬ inward with De Morgan until it sits
only on variables, then distribute to get DNF or CNF. Two formulas are
equivalent iff they have the same full DNF, which gives a complete
algebraic method for equivalence.

### Worked example

**Simple:** prove ¬(A ∨ (¬A ∧ B)) ≡ ¬A ∧ ¬B. Apply De Morgan to the outer
NOT: ¬A ∧ ¬(¬A ∧ B). De Morgan again inside: ¬A ∧ (¬¬A ∨ ¬B) = ¬A ∧ (A ∨
¬B). Distribute: (¬A ∧ A) ∨ (¬A ∧ ¬B). The first term is F, and F ∨ X is X,
so the result is ¬A ∧ ¬B.

**Realistic: a permission rule to CNF for a policy engine.** Some policy
engines and solvers take CNF. Rule: "allow if (owner and not suspended) or
admin". As a formula: (O ∧ ¬S) ∨ M. Distribute OR over AND: (O ∨ M) ∧ (¬S ∨
M). Read it as two separate checks a request must pass: "owner or admin",
and "not suspended, or admin". The CNF form also makes the policy's two
failure reasons visible, which is handy for an error message.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** pushing a negation in without flipping the connective.
¬(A ∧ B) written as ¬A ∧ ¬B. This says "neither A nor B", while the original
says "not both". They differ on the rows with exactly one true.

**The fix:** De Morgan always flips the connective: NOT over AND becomes OR
of NOTs, and NOT over OR becomes AND of NOTs. Say it out loud as "not both
means at least one is missing".

### In real systems

CNF is the input language of SAT solvers (the DIMACS file format is a list
of clauses). DNF appears in database query planning, where a `WHERE` clause
in DNF splits into independent index scans that are unioned. And the DNF
blow-up is real: converting some formulas to DNF or CNF naively produces
exponentially many terms. Solvers use the Tseitin transformation, which adds
helper variables to get a CNF of linear size that is satisfiable exactly
when the original is.

### Check yourself

2. Apply De Morgan to ¬(¬A ∨ B).

2. ¬¬A ∧ ¬B, which is A ∧ ¬B.

## 3.5 The SAT Problem

### In one sentence

SAT asks whether a formula has a satisfying assignment; nobody knows how to
answer it quickly for every formula, and a fast general method would change
computing.

### The idea

Checking a *given* assignment is easy: evaluate the formula, linear time.
Finding one is the hard part. The truth-table method tries all 2ⁿ rows, and
2ⁿ explodes: 60 variables means more rows than a fast computer can check in
centuries. The **SAT problem** is to decide, for any formula, whether some
assignment makes it true.

Nobody knows a method that runs in **polynomial time** (a number of steps
bounded by some fixed power of the formula's size, like n² or n¹⁴) for every
formula. Nobody has proved that no such method exists either. This is the
**P versus NP** question, the most famous open problem in computer science
and one of the Clay Millennium Problems.

Why so much rides on it: a huge number of problems (scheduling, routing,
circuit verification, many puzzles) translate into SAT. So a fast SAT
method would give fast methods for all of them. The book points ahead to
chapter 8, where it also explains why it would break much of today's
cryptography.

And yet, practical **SAT solvers** handle industrial formulas with millions
of variables. They do not beat the worst case; they exploit structure. The
core search is **DPLL**: pick a variable, try a value, simplify, and
propagate forced values (a clause with one remaining literal forces it);
on a dead end, backtrack. Modern solvers add clause learning (CDCL), which
remembers why a dead end happened so the search never repeats it.

### Worked example

**Simple: a formula in CNF.** Is (P ∨ Q ∨ R) ∧ (¬P ∨ ¬Q) ∧ (¬P ∨ ¬R) ∧ (¬Q ∨
¬R) satisfiable? It says "at least one of P, Q, R, and no two of them". So
exactly one is true: P = T, Q = F, R = F works. A small DPLL, with clauses
written as integers (1 is P, −1 is ¬P), finds it:

```ts
type Clause = number[];
function dpll(cs: Clause[], assign = new Map<number, boolean>()): Map<number, boolean> | null {
  cs = cs.filter((c) => !c.some((l) => assign.get(Math.abs(l)) === l > 0))   // drop satisfied clauses
         .map((c) => c.filter((l) => !assign.has(Math.abs(l))));             // drop false literals
  if (cs.length === 0) return assign;                 // every clause satisfied
  if (cs.some((c) => c.length === 0)) return null;    // a clause has no way left: dead end
  const unit = cs.find((c) => c.length === 1);        // a forced literal, if any
  const lit = unit ? unit[0] : cs[0][0];
  const go = (v: boolean) => dpll(cs, new Map(assign).set(Math.abs(lit), v));
  return unit ? go(lit > 0) : go(lit > 0) ?? go(lit < 0);
}
console.log(dpll([[1, 2, 3], [-1, -2], [-1, -3], [-2, -3]]));
console.log(dpll([[1, 2], [-1, 2], [1, -2], [-1, -2]]));
```

```text
Map(3) { 1 => true, 2 => false, 3 => false }
null
```

The second formula rules out all four rows of two variables, so it is
unsatisfiable, and the solver returns null after exploring both branches.

**Realistic: package versions.** A package manager must pick one version of
each package so that every dependency constraint holds. Encode "package X at
version v" as a variable, add clauses for "at most one version of each
package" and "if A@2 then B@3 or B@4". Dependency resolution is now SAT, and
in general it is NP-complete. That is why some resolvers are slow on
pathological graphs, and why tools such as the Dart and Rust ecosystems'
resolvers use solver-style algorithms (PubGrub) with conflict learning.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** "SAT is NP-complete, so a SAT solver is useless for my
problem." Worst-case hardness is about the hardest formulas, not the ones
real systems produce. Industrial solvers routinely handle formulas far
beyond any truth table.

**The fix:** try the solver. Encode the problem, run it with a timeout, and
treat a timeout as information about your encoding. The opposite mistake,
assuming any formula will solve quickly, is fixed the same way: measure.

### In real systems

SAT and its richer cousin SMT (satisfiability modulo theories, which adds
integers, arrays and bit-vectors) are workhorses. Z3 and similar solvers
power symbolic execution for finding bugs, verification tools such as Dafny,
AWS's policy checks, hardware verification at every chip maker, and test
input generation. When a type checker infers types for TypeScript's
conditional types or a scheduler packs containers onto machines, a solver
is often the engine.

### Check yourself

1. Why does the truth-table method fail for 100 variables?
2. What does a unit clause force?

1. It has 2¹⁰⁰ rows, about 10³⁰, far more than any computer can evaluate.
2. Its one remaining literal must be true, because every other literal in the clause is already false. So the solver sets that variable without guessing.

## 3.6 Predicate Formulas

### In one sentence

Predicate formulas add variables over a domain and the quantifiers ∀ and
∃, which lets you state claims about infinitely many objects precisely.

### The idea

Propositional logic cannot say "every integer has a successor", because
that is a claim about infinitely many things. Predicate logic can. Its
formulas contain **predicates** (P(x), x less than y), variables over a
**domain** (the integers, the users, the nodes of a graph), and two
**quantifiers**.

#### Quantifiers

**∀x. P(x)** ("for all x, P(x)") is true when P holds for every x in the
domain. **∃x. P(x)** ("there exists x such that P(x)") is true when P holds
for at least one. Over a finite domain, ∀ is a big AND and ∃ is a big OR:
over the domain 1, 2, 3, ∀x. P(x) is P(1) ∧ P(2) ∧ P(3). Over an infinite
domain no truth table is possible, which is why proofs are needed.

English hides quantifiers in many forms. "Every", "all", "any", "each" and
often plain nouns ("primes are greater than 1") mean ∀. "Some", "there is",
"at least one" and "a" in "a solution exists" mean ∃. "No" means ∀ ... ¬ (or
equivalently ¬∃). The word "any" is the treacherous one: "if any user is an
admin" means ∃, but "any user can log in" means ∀.

#### Mixing quantifiers

Real claims nest them. Goldbach's conjecture is "every even integer greater
than 2 is a sum of two primes": ∀n. (Even(n) ∧ n greater than 2) → ∃p. ∃q.
Prime(p) ∧ Prime(q) ∧ n = p + q. The pattern "for every input there is a
witness" (∀∃) is the shape of most specifications.

#### Order of quantifiers

Swapping a ∀ and an ∃ usually changes the meaning. "Every program has a
bug that crashes it" (∀p. ∃b. Crashes(b, p)) lets each program have its own
bug. "There is a bug that crashes every program" (∃b. ∀p. Crashes(b, p))
demands one universal bug. The second implies the first, never the reverse
in general.

Over a finite domain you can watch this happen:

```ts
const D = [1, 2, 3, 4];
console.log(D.every((x) => D.some((y) => y > x)));  // ∀x ∃y. y > x
console.log(D.some((y) => D.every((x) => y >= x))); // ∃y ∀x. y ≥ x
```

```text
false true
```

The first is false here (nothing in the domain is bigger than 4), though it
is true over all the integers. The second says there is a largest element,
true here and false over the integers. The domain is part of the meaning.

#### Variables over one domain

The book allows a simplification: when every variable ranges over the same
domain, you can leave the domain implicit and write restrictions as
predicates. "For every positive integer n" becomes ∀n. (n greater than 0) →
.... For "there exists", the restriction joins with AND, not with
implication: "some positive integer is even" is ∃n. (n greater than 0) ∧
Even(n). Using → there would make the claim trivially true via any
non-positive n.

#### Negating quantifiers

Negation flips each quantifier and moves inward: ¬∀x. P(x) ≡ ∃x. ¬P(x) and
¬∃x. P(x) ≡ ∀x. ¬P(x). For nested quantifiers, apply it repeatedly. This is
**De Morgan's law for quantifiers**, the infinite version of the
propositional one (∀ is a big AND, ∃ a big OR).

#### Validity for predicate formulas

A predicate formula is **valid** if it is true for every domain and every
interpretation of its predicates. ∀x. P(x) → ∃x. P(x) looks valid, and is,
provided domains are non-empty (the book's convention). ∃y. ∀x. R(x, y) →
∀x. ∃y. R(x, y) is valid: one y that works for everyone works for each.
Its converse is not valid, and a counterexample interpretation (the integers
with R as "less than") shows it.

Unlike propositional validity, predicate validity cannot be checked by any
finite table, and in general no algorithm can decide it (a result related
to the halting problem of chapter 7). Proofs are the tool.

### Worked example

**Simple: negate a definition.** "f is bounded" means ∃M. ∀x. |f(x)| is at
most M. Negate step by step: ¬∃M. ∀x. ... becomes ∀M. ¬∀x. ..., which becomes
∀M. ∃x. |f(x)| greater than M. In English: "for every proposed bound, some
input exceeds it". That is exactly the shape of a proof that f(x) = x² is
unbounded: given M, pick x = M + 1.

**Realistic: an SLO, written precisely.** "Every request gets a response
within 300 ms" is ∀r. Latency(r) at most 300. Real SLOs are not that. "In
every 5-minute window, at least 99% of requests are under 300 ms" is ∀w.
(fraction of r in w with Latency(r) at most 300) at least 0.99. Writing the
quantifiers out shows what the alert must compute (a per-window fraction,
for every window) and what its negation, the alert condition, is: ∃w.
fraction below 0.99.

   NOT  for all x  there is y :  P(x, y)
        |
        v   flip the outer quantifier, push NOT in
   there is x  NOT  there is y :  P(x, y)
        |
        v   flip the next one
   there is x  for all y :  NOT P(x, y)
`} caption="Negating a nested formula, one quantifier at a time. Each step flips one quantifier and moves the NOT one place to the right, until it lands on the predicate." />

### Step by step

Proving ∃y. ∀x. R(x, y) implies ∀x. ∃y. R(x, y), in words.

<Trace columns={["line", "statement", "why"]} rows={[

Why the converse fails: with the integers and R(x, y) meaning x less than y,
∀x. ∃y. x less than y is true (take y = x + 1), but ∃y. ∀x. x less than y is
false (no integer is above every integer, including itself).

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** using → with ∃ for a restricted domain. "Some admin has
2FA" written as ∃u. Admin(u) → Has2FA(u). This is true if there is any
non-admin user at all, because a false hypothesis makes the implication
true. The formula says almost nothing.

**The fix:** ∀ pairs with →, and ∃ pairs with ∧. "Every admin has 2FA" is
∀u. Admin(u) → Has2FA(u). "Some admin has 2FA" is ∃u. Admin(u) ∧ Has2FA(u).

### In real systems

SQL is predicate logic over table rows. `EXISTS` is ∃; "for all" is written
`NOT EXISTS (... WHERE NOT ...)`, the quantifier De Morgan law, because SQL
has no ∀. Specification languages such as TLA+ and Alloy write system
properties as nested quantifiers ("for every two nodes, if both think they
are leader, they are in different terms"). Alloy checks them by searching
small domains for counterexamples, which is the finite-domain trick above
turned into a tool.

2. Is ∀x. ∃y. y = x + 1 true over the natural numbers? What about ∀y. ∃x. y = x + 1?

2. The first is true (y = x + 1 is always a natural number). The second is false: for y = 0 there is no natural x with x + 1 = 0.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 3, Logical Formulas">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working idea: a formula's meaning is its truth table. To know
what (A ∨ B) ∧ ¬C means, list the 8 combinations of A, B, C and compute the
result on each. Nothing else is needed, and nothing else can disagree with
it.

Try it with "the alert fires if CPU is high or memory is high, unless
maintenance is on". Write H for high CPU, M for high memory, X for
maintenance: (H ∨ M) ∧ ¬X. The table has 8 rows and fires on 3 of them: H
only, M only, and both, each with X false.

The beginner's mistake is translating "unless" as "or". "Fire unless
maintenance" means fire when not maintenance, so it is ∧ ¬X. The fix is to
write the table for the English sentence first and check the formula
against it.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

In a real program, formulas are conditions, and the tools combine: a truth
table to check a rewrite, algebra to find it, and a code check that
operands are pure. A good workflow when you touch a hairy condition:

```ts
// Old and new conditions, as pure functions of the named tests.
const oldCond = (s: boolean, t: boolean, m: boolean) => (s || t) && !(s && m) && !(t && m);
const newCond = (s: boolean, t: boolean, m: boolean) => (s || t) && !m;
let diffs = 0;
for (let i = 0; i < 8; i++) {
  const [s, t, m] = [i & 4, i & 2, i & 1].map(Boolean);
  if (oldCond(s, t, m) !== newCond(s, t, m)) diffs++;
}
console.log(`rows where the rewrite changes behaviour: ${diffs}`);
```

```text
rows where the rewrite changes behaviour: 0
```

Put that loop in the test file next to the change. The intermediate mistake
is skipping it because the algebra "looked right". The fix costs eight
evaluations.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath are three precise facts. First, the set AND, OR, NOT is complete:
every truth table has a DNF. Second, equivalence, validity and
satisfiability reduce to each other, so one SAT procedure answers all three.
Third, SAT is NP-complete (the Cook–Levin theorem): every problem whose
solutions can be checked quickly reduces to it.

For predicate logic the situation is different in kind. Validity is
**semi-decidable**: if a formula is valid, a systematic proof search will
eventually find a proof, but if it is not, the search may run forever.
Gödel's completeness theorem says the proof rules capture every valid
formula; Church and Turing showed no algorithm decides validity.

The advanced mistake is applying propositional intuition to quantifiers:
there is no truth table for ∀x over the integers. The fix is to treat
quantified claims as things to prove, using the "arbitrary element" and
"name the witness" moves from section 3.6.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

**Zero variables.** A formula with no variables (T ∧ F) has a one-row truth
table. The empty AND is T and the empty OR is F, which is why `[].every`
returns `true` and `[].some` returns `false`.

**Empty domain.** The book assumes non-empty domains. If it were empty,
∀x. P(x) would be true and ∃x. P(x) false, so ∀x. P(x) → ∃x. P(x) would
fail. Code meets empty domains constantly: an empty list of permissions.

**Implication with a false hypothesis.** A spec "if the input is sorted, the
output is sorted" is satisfied by a function that returns garbage on
unsorted input. That is not a bug in the spec's logic; it is a gap in what
the spec asked for.

**An unsatisfiable spec.** A set of requirements can be contradictory, and
then every implementation fails some requirement. A satisfiability check on
the requirements finds this before anyone writes code.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

**Short-circuiting plus null.** `a !== null && a.ready` and `a.ready && a
!== null` are equivalent as formulas and not as code: with `a` null, the
second throws. Two things at once: an operand that can be undefined, and an
order-dependent evaluator.

**NaN plus negation.** In JavaScript `!(x < 5)` is not the same as `x >= 5`
when x is NaN: every comparison with NaN is false, so `!(NaN < 5)` is true
and `NaN >= 5` is false. Negating a comparison is only safe when the values
are totally ordered.

```ts
const x = NaN;
console.log(!(x < 5), x >= 5);
```

```text
true false
```

**SQL NULL plus NOT IN.** `WHERE id NOT IN (subquery)` returns no rows at
all if the subquery contains a NULL, because `id <> NULL` is unknown, not
true. A three-valued logic plus a negated quantifier gives a result that
surprises everyone once.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, logic shows up as authorisation rules, feature flags, alert
conditions and validation, and the failures are expensive: an over-broad
OR in a permission check is a security hole. Three practices help.

First, keep conditions as named, pure functions so they can be
truth-tabled in tests. Second, when rules come from configuration (policy
files, flag targeting), check new rule sets for equivalence or for specific
properties ("no rule allows anonymous writes") with a solver, the way cloud
providers check IAM policies. Third, log *which clause* fired, not just the
decision, so "why was this allowed?" has an answer.

The common production mistake is a flag condition that grew to a dozen
terms and nobody can say what it does. The fix is to regenerate it from a
table of intended behaviour, rewrite it in DNF or CNF, and give each term a
name and a test.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit this chapter builds is making the implicit explicit: every "or",
"unless", "any" and "if" in a requirement gets pinned to a connective or a
quantifier before it becomes code. It is the same instinct as asking for
the exact API contract before integrating.

The second habit is thinking in rows. When a condition is in doubt, ask
"which combination of inputs would make this go wrong?", which is looking
for a falsifying row. That question is a good debugging and reviewing
reflex well beyond logic.

The mindset mistake is trusting English. The fix: write the formula, then
read it back in English, and check that the two say the same thing to
someone else.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In a larger system, logic is often the configuration layer. Consider an
authorisation service like Google's Zanzibar or an Open Policy Agent
deployment. Rules are formulas; requests are assignments; a decision is an
evaluation. System design questions then become logic questions: can two
policies conflict (is their combination unsatisfiable on some request)?
Does a change grant new access (is new ∧ ¬old satisfiable)?

The second system is dependency resolution in a package manager or a build
tool: a SAT problem. Design consequences follow: keep constraints in a form
a solver can use, produce explanations from the solver's conflict (the
clauses that made it unsatisfiable), and set timeouts, since the worst case
is exponential.

The design mistake is building a custom rule engine with its own ad hoc
evaluation order. The fix is to keep rules declarative and order-free, so
they mean what their formula means and can be checked by tools.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

I learn this chapter by building the tools: a formula type, an evaluator, a
truth-table printer, an equivalence checker, a DNF generator and the tiny
DPLL solver above. Each one is under 30 lines, and each one forces me to
understand a definition exactly.

Then I practise translation in both directions: ten English requirements
into formulas, and ten formulas back into plain English, including at least
three with nested quantifiers and their negations.

**Done when** I can convert any formula of up to four variables to CNF by
hand and confirm it with my tool, negate a three-quantifier statement
correctly in one pass, explain why ∃ pairs with ∧ and ∀ with →, and say in
two sentences what SAT is and why it matters.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**Why is OR inclusive in logic when English often means exclusive?**
Inclusive OR has nicer algebra (De Morgan, distributivity) and matches what
programming languages do. When you mean exclusive, write XOR.

**Do I need to memorise all the equivalence rules?** Learn De Morgan, the
two distributive laws and A → B ≡ ¬A ∨ B by heart. The rest you can
re-derive from a truth table in a minute.

**Is DNF or CNF better?** Neither in general. DNF makes satisfiability easy
(any consistent term works) and validity hard; CNF makes validity of each
clause easy and satisfiability hard. Solvers use CNF.

**If SAT is so hard, how do SAT solvers work at all?** Real formulas have
structure: most variables are forced by propagation, and learned clauses
prune huge parts of the search. The exponential worst case still exists; it
is just rarely the case you have.

**What is the difference between SAT and SMT?** SMT adds theories: integers,
arrays, bit-vectors, strings. A formula like "x + y = 10 and x greater than
y" is SMT. Solvers such as Z3 combine a SAT engine with theory reasoning.

**Why can predicate validity not be checked mechanically?** Because domains
can be infinite, and there is no way to check infinitely many cases in
finite time. Proofs can establish validity, but no algorithm decides it for
every formula.

## Key takeaways

- A connective is defined completely by its truth table; OR is inclusive, and XOR is the exclusive one.
- Two formulas are equivalent when their truth tables match on every row.
- Valid means true on every row, satisfiable on at least one; F is valid exactly when ¬F is unsatisfiable.
- Every formula has a DNF and a CNF, so AND, OR and NOT can express any truth table.
- De Morgan flips the connective as the negation moves inward, for ∧ and ∨ and for ∀ and ∃.
- SAT asks for a satisfying assignment; no known algorithm is fast in the worst case, but solvers are fast in practice.
- Swapping ∀ and ∃ changes the meaning, and the domain is part of every quantified statement.
- Pair ∀ with → and ∃ with ∧ when restricting a variable.

## Glossary

- **Propositional variable:** a letter standing for a proposition.
- **Connective:** an operator building propositions from propositions (NOT, AND, OR, XOR, IMPLIES, IFF).
- **Truth table:** the list of a formula's values for every assignment.
- **Assignment (environment):** a choice of truth value for each variable.
- **Inclusive OR:** true when at least one side is true.
- **Exclusive OR (XOR, ⊕):** true when exactly one side is true.
- **Equivalent (≡):** having the same truth value on every assignment.
- **Valid (tautology):** true on every assignment.
- **Satisfiable:** true on at least one assignment.
- **Unsatisfiable (contradiction):** true on no assignment.
- **Literal:** a variable or its negation.
- **DNF:** an OR of ANDs of literals.
- **CNF:** an AND of ORs of literals (clauses).
- **Complete set of connectives:** one that can express every truth table.
- **De Morgan's laws:** negation flips AND to OR and OR to AND (and ∀ to ∃).
- **SAT:** the problem of deciding whether a formula is satisfiable.
- **Polynomial time:** a running time bounded by a fixed power of the input size.
- **P versus NP:** the open question of whether every quickly checkable problem is quickly solvable.
- **DPLL:** a backtracking SAT search with unit propagation.
- **Unit clause:** a clause with one unassigned literal left, which forces its value.
- **Quantifier:** ∀ ("for all") or ∃ ("there exists").
- **Domain of discourse:** the set the variables range over.

**Self-check.**

<details>
<summary>1. When is P → Q false?</summary>

Only when P is true and Q is false.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How do you check whether F is valid using only a satisfiability tool?</summary>

Ask whether ¬F is satisfiable. If it is not, F is valid; if it is, the satisfying assignment is a row where F is false.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What is De Morgan's law for ∀?</summary>

¬∀x. P(x) ≡ ∃x. ¬P(x): "not everything is P" means "something is not P".

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why does every formula have a DNF?</summary>

For each true row of its truth table, take the AND of literals describing that row; the OR of those terms is true exactly on the true rows.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What is wrong with ∃u. Admin(u) → Has2FA(u) as "some admin has 2FA"?</summary>

Any non-admin u makes the implication true, so the formula holds even if no admin has 2FA. Use ∃u. Admin(u) ∧ Has2FA(u).

</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Is ∀x. ∃y. R(x, y) the same as ∃y. ∀x. R(x, y)?</summary>

No. The second implies the first, but not the other way: over the integers with R as "less than", the first is true and the second is false.

</details>
