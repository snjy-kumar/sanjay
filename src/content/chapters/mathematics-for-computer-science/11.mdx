Chapter 9 was about arrows. This chapter drops the direction: an edge is
just a pair of vertices that are joined, the way a friendship, a shared
exam, a cable between two machines or a road between two towns is
symmetric. That one change produces a different and very rich theory.
The chapter moves quickly through a long list of ideas that every later
course reuses: counting degrees, deciding when two graphs are "the
same", matching people to things they want, finding a stable assignment
when both sides have preferences, colouring to avoid conflicts, deciding
connectivity, and building the cheapest network that connects
everything. Almost every idea comes with an algorithm you can run, so
this page runs them.

## 11.1 Vertex Adjacency and Degrees

### In one sentence

A simple graph is a nonempty set of vertices and a set of edges, each
edge being a set of exactly two distinct vertices; two vertices are
adjacent when they share an edge, an edge is incident to its two
endpoints, and a vertex's degree is the number of edges incident to it.

### The idea

#### What "simple" rules out

The book's definition makes an edge a two-element set of vertices,
written u—v. Three things follow immediately:

- **No direction:** u—v and v—u are the same edge, because they are the same set.
- **No self-loops:** a set containing v twice is just the one-element set containing v, which is not an edge.
- **No multiple edges:** the edge set is a set, so u—v is either in it or not.

Graphs allowing loops or repeated edges are called multigraphs; the
book sets them aside, and so does this page.

#### Adjacency, incidence, degree

Two vertices are **adjacent** if they are the endpoints of some edge. An
edge is **incident** to each of its endpoints. The **degree** of a
vertex, deg(v), is the number of edges incident to it, which in a simple
graph equals the number of vertices adjacent to it. A vertex of degree 0
is **isolated**.

<Figure art={`
        a ------- b             degrees:   a 2
        |        /|                        b 3
        |      /  |                        c 3
        |    /    |                        d 2
        c ------- d      e                 e 0 (isolated)

   edges: a-b  a-c  b-c  b-d  c-d       5 edges, degree sum 10

### Worked example

**Simple: degrees from an adjacency map.** Storing each undirected edge
in both endpoints' neighbour sets makes degree a set size:

```ts
type G = Map<string, Set<string>>;
const graph = (edges: string[]): G => {
  const g: G = new Map();
  for (const e of edges) {
    const [u, v] = e.split('-');
    for (const [a, b] of [[u, v], [v, u]]) { if (!g.has(a)) g.set(a, new Set()); g.get(a)!.add(b); }
  }
  return g;
};
const g = graph(['a-b', 'a-c', 'b-c', 'b-d', 'c-d']);
console.log([...g].map(([v, s]) => `${v}:${s.size}`).join(' '));
```

```text
a:2 b:3 c:3 d:2
```

(The isolated vertex e never appears in an edge list, so a real
implementation must add vertices separately; that is a classic source of
off-by-one bugs in graph code.)

**Realistic: degree in a service map.** In a graph of microservices where
an edge means "these two services talk to each other", a high-degree
service is a hub: an outage there affects many neighbours, and it is
often where caching or rate limiting goes first. An isolated vertex is a
service no one calls, which is either a scheduled job or something to
delete.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** storing an undirected edge only in one direction, say
in the neighbour list of the smaller endpoint.

**Why it fails:** degree, adjacency checks and traversals then see only
half the edges from some vertices. A search started at d will not find
b if b—d was stored only under b.

**The right way:** store each undirected edge in both endpoints' lists,
or store an edge list and derive both directions when needed.

### In real systems

- **Graph databases and libraries** distinguish directed and undirected graphs at creation time precisely because this storage choice affects every algorithm.
- **Social networks** with mutual friendship are simple graphs; follower networks are digraphs.
- **Network topology** (cables between machines) is undirected, though traffic on it is directed.

## 11.2 Sexual Demographics in America

### In one sentence

In any bipartite graph, both sides' degree sums equal the number of
edges, so the ratio of the two sides' average degrees is fixed by the
ratio of their sizes alone, and the handshaking lemma says that in any
simple graph the degree sum is twice the number of edges.

### The idea

#### The book's survey example

The book opens with surveys that reported very different average numbers
of opposite-sex partners for men and for women. Model it as a graph whose
vertices are people and whose edges join two people who have been
partners, considering only male–female pairs. Every edge has exactly one
endpoint on each side. So the degree sum on one side is the number of
edges, and so is the degree sum on the other.

Divide each side's sum by its size and you get the two average degrees.
Since the sums are equal, the averages are in the ratio of the side
sizes, inverted: the smaller side must have the larger average, by
exactly the ratio of the sizes. With roughly equal numbers of men and
women, the averages must be roughly equal, whatever anyone's behaviour.
A survey reporting a large gap is wrong, not surprising. The book adds
that nobody knows for sure why such surveys come out wrong; it might be
exaggeration or under-reporting, but the graph only proves that the
reported numbers cannot all be true.

The same arithmetic explains a "remarkable" finding from a campus survey
the book describes: members of a minority group studied with members of
the majority more often, on average, than the other way round. That is
just what "minority" means; the smaller side of a bipartite graph has the
larger average degree.

#### 11.2.1 Handshaking Lemma

The argument generalises to any simple graph: **the sum of all degrees is
twice the number of edges**, because each edge adds one to the degree of
each of its two endpoints. The name comes from a party: total up how many
hands each person shook, and you get twice the number of handshakes.

A useful consequence: the number of vertices with odd degree is even,
because the degree sum is even, and the even-degree vertices contribute an
even amount.

### Worked example

**Simple: handshakes on a random graph.** With a fixed seed, 25 random
attempts at edges among 12 vertices (repeats and self-pairs are skipped):

```ts
const degs = [...g.values()].map((s) => s.size);
console.log('degrees', JSON.stringify(degs), 'sum', degs.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0),
  'edges', edgesOf(g).length, 'odd-degree vertices', degs.filter((d) => d % 2).length);
```

```text
degrees [4,4,2,6,2,2,5,2,2,5,5,3] sum 42 edges 21 odd-degree vertices 4
```

42 is twice 21, and the four odd-degree vertices are an even number, as
the lemma predicts.

**Realistic: averages across two sides.** A bipartite graph with 40
vertices on the left and 50 on the right, each pair joined with
probability 0.08 (fixed seed):

```ts
const avgL = E / L, avgR = E / R;
console.log(`edges ${E}, avg degree left ${avgL.toFixed(3)}, right ${avgR.toFixed(3)}, ratio ${(avgL / avgR).toFixed(3)}, |R|/|L| ${(R / L).toFixed(3)}`);
```

```text
edges 179, avg degree left 4.475, right 3.580, ratio 1.250, |R|/|L| 1.250
```

The ratio of averages is exactly 50/40, whatever the random edges were.
Change the probability or the seed and the two averages move, but the
ratio does not.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** reading a difference in average degree between two
groups as a difference in behaviour.

**Why it fails:** in a bipartite relationship the averages are forced
apart by the group sizes alone.

**The right way:** before interpreting averages over a two-sided
relation, compute what the size ratio forces; only a gap beyond that
needs explaining (and in a pure two-sided relation, there cannot be one).

### In real systems

- **Marketplace metrics:** in a two-sided market, "average orders per buyer" and "average orders per seller" are in the ratio of sellers to buyers, so comparing them says nothing about engagement.
- **Data quality checks:** if two systems log the two ends of the same relation, the totals must agree; a mismatch is a logging bug. The handshaking lemma is a cheap consistency test.
- **Graph libraries** use the degree-sum identity to size edge arrays: total adjacency entries are twice the edge count.

## 11.3 Some Common Graphs

### In one sentence

A few graphs recur so often they have names: the complete graph K_n with
all n(n − 1)/2 edges, the empty graph with none, the line graph L_n (a
path of n − 1 edges), and the cycle C_n (a line graph with its ends
joined).

### The idea

#### The four families

- **Complete graph K_n:** n vertices, every pair adjacent. Each vertex has degree n − 1, so by handshaking there are n(n − 1)/2 edges.
- **Empty graph:** n vertices, no edges. Every vertex is isolated.
- **Line graph L_n:** vertices v_1 to v_n with edges v_1—v_2, v_2—v_3 and so on up to v_(n−1)—v_n: n − 1 edges in a row. The book also mentions the one-way infinite line graph on the nonnegative integers, with an edge between each k and k + 1.
- **Cycle C_n:** L_n plus the edge v_n—v_1, so n edges, every vertex of degree 2 (for n at least 3).

They nest: the empty graph on n vertices sits inside L_n, L_n inside C_n,
and C_n inside K_n, as subgraphs (11.8).

<Layers caption="The named graphs on n vertices, from fewest edges to most. Each is a subgraph of the next." layers={[
  { title: "empty graph", body: "0 edges, all degrees 0" },
  { title: "line graph L_n", body: "n − 1 edges, two ends of degree 1, the rest degree 2" },
  { title: "cycle C_n", body: "n edges, every degree 2" },
  { title: "complete graph K_n", body: "n(n − 1)/2 edges, every degree n − 1" },
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple: edge counts.**

```ts
for (const n of [5, 10, 100])
  console.log(`n=${n}: K_n edges ${n * (n - 1) / 2}, L_n edges ${n - 1}, C_n edges ${n}`);
```

```text
n=5: K_n edges 10, L_n edges 4, C_n edges 5
n=10: K_n edges 45, L_n edges 9, C_n edges 10
n=100: K_n edges 4950, L_n edges 99, C_n edges 100
```

**Realistic: topologies by name.** A full mesh of n servers (everyone
connected to everyone) is K_n, and its n(n − 1)/2 links are why full
meshes stop scaling: 100 servers need 4,950 links. A daisy chain is L_n,
cheap but fragile (one broken link splits it). A ring is C_n, the
classic token-ring and consistent-hashing shape, which survives any one
link failure.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** counting K_n's edges as n(n − 1), one for each ordered
pair.

**Why it fails:** that counts each undirected edge twice, once from each
end.

**The right way:** count ordered pairs and divide by two, or count
unordered pairs directly: n choose 2.

### In real systems

- **Full-mesh VPNs and service meshes** pay the K_n cost in configuration and connections, which is why hub-and-spoke or sidecar proxies are used at scale.
- **Rings** appear in consistent hashing, token-passing protocols and some interconnects.
- **Chains** appear in replication pipelines (chain replication) where a write passes along L_n.

## 11.4 Isomorphism

### In one sentence

Two graphs are isomorphic when some bijection between their vertex sets
maps edges exactly to edges and non-edges to non-edges, so they are the
same graph up to renaming; properties that survive renaming (number of
vertices and edges, degree counts, cycles) can prove two graphs are not
isomorphic.

### The idea

#### Same picture, different labels

Two drawings can look nothing alike and still describe the same
connections. The book adapts the digraph definition from chapter 9: an
**isomorphism** from G to H is a bijection f from V(G) to V(H) such that
u—v is an edge of G exactly when f(u)—f(v) is an edge of H. When one
exists, G and H are **isomorphic**.

Isomorphism is an equivalence relation: the identity map shows
reflexivity, the inverse of an isomorphism is an isomorphism (symmetry),
and composing two isomorphisms gives one (transitivity). The book's
remark that "graph theory is all about properties preserved by
isomorphism" means that when we say "K_5" or "C_5" we mean the whole
equivalence class, not a particular labelled set.

#### Preserved properties

A property is **preserved under isomorphism** if every graph isomorphic
to one with the property also has it. Because f is a bijection, vertex
counts agree. Because f maps edges to edges both ways, edge counts agree,
and v and f(v) have the same degree, so the number of vertices of each
degree agrees. Cycles map to cycles, so the lengths of cycles agree, and
so on.

These invariants are a quick way to prove two graphs are **not**
isomorphic: find a preserved property one has and the other lacks. They
do not prove two graphs **are** isomorphic; for that you need an actual
bijection.

#### How hard is it?

Deciding isomorphism is usually easy in practice, but the book notes that
no procedure is known that is guaranteed to run in polynomial time on
every pair of graphs. A fast method would help chemistry databases search
for molecules by their bond graphs; proof that none exists would also be
valuable, because some security protocols are designed around the
assumption that the problem is hard. From here on the book, and this
page, assume graphs are finite.

### Worked example

**Simple: the pentagon and the pentagram are the same graph.** C_5 drawn
as a pentagon uses edges 0—1, 1—2, 2—3, 3—4, 4—0. The five-pointed star
drawn through the same points uses 0—2, 2—4, 4—1, 1—3, 3—0. Brute force
over all 120 bijections:

```ts
function iso(n: number, e1: number[][], e2: number[][]) {
  const s2 = new Set(e2.map(([u, v]) => key(u, v)));
  if (e1.length !== e2.length) return null;
  for (const f of perms(n)) if (e1.every(([u, v]) => s2.has(key(f[u], f[v])))) return f;
  return null;
}
const pentagon = [[0, 1], [1, 2], [2, 3], [3, 4], [4, 0]];
const pentagram = [[0, 2], [2, 4], [4, 1], [1, 3], [3, 0]];
console.log('pentagon ≅ pentagram via', JSON.stringify(iso(5, pentagon, pentagram)));
```

```text
pentagon ≅ pentagram via [2,4,1,3,0]
```

Read the answer as f(0) = 2, f(1) = 4, f(2) = 1, f(3) = 3, f(4) = 0. Edge
0—1 maps to 2—4, an edge of the star; 1—2 to 4—1, and so on around.
(Since both graphs have five edges and the check maps every edge of the
first onto an edge of the second, edges also come back the other way.)

**Realistic: invariants rule things out, but not in.**

```ts
console.log('path vs star:', iso(5, path5, star5), ' degree sequences', degSeq(5, path5), degSeq(5, star5));
console.log('C6 vs two triangles: degree sequences', degSeq(6, a), degSeq(6, b), ' isomorphic?', iso(6, a, b) !== null);
```

```text
path vs star: null  degree sequences 22211 41111
C6 vs two triangles: degree sequences 222222 222222  isomorphic? false
```

The path and the star on five vertices both have four edges, but their
degree sequences differ, so they are not isomorphic, and the invariant
proves it without trying 120 bijections. C_6 and two disjoint triangles
have identical degree sequences (every vertex has degree 2) and are still
not isomorphic: one is connected and the other is not, and one has a
triangle and the other does not. A matching invariant is evidence, not
proof.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** concluding two graphs are isomorphic because they have
the same number of vertices, edges and the same degree sequence.

**Why it fails:** these are necessary, not sufficient. C_6 and two
triangles agree on all three.

**The right way:** use invariants only to rule out isomorphism; to prove
it, exhibit the bijection and check every edge.

### In real systems

- **Chemistry and drug databases** search for molecules by structure, which is a graph isomorphism or subgraph isomorphism problem; practical tools use clever invariants (canonical labellings) to make it fast on real molecules.
- **Compilers and build caches** detect duplicate computations by canonicalising expression graphs so that equal structures get equal keys.
- **Network analysis** compares a deployed topology against its intended design up to renaming of devices.

## 11.5 Bipartite Graphs & Matchings

### In one sentence

A bipartite graph splits its vertices into a left set and a right set
with every edge crossing between them, and Hall's theorem says the left
side can be matched into the right side exactly when no subset of the
left has fewer neighbours than members; degree-constrained and regular
bipartite graphs always pass this test.

### The idea

#### Bipartite graphs

A graph is **bipartite** when its vertices can be partitioned into two
nonempty sets L(G) and R(G) so that every edge has one end in each. The
survey graph in 11.2 is one. So is any "who can do which job", "which
student takes which course" or "which machine can run which task" graph.

#### 11.5.1 The Bipartite Matching Problem

Suppose each left vertex is an applicant and each right vertex is a job,
with an edge when the applicant would accept the job, and there are at
least as many jobs as applicants. Can every applicant get a different job
they accept? Formally, a **matching** is a set of edges no two of which
share an endpoint. A matching **covers** a set of vertices if each is an
endpoint of a matching edge, and it is **perfect** if it covers every
vertex.

**The matching condition.** Write N(S) for the set of neighbours of a set
S of left vertices: all right vertices adjacent to at least one member of
S. If some S has more members than neighbours, it is a **bottleneck**:
those |S| applicants have fewer than |S| acceptable jobs between them, so
they cannot all be placed. So "no bottleneck" is necessary.

**Hall's theorem: it is also sufficient.** There is a matching covering
L(G) exactly when no subset of L(G) is a bottleneck.

**Proof, necessity.** If a matching covers the left side, any set S of
left vertices is matched to |S| distinct right vertices, all in N(S). So
|N(S)| is at least |S|.

**Proof, sufficiency, by strong induction on the number m of left
vertices.** With one left vertex, "no bottleneck" means it has at least
one neighbour; match it there. For m + 1 left vertices (m at least 1),
take two cases.

- **Case 1: every nonempty set of at most m left vertices has strictly more neighbours than members.** Then there is slack. Match any left vertex to any neighbour and remove both. Every remaining set S lost at most one neighbour, so it still has at least |S|, and the induction hypothesis matches the rest.
- **Case 2: some nonempty set X of at most m left vertices has exactly |X| neighbours, Y = N(X).** The condition holds inside X (its subsets are subsets of the whole left side, and their neighbours lie in Y), so by induction X can be matched onto Y. Now match the remaining left vertices into the remaining right vertices, avoiding Y. That smaller problem also has no bottleneck: if some set S of remaining left vertices had fewer than |S| neighbours outside Y, then S together with X would have fewer than |S| + |X| neighbours in the whole graph, a bottleneck in the original. So induction matches it too, and the two matchings together cover everything.

The proof gives an algorithm, though not an efficient one, since Case 2
asks for a tight set. Efficient matching algorithms exist (augmenting
paths, below), so a problem that reduces to matching is solved in
practice.

#### An easy matching condition

Checking every subset is hopeless: a 30-element set has more than a
billion subsets. A simple degree condition is enough to guarantee a
matching. Call a bipartite graph **degree-constrained** if every left
degree is at least every right degree: there is a d with deg(l) at least
d at least deg(r) for all l and r.

**Theorem: a degree-constrained bipartite graph has a matching covering
L(G).** Take any set S of left vertices. The edges leaving S number at
least d|S|, since each member has degree at least d. Each of those edges
lands in N(S), and each vertex of N(S) receives at most d edges in total.
So d|N(S)| is at least the number of edges leaving S, which is at least
d|S|. Cancel d (positive whenever the graph has an edge) to get
|N(S)| at least |S|, and apply Hall.

**Theorem: every regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching.** A
graph is **regular** if all degrees are equal (and positive, for this
theorem to be meaningful). A regular bipartite graph is degree-constrained
both ways round, so there is a matching covering L and one covering R.
The first forces |L| at most |R|, the second |R| at most |L|, so the
sides are equal, and a matching covering L also covers R: it is perfect.

### Worked example

**Simple: finding a bottleneck, then fixing it.** Search all subsets for a
bottleneck, and compute a maximum matching with augmenting paths (try to
place each applicant; if their job is taken, try to move its holder
elsewhere, recursively):

```ts
const likes: Record<string, string[]> = { Asha: ['db', 'ml'], Ben: ['db'], Chen: ['web', 'ml', 'ops'], Dara: ['db', 'ml'] };
const L = Object.keys(likes);
const N = (S: string[]) => new Set(S.flatMap((x) => likes[x]));
function bottleneck(): string[] | null {
  for (let mask = 1; mask < 1 << L.length; mask++) {
    const S = L.filter((_, i) => mask & (1 << i));
    if (N(S).size < S.length) return S;
  }
  return null;
}
function matching(): Map<string, string> {
  const owner = new Map<string, string>(); // job -> applicant
  const tryAug = (u: string, seen: Set<string>): boolean => {
    for (const r of likes[u]) {
      if (seen.has(r)) continue; seen.add(r);
      if (!owner.has(r) || tryAug(owner.get(r)!, seen)) { owner.set(r, u); return true; }
    }
    return false;
  };
  for (const u of L) tryAug(u, new Set());
  return owner;
}
console.log('bottleneck:', JSON.stringify(bottleneck()), ' N =', JSON.stringify([...N(bottleneck() ?? [])]));
console.log('best matching:', JSON.stringify([...matching()]));

console.log('after Dara adds ops — bottleneck:', JSON.stringify(bottleneck()), ' matching:', JSON.stringify([...matching()]));
```

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** matching greedily (give each applicant their first
available choice) and declaring "no matching exists" when someone is
left over.

**Why it fails:** an earlier greedy choice may have taken the only job a
later applicant can do, while the earlier applicant had alternatives.

**The right way:** use augmenting paths: when a job is taken, try to
re-place its holder. A maximum matching found this way is a certificate;
if it does not cover the left side, the search also yields a bottleneck
set.

### In real systems

- **Assignment problems** (tasks to machines, students to project slots, reviewers to papers) are bipartite matching, often with weights; augmenting-path and flow algorithms solve them.
- **Scheduling with regular graphs**: round-robin tournament and switch scheduling use the fact that regular bipartite graphs decompose into perfect matchings.
- **Input-queued network switches** compute a matching between inputs and outputs in every time slot; matching algorithms sit in their hardware.

## 11.6 The Stable Marriage Problem

### In one sentence

When two equal-sized groups each rank everyone on the other side, a
simple day-by-day proposal ritual always ends, marries everyone, leaves
no "rogue couple" who would both rather be together, and gives every
proposer the best partner they could have in any stable matching while
giving every receiver the worst.

### The idea

#### Rogue couples and stability

There are n proposers and n receivers. Each proposer ranks all receivers,
and each receiver ranks all proposers; the rankings need not agree. A
**matching** here pairs everyone off one-to-one. A **rogue couple** is a
proposer and a receiver, not matched to each other, who each prefer the
other to their own partner. A **stable matching** has no rogue couple.

The book's small example has two people on each side, where both
proposers rank the same receiver first and both receivers rank the same
proposer first. Pairing the two mutual favourites is stable: they will
not leave each other, and the other two have no one to leave for.

Stability is not guaranteed in every pairing problem. In the "buddy"
version, where anyone may pair with anyone, there may be no stable
pairing at all. The two-sided structure is what makes the guarantee
possible.

#### 11.6.1 The Mating Ritual

The book describes the procedure, due to Gale and Shapley, as a ritual
that repeats daily (it uses men serenading women; here, proposers and
receivers):

- **Morning:** each proposer goes to the top receiver still on their list. A proposer with an empty list stays home.
- **Afternoon:** each receiver with at least one suitor tells the favourite to stay around, and tells the others no.
- **Evening:** each rejected proposer crosses that receiver off their list.
- **Termination:** on the first day when every receiver has at most one suitor, each receiver is matched to that suitor.

#### 11.6.2 There is a Marriage Day

On any day the ritual does not stop, some receiver has two or more
suitors, so at least one proposer crosses someone off. Lists start with n
entries each, n² in all, and never grow. So there are at most n² days
before the termination condition must hold. (Real runs usually finish
much sooner, but n² is enough to prove termination.)

#### 11.6.3 They All Live Happily Ever After…

The key observation is that a receiver's favourite suitor can only
improve: today's favourite was not rejected, so he is back tomorrow, and
tomorrow's favourite is at least as good. The book turns this into an
invariant.

**Invariant P:** for every receiver r and proposer p, if r is crossed off
p's list, then r has a suitor she prefers to p.

**P is preserved.** r is crossed off p's list only on a day when r has a
suitor she prefers to p. From then on her favourite changes only to
someone even better. So the statement stays true. It holds vacuously at
the start (nobody is crossed off), so by the invariant principle it holds
throughout.

**Theorem: everyone is matched at the end.** Suppose proposer p is
unmatched on the last day. Then p has no one to visit, so his list is
empty: every receiver was crossed off. By P, every receiver has a suitor,
and since it is the last day each has exactly one, so all n receivers are
matched to n distinct proposers. That is everyone, including p, which
contradicts the assumption.

**Theorem: the result is stable.** Take any proposer p and receiver r who
are not matched to each other at the end. Either r was crossed off p's
list, in which case by P she has a partner she prefers to p, so she will
not leave; or r is still on p's list, in which case p's partner is higher
on his list than r (he works down the list in order), so he will not
leave. Either way they are not rogue.

#### 11.6.4 …Especially the Men

Receivers seem to have the power: they choose daily and their suitors only
improve. Yet the proposers come out ahead. Call r **feasible** for p if
there is some stable matching in which they are paired. p's **optimal**
partner is his most preferred feasible one, and **pessimal** his least.

**Invariant Q:** if r is crossed off p's list, then r is not feasible for p.

**Q is preserved.** Suppose Q holds and r is about to be crossed off p's
list, because she has a suitor t she prefers to p. Since Q holds, all of
t's feasible partners are still on his list, and he is visiting r, the top
of his list, so r is t's favourite among his feasible partners. Now
suppose some stable matching pairs r with p. In it, t is paired with some
feasible partner he likes less than r, and r is with p, whom she likes
less than t. Then t and r are a rogue couple, contradicting stability. So
r is not feasible for p.

**Theorem: the ritual gives every proposer his optimal partner and every
receiver her pessimal partner.** Everyone above p's final partner on his
list was crossed off, hence infeasible by Q, so his final partner is the
best feasible one. And if a receiver r, matched to p, had a worse feasible
partner p′ in some stable matching M, then in M, p is with someone he
likes less than r (r is his optimal partner, and he is not with her in
M), and r is with p′, whom she likes less than p: r and p are rogue in M.
So p is r's least preferred feasible partner.

#### 11.6.5 Applications

The book notes that Gale and Shapley published the algorithm in 1962,
and that a similar procedure had already been in use for about a decade
to assign new medical graduates to hospital residencies, where it ended
years of disruption from unstable assignments. Shapley later shared the
2012 economics Nobel prize for this line of work. Hospitals take several
residents each, which is a minor extension. Similar ritual-like methods
are used to assign web requests to servers and in online matching
services, because the procedure is fast and can be run in a distributed
way.

### Worked example

**Simple: the ritual, day by day.** Preferences are lists, best first.
`ritual` simulates mornings, afternoons and evenings exactly as described,
and `stable` checks every non-matched pair for a rogue couple:

```ts
type Prefs = number[][];
function ritual(P: Prefs, R: Prefs, log = false) {
  const n = P.length, lists = P.map((l) => [...l]);
  const rank = R.map((l) => { const r: number[] = []; l.forEach((p, i) => (r[p] = i)); return r; });
  let day = 0;
  for (;;) {
    day++;
    const suitors: number[][] = Array.from({ length: n }, () => []);
    lists.forEach((l, p) => { if (l.length) suitors[l[0]].push(p); });
    if (log) console.log(`day ${day}: ` + suitors.map((s, r) => `R${r}←[${s.map((p) => 'P' + p).join(',')}]`).join(' '));
    if (suitors.every((s) => s.length <= 1)) return { day, match: suitors.map((s) => s[0]) };
    suitors.forEach((s, r) => {
      if (s.length > 1) {
        const fav = s.reduce((a, b) => (rank[r][a] < rank[r][b] ? a : b));
        for (const p of s) if (p !== fav) lists[p].shift();
      }
    });
  }
}
const P: Prefs = [[0, 1, 2, 3], [1, 0, 3, 2], [0, 2, 1, 3], [2, 0, 1, 3]];
const R: Prefs = [[1, 2, 0, 3], [0, 1, 2, 3], [3, 0, 2, 1], [0, 1, 2, 3]];
const res = ritual(P, R, true);
console.log('married on day', res.day, ':', res.match.map((p, r) => `P${p}–R${r}`).join(' '), ' stable?', stable(P, R, res.match));
```

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** assuming the side that chooses (the receivers) is the

**Why it fails:** proposers get their best feasible partner, receivers
their worst, as invariant Q proves; the cyclic example shows the gap can
be as large as first choice versus last.

**The right way:** decide deliberately which side proposes. In the
residency match, the algorithm was changed to be applicant-proposing so
that the applicants get the favourable outcome.

### In real systems

- **Residency and school-choice matching** use Gale–Shapley variants with capacities (one hospital, many residents), and the choice of proposing side is a policy decision.
- **Load assignment** (requests to servers with preferences on both sides, such as latency for requests and cost for servers) uses ritual-like algorithms because they are fast and parallel.
- **Strategy-proofness:** in the proposer-proposing version, proposers have no incentive to misreport their rankings, which is part of why the algorithm is trusted in practice.

## 11.7 Coloring

### In one sentence

A colouring gives each vertex a colour so that adjacent vertices differ,
the chromatic number χ(G) is the fewest colours that work, finding it is
hard in general, and a graph whose degrees are at most k can always be
coloured with k + 1 colours by a simple induction.

### The idea

#### 11.7.1 An Exam Scheduling Problem

When edges stand for **conflicts** rather than affinities, colouring
appears. Make each exam a vertex, and join two exams when someone takes
both (so they cannot share a time slot). A time slot is a colour; a
timetable with no clashes is a colouring in which adjacent vertices have
different colours; and the shortest exam period uses the fewest colours.

A graph is **k-colourable** if it has a valid colouring with at most k
colours, and its **chromatic number** χ(G) is the least such k. The
book's five-exam example needs three slots because it contains a
triangle, whose three exams must all differ.

Checking a proposed colouring is easy; finding a colouring with few
colours seems very hard. No fast algorithm is known for deciding whether
a graph is 3-colourable, and finding one (or proving none exists) is tied
to the famous P versus NP question, which carries a million-dollar
prize.

#### 11.7.2 Some Coloring Bounds

- **Cycles:** an even cycle is 2-colourable (alternate), so χ(C_even) = 2; an odd cycle needs 3, because alternating colours around it clashes when you return to the start.
- **Complete graphs:** χ(K_n) = n, since every pair is adjacent.
- **Bipartite graphs:** colour the left side one colour and the right another. Conversely, any 2-colouring splits the vertices into two sides with all edges crossing. Only edgeless graphs have chromatic number 1, so a graph with at least one edge is bipartite exactly when χ(G) = 2.

**Theorem: a graph with maximum degree at most k is (k + 1)-colourable.**
The book warns that inducting on k is a dead end, and suggests the usual
graph choice: induct on the number of vertices n.

- **Base case, n = 1:** one vertex, degree 0, one colour.
- **Inductive step:** take a graph with n + 1 vertices and maximum degree at most k. Remove any vertex v and its edges. The rest has n vertices and maximum degree still at most k, so it is (k + 1)-colourable by hypothesis. Put v back: it has at most k neighbours, using at most k colours, so at least one of the k + 1 colours is free for v.

The bound is tight for K_(k+1) (degree k, needs k + 1 colours), and for any
graph of maximum degree k that contains K_(k+1). It can also be far from
tight: a star with n vertices has maximum degree n − 1 but is 2-colourable.

#### 11.7.3 Why coloring?

Conflicts are everywhere. The book's examples:

- **Rolling software updates:** servers that share a critical function cannot be down at the same time; colour the conflict graph and update one colour class per wave.
- **Radio frequencies:** stations with overlapping coverage need different frequencies; fewer colours means fewer licences.
- **Register allocation:** program variables whose live ranges overlap need different registers; colours are registers.
- **Map colouring:** neighbouring regions need different colours. The four colour theorem says planar maps need at most four; testing whether three suffice is as hard as general satisfiability, even for planar graphs. Chapter 12 proves the easier five colour theorem.

### Worked example

**Simple: greedy colouring of the exam graph.** Visit vertices in some
order and give each the smallest colour not used by an already-coloured
neighbour. This is exactly the inductive proof run as code:

```ts

  for (const v of order) {
    const used = new Set([...g.get(v)!].map((u) => c.get(u)));
    let k = 0; while (used.has(k)) k++;
    c.set(v, k);
  }
  return c;
}
```

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** proving the degree bound by induction on k, or by
adding a vertex to a smaller graph ("build-up").

**Why it fails:** induction on k gets stuck, because removing something
to reduce the maximum degree is not straightforward. Build-up arguments
risk proving the claim only for graphs you can build by your chosen
additions.

**The right way:** start from an arbitrary graph of the size you need,
remove a vertex, apply the hypothesis, and put the vertex back (the
shrink-down, grow-back pattern the book recommends).

### In real systems

- **Register allocation** in compilers is graph colouring on the interference graph, with spilling to memory when colours (registers) run out.
- **Timetabling and rolling deployments** colour conflict graphs to decide which tasks can run in the same slot.
- **Wireless channel assignment** colours the interference graph of access points.
- **Parallel computation on meshes** colours elements so that same-colour elements can be updated simultaneously without write conflicts.

## 11.8 Simple Walks

### In one sentence

Walks, paths and closed walks in simple graphs work as in digraphs but
ignore direction, a cycle must have length at least three, and the
clean way to say which closed walks describe the same cycle is to define
a cycle as a subgraph isomorphic to some C_n.

### The idea

#### 11.8.1 Walks, Paths, Cycles in Simple Graphs

A **walk** is an alternating sequence of vertices and edges, starting and
ending with a vertex, where each edge joins the vertex before it to the
vertex after it. Its **length** is the number of edge occurrences, one
less than the number of vertex occurrences. A **path** is a walk with no
repeated vertex. A **closed walk** starts and ends at the same vertex; a
single vertex is a closed walk (and a path) of length 0.

A **cycle** is a closed walk of length at least 3 whose vertices are
distinct except that the first equals the last. Two differences from
digraphs:

- **No length-2 cycles.** Going out along an edge and straight back is always possible in a simple graph and tells you nothing, so it does not count.
- **No length-1 cycles**, because there are no self-loops.

#### 11.8.2 Cycles as Subgraphs

A cycle has no start and no direction, yet as a closed walk it must have
both. The cycle through a, b, c, d can be written starting at any of its
four vertices and in either direction: eight closed walks for one cycle.
To avoid this, the book defines subgraphs and then cycles as subgraphs.

A graph G is a **subgraph** of H if V(G) is a subset of V(H) and E(G) is a
subset of E(H). (Every edge of G must still be an edge of H, so you cannot
invent edges, and both endpoints of each edge of G must be in V(G),
because G must itself be a graph.)

For n at least 3, C_n is the graph on 1 to n with edges 1—2, 2—3, up to
(n − 1)—n and n—1. A **cycle of G** is a subgraph of G isomorphic to some
C_n. This captures exactly the idea of "the same cycle regardless of
where you start or which way you go".

## 11.9 Connectivity

### In one sentence

Two vertices are connected when some path joins them, the connected
components partition a graph, a graph is 2-colourable exactly when it has
no odd cycle, k-edge-connectivity measures how many edge failures a
connection survives, and a graph with fewer edges than vertices must have
at least |V| − |E| components.

### The idea

#### Connected vertices and graphs

Vertices u and v are **connected** if there is a path from u to v; every
vertex is connected to itself by a length-0 path. A graph is
**connected** if every pair of vertices is connected. Connectedness is an
equivalence relation on vertices (reflexive by the length-0 path,
symmetric by reversing, transitive by joining walks and shortening to a
path).

#### 11.9.1 Connected Components

A **connected component** is a subgraph made of a vertex together with
every vertex and edge connected to it. The components are the equivalence
classes of "connected", together with their edges. A graph is connected
exactly when it has one component; the empty graph on n vertices has n.
Road networks of separate continents, or air-gapped networks kept apart
from the internet, are everyday examples of graphs with several
components.

#### 11.9.2 Odd Cycles and 2-Colorability

**Theorem: these are equivalent for any graph.** (1) It contains an odd
cycle. (2) It is not 2-colourable. (3) It contains a closed walk of odd
length. The book proves 1 implies 2 implies 3 implies 1.

- **1 implies 2.** An odd cycle needs three colours, so the whole graph cannot be 2-coloured.
- **2 implies 3.** It is enough to handle one component at a time (if every component were 2-colourable, so would the graph be), so assume G is connected. Fix a vertex r. For each vertex u, pick any walk w_u from u to r, and colour u black if w_u has even length and white if odd. Since G is not 2-colourable, this colouring fails somewhere: there is an edge u—v with u and v the same colour. Then go from u to r along w_u, back from r to v along the reverse of w_v, and then across the edge v—u. That is a closed walk of length |w_u| + |w_v| + 1. The two walk lengths have the same parity (same colour), so their sum is even and the total is odd.
- **3 implies 1.** By well ordering, take an odd closed walk w of minimum length. If w is a cycle, done. Otherwise some vertex repeats besides the start/end. If the start vertex x itself reappears in the middle, w splits into two closed walks at x whose lengths add to |w|; one of them is odd and shorter, a contradiction. If some other vertex y appears twice, w is a walk f from x to y, then a closed walk g from y back to y, then a walk r from y to x. If g is odd it is a shorter odd closed walk; if g is even, then f followed by r is a closed walk of odd length (|w| minus an even number) that is shorter. Either way, a contradiction. (Its length is at least 3 since a closed walk of length 1 is impossible in a simple graph, so the minimal one is a genuine cycle.)

The practical reading: **2-colourability is easy to test**. Breadth-first
search colours by parity of distance from a start vertex; an edge between
two same-coloured vertices witnesses an odd cycle.

#### 11.9.3 k-connected Graphs

Connectivity that survives failures matters for cables, pipelines and
power lines. Two vertices are **k-edge connected** if they remain
connected after deleting any k − 1 edges, and a graph (with at least two
vertices) is k-edge connected if every pair of distinct vertices is. Being
k-connected implies being j-connected for every j up to k, and
"1-connected" is the same as "connected". (Texts often use "k-connected"
for the vertex-deletion version; the book uses edges, and drops the word
"edge".) Every cycle is 2-connected, and K_n is (n − 1)-connected.

An edge whose removal disconnects two connected vertices is a **cut edge**
(also called a bridge). A graph with more than one vertex is 2-connected
exactly when it is connected with no cut edges, and **an edge is a cut
edge exactly when it is not on any cycle**: if the edge u—v is on a cycle,
the rest of the cycle reconnects u and v; if removing it disconnects u and
v, there was no other u-to-v path, so no cycle through it.

Two vertices joined by k paths that share no edge are k-connected, since
an attacker must cut at least one edge on each path. **Menger's theorem**
says the converse also holds: k-connected vertices are always joined by k
edge-disjoint paths. The book states it without proof.

#### 11.9.4 The Minimum Number of Edges in a Connected Graph

**Theorem: every graph G has at least |V(G)| − |E(G)| connected
components.** By induction on the number k of edges. With no edges, each
vertex is its own component: |V| components, matching |V| − 0. For a
graph with k + 1 edges, remove one edge e to get a graph with k edges and,
by hypothesis, at least |V| − k components. Put e back. If its ends were
already in the same component, nothing changes, and |V| − k is more than
|V| − (k + 1). If they were in different components, those two merge, and
the count drops by one, to at least |V| − (k + 1). Either way the claim
holds.

**Corollary: a connected graph on n vertices has at least n − 1 edges.**
One component means 1 is at least n − |E|.

The book flags two lessons from the proof. Inducting on edges (or
vertices) is the first thing to try for graph problems. And the proof
**removes** an edge from an arbitrary (k + 1)-edge graph rather than
**adding** one to a k-edge graph, because "build-up" arguments can
silently fail to reach every graph of the larger size.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** treating "connected" and "2-connected" as the same for

**The right way:** look for cut edges (bridges). A network that must
survive any single link failure needs to be 2-edge-connected, meaning
every edge lies on a cycle.

### In real systems

- **Network reliability engineering** finds bridges and articulation points in topology graphs to locate single points of failure.
- **Union-find** maintains connected components as edges arrive, which is how Kruskal's algorithm (11.10) and many clustering methods work.
- **Bipartiteness tests** check two-sided constraints, such as whether a set of "must differ" rules (two teams, two shifts) can be satisfied.
- **Garbage collectors** compute the component reachable from roots; everything else is unreachable.

## 11.10 Forests & Trees

### In one sentence

A forest is a graph with no cycles and a tree is a connected forest; trees
are the minimal connected graphs, with exactly n − 1 edges, a unique path
between any two vertices, at least two leaves, and every connected graph
contains one (a spanning tree), the cheapest of which can be found by
repeatedly adding the lightest edge that does not close a cycle.

### The idea

**Definitions.** A graph is **acyclic** if it has no cycles. An acyclic
graph is a **forest**; a connected acyclic graph is a **tree**. The
components of a forest are trees.

#### 11.10.1 Leaves, Parents & Children

A **leaf** is a vertex of degree 1 in a tree (or forest). Pick any vertex
as the **root** and draw it at the top; every other vertex has a unique
**parent**, the next vertex on the path to the root, and its **children**
are the neighbours further from the root. This gives the familiar family
vocabulary: ancestors, descendants, siblings. The same undirected tree
can be rooted anywhere, and each choice gives a different family tree.

#### 11.10.2 Properties

**Lemma: every connected subgraph of a tree is a tree.** A cycle in the
subgraph would be a cycle in the tree.

**Lemma: a tree with n vertices has n − 1 edges.** By induction on n. One
vertex, zero edges. For n + 1 vertices, take a leaf (one exists, see
below) and delete it with its edge. What remains is connected (a path
between two other vertices never passes through a leaf, since a path
entering a degree-1 vertex cannot leave it) and acyclic, so it is a tree
with n vertices and n − 1 edges; adding the leaf back gives n edges.

**Leaves exist.** Take a longest path in a tree with at least two
vertices. Its endpoint v has the previous path vertex as a neighbour. Any
other neighbour w of v is either on the path, closing a cycle, or off it,
giving a longer path. Neither can happen, so v has degree 1. Both ends
of the longest path are leaves, so **every tree with at least two
vertices has at least two leaves**.

**Theorem: for a tree T, all of these hold.**

1. Every connected subgraph is a tree.
2. There is a unique path between any two vertices. (Two different paths between u and v would contain a cycle.)
3. Adding any edge between nonadjacent vertices creates a cycle. (The new edge plus the unique path between its ends.)
4. Removing any edge disconnects the graph. (Every edge of a tree is a cut edge, since it lies on no cycle.)
5. If T has at least two vertices, it has at least two leaves.
6. The number of vertices is one more than the number of edges.

Properties 3 and 4 say that trees are **maximally acyclic** and
**minimally connected**. And the bound from 11.9.4 is tight exactly here:
a connected graph needs n − 1 edges, and a tree has exactly n − 1.

#### 11.10.3 Spanning Trees

A **spanning subgraph** uses all the vertices of G. A **spanning tree** is
a spanning subgraph that is a tree.

**Theorem: every connected graph has a spanning tree.** Among the
connected spanning subgraphs of G (G itself is one), take one, T, with
the fewest edges. If T had a cycle, removing any edge of that cycle would
keep it connected (the rest of the cycle reroutes) and spanning, with
fewer edges, contradicting minimality. So T is acyclic and connected: a
spanning tree.

#### 11.10.4 Minimum Weight Spanning Trees

Give every edge a **weight**, such as cable cost or latency. The weight of
a spanning tree is the sum of its edge weights, and a **minimum weight
spanning tree** (MST) has the least weight among all spanning trees.

The book gives one general procedure and proves it correct. Grow a forest
F one edge at a time, starting from no edges. At each step, call an edge
**gray** with respect to a colouring of the vertices into black and white
(where no edge of F crosses between colours) if it joins a black vertex
to a white one. Add a minimum-weight gray edge. Different rules for
choosing the colouring give the classic algorithms:

- **Kruskal-style:** consider edges in increasing weight and add each one that does not close a cycle with F. (Colour black the component of F containing one end of the next accepted edge, and everything else white; that edge is then a lightest gray edge.)
- **Prim-style:** grow one tree from a start vertex; colour the tree black and the rest white, and add the lightest edge leaving the tree.
- **A third option** in the book: in each round, every component of F takes its own lightest outgoing edge (in the style of Borůvka's method).

**Why it is correct.** The invariant is: **F is a subgraph of some minimum
spanning tree**. Initially F has no edges, so this holds. Suppose F is
contained in an MST T, and the algorithm adds a minimum-weight gray edge
e. If e is in T, F + e is still in T. If not, adding e to T creates a cycle
(tree property 3). That cycle crosses from black to white along e, so it
must cross back along some other gray edge e′, which is in T but not in F
(edges of F never cross colours). Swap: T − e′ + e is still a spanning tree
(connected, n − 1 edges), and its weight is at most T's because e was a
lightest gray edge. So it is also an MST, and it contains F + e. When F
has n − 1 edges, it is itself a spanning tree inside an MST, so it is that
MST.

```ts
type E = [string, string, number];
function kruskal(edges: E[]) {
  const parent = new Map<string, string>();
  const find = (x: string): string => {
    if (!parent.has(x)) parent.set(x, x);
    const p = parent.get(x)!;
    return p === x ? x : (parent.set(x, find(p)), parent.get(x)!);
  };
  const out: E[] = [];
  for (const e of [...edges].sort((x, y) => x[2] - y[2])) {
    const [u, v] = [find(e[0]), find(e[1])];
    if (u !== v) { parent.set(u, v); out.push(e); }
  }
  return out;
}
function prim(edges: E[], start: string) {
  const inT = new Set([start]), V = new Set(edges.flatMap((e) => [e[0], e[1]])), out: E[] = [];
  while (inT.size < V.size) {
    const gray = edges.filter((e) => inT.has(e[0]) !== inT.has(e[1]));
    const best = gray.reduce((x, y) => (y[2] < x[2] ? y : x));
    out.push(best); inT.add(best[0]); inT.add(best[1]);
  }
  return out;
}
```

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** building a cheap network by giving every vertex its
single cheapest edge and stopping there.

**Why it fails:** the chosen edges can form several separate clusters (in
W, a picks a—c, d picks d—e, f picks e—f, but nothing joins a, b, c to
d, e, f), so the result need not be connected.

**The right way:** that step is only round one of the component-by-
component method. Repeat it on components until one remains, or run
Kruskal or Prim, which never stop before n − 1 edges.

### In real systems

- **Network design:** laying cable, pipes or power lines to connect sites at least cost is the MST problem.
- **Clustering:** stopping Kruskal early, or removing the heaviest MST edges, gives single-linkage clusters.
- **Spanning tree protocols** in switched Ethernet compute a spanning tree to prevent broadcast loops, keeping redundant links switched off until a failure.
- **Approximation:** an MST gives a fast 2-approximation for the metric travelling salesperson tour (walk the tree, skipping repeats).
- **Data structures:** file systems, the DOM, parse trees and organisation charts are rooted trees; the parent and child vocabulary is exactly 11.10.1.

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **The empty graph:** n vertices, no edges, n components, chromatic number 1, a forest but not a tree unless n = 1.
- **One vertex:** a tree with no leaves; the "two leaves" property needs at least two vertices.
- **Ties in weights:** several minimum spanning trees can exist; with distinct weights there is exactly one.
- **Unequal sides in matching:** a perfect matching needs |L| = |R|; Hall's condition then covers both sides.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **Length-2 walks:** going out and back along one edge is never a cycle in a simple graph.
- **Isomorphism versus equal invariants:** matching degree sequences and edge counts do not prove two graphs isomorphic; only an explicit bijection does.
- **Greedy colouring order:** the same graph can need 2 colours under one order and n under another, as the crown graph shows.
- **Stable matching with unequal sides or incomplete lists:** the ritual still runs, but some participants stay unmatched and the "everyone marries" proof needs adjusting.

## Questions people ask

**Why does the book separate simple graphs from digraphs?** Many
questions (colouring, matching, connectivity) only care whether two
things are related, not in which direction. Dropping direction makes the
definitions and proofs cleaner.

**Is graph isomorphism hard?** No efficient algorithm is known for all
graphs, though it is not known to be as hard as the hardest search
problems, and practical tools handle most real graphs quickly. The useful
habit is to compare invariants first to rule pairs out cheaply.

**Is the stable matching algorithm used in practice?** Yes. Deferred
acceptance is the basis of national medical residency matching and of
many school choice systems. Real versions handle extras such as couples
and capacities.

**Why are proposers favoured if receivers choose?** Proposers work down
their lists from the top and only move on when rejected, so each one ends
with the best partner possible in any stable matching. Receivers only
ever choose among those who show up.

**Does greedy colouring ever give the optimum?** For some ordering of the
vertices, yes, it always can. Finding that ordering is as hard as
colouring itself, so in practice greedy is a heuristic with the
guarantee of at most one more than the maximum degree.

**Why does Kruskal's algorithm work if it is "just greedy"?** Because of
the exchange argument: whenever it adds a lightest gray edge, swapping
that edge into any minimum spanning tree that lacks it cannot raise the
weight. Greedy succeeds here because the problem has that exchange
property; many problems do not.

## Key takeaways

- A simple graph has undirected edges, no self-loops and no repeated edges; degree is the number of incident edges.
- Handshaking lemma: the degree sum is twice the number of edges, so the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
- Isomorphism is a bijection preserving adjacency; invariants such as degree sequences can rule it out but not prove it.
- Hall's theorem: a bipartite graph has a matching covering L exactly when every subset S of L has at least |S| neighbours; regular bipartite graphs have perfect matchings.
- The proposal ritual always ends in a stable matching that is best for every proposer and worst for every receiver among stable matchings.
- A graph with maximum degree k is (k + 1)-colourable; a graph is 2-colourable exactly when it has no odd cycle.
- An edge is a cut edge exactly when it lies on no cycle; a graph has at least |V| − |E| components.
- Trees are connected and acyclic, have n − 1 edges, unique paths and at least two leaves; every connected graph has a spanning tree.
- Adding a lightest gray edge repeatedly (Kruskal, Prim, or by components) builds a minimum spanning tree.

## Glossary

- **Simple graph:** a set of vertices and a set of edges, each edge a two-element set of vertices.
- **Adjacent:** joined by an edge. **Incident:** an edge is incident to its endpoints.
- **Degree:** the number of edges incident to a vertex.
- **Handshaking lemma:** the sum of degrees equals twice the number of edges.
- **Complete graph K_n:** every pair of n vertices adjacent.
- **Empty graph:** vertices with no edges.
- **Line graph L_n and cycle C_n:** a path of n vertices, and a ring of n vertices.
- **Isomorphism:** a bijection between vertex sets that preserves adjacency in both directions.
- **Bipartite graph:** vertices split into two sides with every edge crossing.
- **Matching:** a set of edges with no shared endpoints; **perfect** if it covers every vertex.
- **Hall's condition:** every subset of the left side has at least as many neighbours as members.
- **Stable matching:** a perfect matching with no rogue couple, a pair who both prefer each other to their partners.
- **Feasible partner:** someone you are paired with in at least one stable matching.
- **Colouring:** an assignment of colours to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ; **chromatic number** χ is the fewest colours needed.
- **Walk, path, cycle:** an edge-by-edge route; one with no repeated vertex; a closed one of length at least 3 with no other repeats.
- **Subgraph:** a graph whose vertices and edges are subsets of another's.
- **Connected component:** a maximal set of mutually connected vertices with their edges.
- **k-edge connected:** stays connected after removing any k − 1 edges.
- **Cut edge (bridge):** an edge whose removal disconnects its endpoints.
- **Forest and tree:** an acyclic graph, and a connected acyclic graph.
- **Leaf:** a vertex of degree 1 in a tree.
- **Spanning tree:** a tree using all vertices of a graph.
- **Minimum spanning tree:** a spanning tree of least total edge weight.
- **Gray edge:** an edge joining the two colour classes in the MST procedure.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up (by hand).**

1. A graph has degrees 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1. How many edges does it have, and could the degrees 3, 3, 3, 2 belong to a simple graph?
2. Show that the two 6-vertex graphs "C_6" and "two disjoint triangles" are not isomorphic, using one invariant.
3. Run the proposal ritual by hand for three proposers and three receivers with lists you make up, then swap roles and compare the results.
4. Run Prim's algorithm on W starting from f and list the edges in the order they are added.

**Build (code and test).**

5. Write a function that checks Hall's condition by trying every subset of the left side, and compare its answer with an augmenting-path matching algorithm on random bipartite graphs with up to 12 left vertices.
6. Implement the proposal ritual and a stability checker, then on 1,000 random instances confirm that the result is stable and that swapping roles changes who does better.
7. Write a bridge finder based on depth-first search and low-link values, and test it against the brute-force "remove each edge" method on random graphs.

**Stretch (change the problem).**

8. Prove that a graph is a tree exactly when it is connected and has n − 1 edges.
9. Find a family of graphs on which greedy colouring in some vertex order uses many more colours than the chromatic number, and explain the ordering that makes greedy optimal for it.

**Self-check.**

<details>
<summary>1. Why must the number of odd-degree vertices be even?</summary>

The degree sum is twice the number of edges, an even number, and a sum of integers is even only if it has an even number of odd terms.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What is a bottleneck in a bipartite matching problem?</summary>

A set S of left vertices with fewer neighbours than members; by Hall's theorem, a matching covering the left side exists exactly when there is none.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why is the ritual's result stable?</summary>

If p prefers r to his partner, r crossed him off earlier for someone she preferred, and her suitors only improve, so she prefers her own partner to p.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why is every edge of a tree a cut edge?</summary>

A tree has no cycles, and an edge whose endpoints stay connected after its removal would lie on a cycle.

</details>
