A directed graph is a set of points with arrows between them, and once you
look for them they are everywhere: links between web pages, calls between
functions, imports between modules, prerequisite chains in a course
catalogue, steps in a build pipeline. This chapter treats digraphs and
binary relations as the same thing seen two ways, and uses that to answer
practical questions: how many routes are there from one vertex to
another, how fast can a set of dependent tasks finish on parallel
machines, and what exactly is an "order". It ends with a catalogue of
relational properties (reflexive, transitive, antisymmetric and the rest)
that every later chapter uses without comment.

## 9.1 Vertex Degrees

### In one sentence

Every directed edge leaves one vertex and enters one, so the in-degrees
and the out-degrees of a digraph both add up to the number of edges.

### The idea

A **directed graph** (digraph) G has a non-empty set of **vertices** V(G),
also called nodes, and a set of **directed edges** E(G). An edge goes from
its **tail** u to its **head** v and is written u → v. The book points
out that a digraph is exactly a binary relation on V whose domain and
codomain are both V: the edges are the related pairs. Chapter 4 already
drew relations as arrows; now the arrows are the main object.

The **in-degree** of v is the number of edges with head v, and the
**out-degree** is the number with tail v. Since each edge has exactly one
tail and one head, counting edges by their tails and by their heads gives
the same total:

sum of in-degrees = sum of out-degrees = |E|.

This is the double-counting idea that chapter 14 will develop: count the
same set two ways and equate the answers.

The book's examples of digraphs include the divisibility relation on a
range of integers and the link structure of the web, where in-degree
(how many pages link here) was an early signal of a page's importance in
search ranking.

### Worked example

**Simple: degrees from an adjacency list.** Represent the digraph as a Map
from each vertex to its out-neighbours:

```ts
type Graph = Map<string, string[]>;
const G: Graph = new Map([['a', ['b', 'c']], ['b', ['c', 'd']], ['c', ['d']], ['d', ['a', 'e']], ['e', []]]);
const V = [...G.keys()];
const outDeg = (v: string) => G.get(v)!.length;
const inDeg = (v: string) => V.filter((u) => G.get(u)!.includes(v)).length;
console.log(V.map((v) => `${v}: in ${inDeg(v)} out ${outDeg(v)}`).join(' | '),
  ' sums', V.reduce((s, v) => s + inDeg(v), 0), V.reduce((s, v) => s + outDeg(v), 0));
```

```text
a: in 1 out 2 | b: in 1 out 2 | c: in 2 out 1 | d: in 2 out 2 | e: in 1 out 0  sums 7 7
```

**Realistic: what degrees tell you in a dependency graph.** In a module
import graph, out-degree is how many modules a file imports, and in-degree
is how many import it. A module with high in-degree is load-bearing:
changing its interface ripples widely. A module with in-degree 0 is an
entry point or dead code. A vertex with out-degree 0, like e above, is a
sink: in a build graph, a leaf library; in a state machine, a final
state. Note the asymmetry the adjacency list creates: out-degree is a
lookup, but in-degree requires scanning every list, which is why graph
libraries often store reverse edges too.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** using undirected intuition, "the degree sum is twice
the number of edges", for a digraph.

**Why it fails:** that is the undirected handshake rule (chapter 11). In a
digraph each edge contributes once to the in-degree sum and once to the
out-degree sum, not twice to a single sum.

**The right way:** keep in- and out-degrees separate; their total over
both is 2|E|, but each alone is |E|.

### In real systems

- **Web ranking:** PageRank refines in-degree by weighting each incoming link by the importance of the page it comes from.
- **Social networks:** followers (in-degree) and following (out-degree) are tracked separately, and the asymmetry is the point.
- **Build tools** compute in-degrees to find which targets have no unbuilt dependencies left (Kahn's topological sort algorithm, 9.5).

## 9.2 Walks and Paths

### In one sentence

A walk follows edges and may revisit vertices, a path never repeats a
vertex, and the shortest walk between two vertices is always a path.

### The idea

A **walk** is an alternating sequence of vertices and edges, starting and
ending at vertices, where each edge goes from the vertex before it to the
vertex after it. Its **length** is the number of edges, so a single vertex
is a walk of length 0. A **path** is a walk that never visits a vertex
twice. A **closed walk** starts and ends at the same vertex, and a
**cycle** is a positive-length closed walk whose only repeat is the
start/end vertex.

Walks can be **merged**: a walk from u to v followed by a walk from v to w
gives a walk from u to w, of length the sum. (The book notes that it is
not quite concatenation, since v would appear twice in a row.)

#### Finding a path

**Theorem.** The shortest walk from u to a different vertex v is a path.

**Proof.** If there is any walk from u to v, well ordering gives a
shortest one, w. Suppose w repeats some vertex x. Then w is: a walk from u
to x, then a positive-length walk from x back to x, then a walk from x to
v. Cut out the middle part. What is left is still a walk from u to v,
and it is strictly shorter, contradicting the choice of w. So w repeats
nothing: it is a path.

The **distance** dist(u, v) is the length of a shortest path from u to v
(infinite if none). It satisfies the **triangle inequality**: dist(u, v) ≤
dist(u, x) + dist(x, v), because merging shortest paths through x gives a
walk, and the shortest walk is no longer than that. The book warns that
distance in a digraph is not symmetric: dist(u, v) and dist(v, u) can
differ, and one can be infinite while the other is finite.

The same cutting argument shows that the shortest positive-length closed
walk through a vertex is a cycle. So a digraph has a closed walk of
positive length iff it has a cycle, which 9.5 uses to define DAGs.

### Worked example

**Simple: the proof as a program.** Scan a walk; whenever a vertex
reappears, cut back to its first occurrence. That is exactly the proof's
"remove the loop" step, applied repeatedly:

```ts
function toPath(walk: string[]): string[] {
  const out: string[] = [];
  for (const v of walk) { const i = out.indexOf(v); if (i >= 0) out.length = i; out.push(v); }
  return out;
}
console.log('walk a b c d a c d e →', toPath(['a', 'b', 'c', 'd', 'a', 'c', 'd', 'e']).join(' '));
```

```text
walk a b c d a c d e → a c d e
```

**Realistic: why the theorem matters for routing.** A route planner never
needs to consider walks that revisit a place, so the search space is the
set of paths, which is finite even when walks are infinitely many. Every
shortest-path algorithm (breadth-first search for unweighted graphs,
Dijkstra for non-negative weights) relies on this. With negative edge
weights the theorem can fail, since going round a negative cycle makes a
walk "shorter", which is why Bellman–Ford has to detect such cycles.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** assuming dist(u, v) = dist(v, u) in a digraph, for
example when caching distances in a symmetric table.

**Why it fails:** edges have direction. In the example graph, e is
reachable from a in three steps, but a is not reachable from e at all.

**The right way:** store distances as an ordered-pair table, and only
symmetrise for undirected graphs.

### In real systems

- **Navigation with one-way streets** is a digraph distance problem; the route there and the route back differ.
- **Network routing protocols** compute shortest paths, and loop-free forwarding is the path property.
- **Cycle detection in dependency managers** reports a closed walk as an error, using the fact that any closed walk contains a cycle to show the user a minimal one.

## 9.3 Adjacency Matrices

### In one sentence

Write a digraph as a 0/1 matrix, and its k-th power counts the walks of
length k between every pair of vertices.

### The idea

Number the vertices 1 to n. The **adjacency matrix** A has entry A_uv = 1
if u → v is an edge and 0 otherwise. More generally the book defines the
**length-k walk counting matrix** C, whose entry C_uv is the number of
walks of length k from u to v. A itself is the length-1 walk counting
matrix, and the identity matrix is the length-0 one.

**Theorem.** If C counts walks of length k and D counts walks of length
m, then the matrix product CD counts walks of length k + m.

**Proof.** Every walk of length k + m from u to v splits uniquely into a
length-k walk from u to some vertex w followed by a length-m walk from w
to v. For a fixed w, there are C_uw · D_wv such pairs. Summing over all w
gives the number of walks, and that sum is exactly the definition of the
(u, v) entry of CD.

**Corollary.** A^k is the length-k walk counting matrix, by induction on k.

#### Shortest paths

A path never needs more than n − 1 edges (it has at most n vertices).
So the distance from u to v is the smallest k for which A^k has a
non-zero (u, v) entry, if any k up to n − 1 works. The book notes this
is a simple, not efficient, method; refinements lead to the
shortest-path algorithms of an algorithms course, including weighted
versions.

### Worked example

**Simple: walks of length 3, and distances from matrix powers.**

```ts
const idx = new Map(V.map((v, i) => [v, i]));
const A = V.map((u) => V.map((v) => (G.get(u)!.includes(v) ? 1 : 0)));
const mul = (X: number[][], Y: number[][]) => X.map((row) => Y[0].map((_, j) => row.reduce((s, x, k) => s + x * Y[k][j], 0)));
let P = A; const dist = V.map((_, i) => V.map((_, j) => (i === j ? 0 : A[i][j] ? 1 : Infinity)));
for (let k = 2; k <= V.length - 1; k++) { P = mul(P, A); P.forEach((row, i) => row.forEach((x, j) => { if (x > 0 && dist[i][j] === Infinity) dist[i][j] = k; })); if (k === 3) console.log('A^3 row a (walks of length 3 from a):', JSON.stringify(P[0])); }
console.log('dist from a:', V.map((v) => `${v}=${dist[0][idx.get(v)!]}`).join(' '), ' dist from e to a:', dist[idx.get('e')!][0]);
```

```text
A^3 row a (walks of length 3 from a): [2,0,0,1,2]
dist from a: a=0 b=1 c=1 d=2 e=3  dist from e to a: Infinity
```

Check by hand: the length-3 walks from a are a b c d, a b d a, a b d e, a
c d a and a c d e, so two end at a, one at d and two at e, matching the
row [2, 0, 0, 1, 2].

**Realistic: counts grow with cycles.** Count walks from a to d of each
length from 1 to 8:

```ts
let Q = A; const counts: number[] = [];
for (let k = 1; k <= 8; k++) { counts.push(Q[0][3]); Q = mul(Q, A); }
console.log('walks a→d of length 1..8:', counts.join(' '));
```

```text
walks a→d of length 1..8: 0 2 1 0 4 4 1 8
```

Because the graph has cycles through a (a b d a and a c d a, and a b c d
a), walks can loop, and the counts keep growing roughly exponentially. In
a DAG every power beyond the longest path length is the zero matrix,
since walks cannot loop. That gives a matrix test for acyclicity: G is a
DAG iff A^n = 0.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** reading A^k as "is there a path of length k?" and
treating entries as paths.

**Why it fails:** A^k counts walks, which may repeat vertices. A non-zero
entry for k = 5 might come only from walks that loop.

**The right way:** use the first k with a non-zero entry for distances
(the shortest walk is a path), and remember that later powers count walks,
not paths. Counting paths is a much harder problem.

### In real systems

- **Graph analytics** compute walk counts and related quantities with sparse matrix products; Katz centrality sums weighted walk counts of all lengths.
- **Markov chains** (chapter 20's random walks) use powers of a transition matrix, which is a weighted adjacency matrix: entries become probabilities.
- **Graph databases and GPU graph libraries** often expose graph algorithms as linear algebra over different "semirings", where replacing + and · with min and + turns walk counting into shortest paths.

## 9.4 Walk Relations

### In one sentence

Reachability is itself a relation: G⁺ relates u to v when a positive-length
walk goes from u to v, G* also includes length-0 walks, and both are built
by composing the edge relation with itself.

### The idea

When there is a walk from v to w, w is **reachable** from v, or v is
**connected** to w. Two walk relations capture this:

- **G⁺**, the positive walk relation: u G⁺ v iff there is a walk of length
  at least 1 from u to v.
- **G***, the walk relation: u G* v iff there is a walk of length at least
  0 from u to v. It is G⁺ plus every vertex related to itself.

#### Composition of relations

For relations S from A to B and R from B to C, the **composition** R ∘ S
relates a to c iff there is some b with a S b and b R c. This extends
function composition (chapter 4) to relations; the book keeps the same
right-to-left order, so R ∘ S means "first S, then R".

For a digraph G viewed as a relation, G ∘ G relates u to v iff there is a
walk of length exactly 2, and in general G^n (n-fold composition) is
"walk of length exactly n". So G⁺ is the union of G^n over n ≥ 1, and G*
is the union over n ≥ 0, where G^0 is the identity relation.

Composition of relations is the Boolean version of matrix multiplication:
replace "sum of products" with "OR of ANDs". The 0/1 matrix of R ∘ S is
the Boolean product of their matrices, which is why the walk-counting
matrix and the walk relation are so closely tied: (A^k)_uv is non-zero
exactly when u G^k v.

### Worked example

**Simple: the reachability matrix of the running example.** From the
distances computed in 9.3, every finite entry is a G* pair. From a we
reach a, b, c, d, e (a itself by length 0, and also by the closed walk a b
d a, so a G⁺ a holds too). From e we reach only e by a length-0 walk,
so e G* e but not e G⁺ e. This one example shows the difference: G⁺ has a
self-pair only for vertices on a cycle.

**Realistic: transitive closure in practice.** Computing G⁺ for all pairs
is the **transitive closure**. The classic algorithm (Warshall's) builds
it by allowing intermediate vertices one at a time: after round k,
reach[i][j] is true if some walk from i to j uses only vertices 1 to k in
its middle. It takes about n³ simple steps. The Dilworth test in 9.5's
code uses exactly this triple loop:

```text
for k, for i, for j: if reach[i][k] and reach[k][j] then reach[i][j] = true
```

(This line is pseudocode, not a runnable snippet.) Package managers
compute closures like this to answer "what does installing X pull in?",
and databases expose recursive queries (SQL's WITH RECURSIVE) to compute
it on stored edge tables.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** reading R ∘ S left to right, as "R first, then S".

**Why it fails:** the book, following function notation, means S first.
Mixing conventions gives the wrong relation when R and S are not
symmetric.

**The right way:** read R ∘ S as "R after S", like f(g(x)) for f ∘ g, and
write the middle element explicitly when in doubt: a S b and b R c.

### In real systems

- **SQL joins** compute relation composition; a recursive common table expression computes the transitive closure.
- **Access control:** "user can access resource" is often the composition of user-to-role and role-to-permission relations, and nested groups require a closure.
- **Build systems** compute the closure of the dependency relation to know what must be rebuilt when one file changes.

## 9.5 Directed Acyclic Graphs & Scheduling

### In one sentence

A digraph with no cycles describes a valid set of "must come before"
constraints; it can always be put in a single valid order, and the
longest chain decides how fast the tasks can finish in parallel.

### The idea

The book starts from course prerequisites: if s is a direct prerequisite
of t, you must complete everything u with u D⁺ t before t. A
prerequisite cycle would make graduation impossible, so the graph must be
a **directed acyclic graph (DAG)**: a digraph with no cycles (equivalently,
no positive-length closed walks).

#### Scheduling

A **topological sort** of a finite DAG is a list of all its vertices in
which each vertex comes before every vertex reachable from it. The book's
example is getting dressed: socks before shoes, shirt before tie, and so
on.

A vertex is **minimal** if no other vertex reaches it (nothing must come
before it) and **minimum** if it reaches every other vertex. The book
notes these words are easy to confuse: a DAG can have several minimal
vertices but at most one minimum.

**Theorem.** Every finite DAG has a topological sort.

**Proof idea.** A finite DAG has a minimal vertex: start anywhere and walk
backwards along edges; since there are no cycles and finitely many
vertices, the backward walk must stop, at a vertex with nothing before
it. Put it first, delete it, and repeat on the remaining DAG (still
acyclic). Induction on the number of vertices finishes it.

#### Parallel task scheduling

With unlimited processors, tasks that do not depend on each other can run
at the same time. A **parallel schedule** partitions the vertices into
blocks A0, A1, ... (step 0, step 1, ...) so that nothing in a later block
is needed by something in an earlier one. Its **time** is the number of
blocks, and the **number of processors** it needs is the size of the
largest block.

A **chain** is a set of vertices any two of which are comparable (one
reaches the other); think of it as a sequence of tasks that must happen
one after another. The **depth** of a vertex is the size of the largest
chain ending at it, minus one in the book's step numbering (so minimal
vertices have depth 0).

**Theorem.** Scheduling each vertex at step = its depth gives a
minimum-time schedule.

Every vertex is scheduled after all its predecessors (they have smaller
depth), so the schedule is valid. And no schedule can be faster: the
tasks of a largest chain must all go in different steps. So:

**Corollary.** Parallel time = size of the largest chain, the **critical
path**.

With a bounded number of processors things get harder, and the book
leaves that to problems; in general it is a hard optimisation problem.

#### Dilworth's lemma

An **antichain** is a set of vertices no two of which are comparable:
tasks that could all run at once. Each step of the depth schedule is an
antichain. If the largest chain has size t, the depth schedule
partitions the vertices into t antichains.

**Dilworth's lemma.** For every t at least 1, a DAG with n vertices has
either a chain of size greater than t or an antichain of size at least n
/ t.

**Proof.** If no chain is longer than t, the depth schedule uses at most t
steps, each an antichain, and together they hold all n vertices. If every
antichain had fewer than n / t vertices, the total would be less than t ·
(n / t) = n. So some antichain has at least n / t.

Setting t = √n: every DAG with n vertices has a chain longer than √n or
an antichain of size at least √n. Either many things must happen in
sequence, or many things are independent.

### Worked example

**Simple: topological sort of a deployment pipeline.** Edges say "u must
happen before v". Repeatedly remove a minimal vertex:

```ts
const deps: Record<string, string[]> = {
  'write code': ['unit tests', 'build image'], 'unit tests': ['merge'], 'build image': ['scan image', 'push image'],
  'scan image': ['merge'], 'merge': ['deploy staging'], 'push image': ['deploy staging'],
  'provision db': ['run migrations'], 'run migrations': ['deploy staging'], 'deploy staging': ['smoke test'], 'smoke test': ['deploy prod'], 'deploy prod': [],
};
const V = Object.keys(deps);
function topo(): string[] {
  const left = new Set(V), order: string[] = [];
  while (left.size) {
    const m = [...left].find((v) => ![...left].some((u) => deps[u].includes(v)));
    if (!m) throw new Error('cycle'); order.push(m); left.delete(m);
  }
  return order;
}
console.log('topological sort:', topo().join(' → '));
```

```text
topological sort: write code → unit tests → build image → scan image → merge → push image → provision db → run migrations → deploy staging → smoke test → deploy prod
```

(This snippet lives in its own file, so `V` here is the pipeline's
vertex list, not the earlier graph's.)

**Realistic: the minimum-time parallel schedule, and a cycle.** Compute
each task's depth in topological order, group by depth, then add a bad
edge:

```ts
const depth = new Map<string, number>();
for (const v of topo()) { const preds = V.filter((u) => deps[u].includes(v)); depth.set(v, preds.length ? 1 + Math.max(...preds.map((u) => depth.get(u)!)) : 0); }
const steps: string[][] = [];
for (const v of V) (steps[depth.get(v)!] ??= []).push(v);
steps.forEach((s, k) => console.log(`step ${k}: ${s.join(', ')}`));
console.log('parallel time', steps.length, ' processors needed', Math.max(...steps.map((s) => s.length)));
deps['deploy prod'] = ['write code'];
try { topo(); } catch (e) { console.log('with deploy prod → write code:', (e as Error).message); }
```

```text
step 0: write code, provision db
step 1: unit tests, build image, run migrations
step 2: scan image, push image
step 3: merge
step 4: deploy staging
step 5: smoke test
step 6: deploy prod
parallel time 7  processors needed 3
with deploy prod → write code: cycle
```

The pipeline needs 7 steps however many runners you have, because the
critical path has 7 tasks; three runners suffice to achieve it. Speeding
up a task off the critical path (unit tests, say) does not shorten the
pipeline at all; speeding up the image scan might. And once a cycle is
added, no minimal vertex exists and the sort correctly refuses.

**Dilworth on random DAGs.** For 200 random 12-vertex DAGs, compute the
largest chain t (by longest-path dynamic programming on the transitive
closure) and the largest antichain l (by brute force over all 4,096
subsets), and check l · t ≥ n:

```ts
let ok = true;
for (let trial = 0; trial < 200; trial++) {
  const n = 12; const adj = Array.from({ length: n }, (_, i) => Array.from({ length: n }, (_, j) => i < j && Math.random() < 0.2));
  for (let k = 0; k < n; k++) for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) for (let j = 0; j < n; j++) if (adj[i][k] && adj[k][j]) adj[i][j] = true; // transitive closure
  const chain = Array(n).fill(1); for (let j = 0; j < n; j++) for (let i = 0; i < j; i++) if (adj[i][j]) chain[j] = Math.max(chain[j], chain[i] + 1);
  const t = Math.max(...chain); let l = 0;
  for (let m = 1; m < 1 << n; m++) { const s = [...Array(n).keys()].filter((i) => (m >> i) & 1); if (s.length > l && s.every((i) => s.every((j) => !adj[i][j]))) l = s.length; }
  if (l * t < n) ok = false;
}
console.log('Dilworth bound (largest antichain × largest chain ≥ n) on 200 random 12-vertex DAGs:', ok);
```

```text
Dilworth bound (largest antichain × largest chain ≥ n) on 200 random 12-vertex DAGs: true
```

Edges only go from lower to higher index, so every generated graph is a
DAG by construction. The graphs are random, but the result is the same
on every run, because the lemma guarantees it.

**The wrong way:** adding more parallel workers to speed up a pipeline

### In real systems

- **Spreadsheets** recalculate cells in topological order of their formula dependencies and report circular references as errors.
- **Project management** uses the critical path method, named after exactly this corollary.
- **Data pipelines** (Airflow and similar) are literally called DAGs; the scheduler runs tasks whose predecessors have succeeded.
- **Package managers** install dependencies in topological order and fail on cycles.

## 9.6 Partial Orders

### In one sentence

A partial order is a relation that behaves like reachability in a DAG:
transitive, and either never relating an element to itself (strict) or
always doing so but never relating two different elements both ways
(weak).

### The idea

#### The properties of the walk relation in DAGs

The book extracts the properties of walk relations that make them
"order-like". A relation R on a set A is:

- **transitive** if a R b and b R c imply a R c;
- **reflexive** if a R a for every a;
- **irreflexive** if a R a for no a.

For any digraph, G⁺ and G* are transitive (merge the walks), and G* is
reflexive (length-0 walks). And G is a DAG iff G⁺ is irreflexive, since
u G⁺ u is exactly a positive-length closed walk.

#### Strict partial orders

A **strict partial order** is a relation that is transitive and
irreflexive. Examples: less than on numbers, proper subset on sets, "is
a proper prerequisite of" on courses.

**Theorem.** R is a strict partial order iff R is the positive walk
relation of some DAG. (Given R, use R itself as the DAG: it is acyclic
because a cycle plus transitivity would give a R a. Given a DAG, its G⁺
is transitive and irreflexive.)

A relation is **asymmetric** if a R b implies NOT b R a. For a DAG, G⁺ is
asymmetric (both directions would make a cycle), and the book derives
that a strict partial order can equally be defined as transitive and
asymmetric.

Different DAGs can have the same G⁺. The smallest one, keeping only the
edges u → v with nothing strictly between u and v, is the **covering
relation**, drawn as a **Hasse diagram**. For a finite strict partial
order it is unique.

#### Weak partial orders

A relation is **antisymmetric** if for all a different from b, a R b
implies NOT b R a: no two distinct elements are related both ways (an
element may be related to itself). A **weak partial order** is
transitive, antisymmetric and reflexive. Examples: ≤ on numbers, ⊆ on
sets, divisibility on the positive integers.

Weak and strict orders come in pairs: given a strict order, add the
self-pairs to get a weak one; given a weak order, remove them. And a
relation is a weak partial order iff it is the walk relation G* of some
DAG.

The book adds a warning about the word "antisymmetric": it is not the
opposite of symmetric. A relation can be both (equality is) or neither.

### Worked example

**Simple and realistic: checking properties mechanically.** A small
property checker over a finite set, tried on four relations on 1 to 12:

```ts
type Rel<T> = (a: T, b: T) => boolean;
const props = <T>(A: T[], R: Rel<T>) => {
  const all = (f: (a: T, b: T, c: T) => boolean) => A.every((a) => A.every((b) => A.every((c) => f(a, b, c))));
  return {
    refl: A.every((a) => R(a, a)), irrefl: A.every((a) => !R(a, a)),
    sym: all((a, b) => !R(a, b) || R(b, a)), antisym: all((a, b) => a === b || !(R(a, b) && R(b, a))),
    asym: all((a, b) => !(R(a, b) && R(b, a))), trans: all((a, b, c) => !(R(a, b) && R(b, c)) || R(a, c)),
  };
};

console.log('same rem mod 3: ', show(props(N, (a, b) => a % 3 === b % 3)));

```

```text
less than:       irrefl,antisym,asym,trans
same rem mod 3:  refl,sym,trans

```

Reading the results:

- Divisibility is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive: a weak partial order.
- Less than is irreflexive and transitive: a strict partial order (and, as the output shows, asymmetric and antisymmetric too, as every strict order is).
- Same remainder mod 3 is reflexive, symmetric and transitive: an equivalence relation (9.10).
- "Differ by at most 1" fails transitivity (1 relates to 2, 2 to 3, but not 1 to 3), so it is not an order or an equivalence. This "closeness" relation is a classic trap when people try to group values by similarity.

### Step by step

Why a strict partial order R is asymmetric.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** "antisymmetric means not symmetric".

**Why it fails:** equality is both symmetric and antisymmetric, and the
relation 1 R 2, 2 R 1, 1 R 3 on three elements is neither.

**The right way:** read antisymmetric as "distinct elements are never
related in both directions", and test it pair by pair.

### In real systems

- **Sorting comparators** must implement a strict weak ordering in most standard libraries; a comparator that is not transitive or not irreflexive can make sort routines return garbage or even crash.
- **Version constraints** (semantic versioning) define a partial order on releases, with pre-release tags making some versions incomparable in practice.
- **Happened-before** in distributed systems (Lamport's relation) is a strict partial order on events: two events on different machines with no message between them are incomparable, which is what "concurrent" means.

## 9.7 Representing Partial Orders by Set Containment

### In one sentence

Every partial order has exactly the same shape as some family of sets
ordered by inclusion: map each element to the set of elements below it.

### The idea

Two relations have the "same shape" when they are **isomorphic**: there is
a bijection f from A to B with a R a' iff f(a) S f(a'). The book shows
every weak partial order is isomorphic to ⊆ on a family of sets, using
the construction: send each a to the set of elements b with b ≤ a (its
"down-set").

- If a ≤ a', then everything below a is below a' (transitivity), so
  down(a) ⊆ down(a').
- If down(a) ⊆ down(a'), then since a is in down(a) (reflexivity), a is
  in down(a'), so a ≤ a'.
- The map is injective: if down(a) = down(a'), then a ≤ a' and a' ≤ a, so
  a = a' (antisymmetry).

Each of the three properties of a weak order is used exactly once. The
same trick with strict orders gives proper containment.

The value of the theorem is the picture: anything you can prove about
families of sets under ⊆ that only uses order properties holds for all
partial orders, and a confusing order can be visualised as nested sets.

### Worked example

**Simple: divisibility as containment.** Each number maps to its set of
divisors in the range:

```ts
const down = (a: number) => N.filter((d) => a % d === 0);
const subset = (X: number[], Y: number[]) => X.every((x) => Y.includes(x));
console.log('a | b iff down(a) ⊆ down(b) on 1..12:', N.every((a) => N.every((b) => (b % a === 0) === subset(down(a), down(b)))), ' down(12) =', JSON.stringify(down(12)));
```

```text
a | b iff down(a) ⊆ down(b) on 1..12: true  down(12) = [1,2,3,4,6,12]
```

**Realistic: permissions as down-sets.** In a role hierarchy (viewer below
editor below admin, with billing-admin incomparable to editor), represent
each role by the set of permissions it grants. "Role r is at most role s"
becomes "r's permission set is a subset of s's". The theorem guarantees
this representation is always possible for any hierarchy that is a
genuine partial order, and the implementation becomes a fast bit-set
subset test.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** representing elements by up-sets and down-sets
interchangeably without flipping the order.

**Why it fails:** with up-sets (elements above a), a ≤ a' iff up(a) ⊇
up(a'): the containment reverses.

**The right way:** pick one and state the direction; down-sets preserve
the order, up-sets reverse it.

### In real systems

- **Bit-set encodings of orders** (assign each element a bit vector of its down-set) are used to answer "is A a subtype of B?" in constant time in some compilers and type checkers.
- **Capability and role systems** are often implemented as permission sets with subset checks.
- **Lattice-based security models** (classification levels plus compartments) are partial orders represented by set containment of labels.

## 9.8 Linear Orders

### In one sentence

A linear (total) order is a partial order in which every two different
elements are comparable, like the numbers on a line.

### The idea

Elements a and b are **comparable** under R if a R b or b R a. A partial
order in which every two different elements are comparable is a **linear
order** (also called total order). ≤ and less than on the reals are
linear; ⊆ is not, nor is divisibility (2 and 3 are incomparable).

A topological sort of a finite DAG is exactly a linear order that
**extends** its partial order: whenever a comes before b in the partial
order, it comes before b in the list. Every finite partial order has such
a **linear extension**, by 9.5. Usually many: the count measures how much
freedom the order leaves.

### Worked example

**Simple: counting linear extensions.** How many ways can 1 to 6 be listed
so that every number comes after all its divisors?

```ts
const S = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]; let count = 0;
const extend = (left: number[]) => { if (!left.length) { count++; return; } for (const v of left) if (!left.some((u) => u !== v && v % u === 0)) extend(left.filter((x) => x !== v)); };
extend(S); console.log('topological sorts of divisibility on 1..6:', count);
```

```text
topological sorts of divisibility on 1..6: 25
```

Check by hand: 1 must come first. Among 2, 3, 4, 6, the constraints are
2 before 4, 2 before 6, 3 before 6, which allow five orders (2 3 4 6, 2 3
6 4, 2 4 3 6, 3 2 4 6, 3 2 6 4). 5 is unconstrained and can go in any of
five positions among them. 5 · 5 = 25.

**Realistic: why linear extensions matter.** A single-threaded executor
must pick one linear extension of the dependency order; different picks
give different but equally valid runs. Bugs that appear only under one
order (a test that passes when run alone but fails after another) are
signs of a hidden dependency the declared partial order does not
capture. Some test runners deliberately randomise the linear extension to
expose them.

### Step by step

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** assuming a sort with a partial-order comparator produces
"the" order.

**Why it fails:** incomparable elements may land in any relative order,
and many sorting algorithms assume totality, giving inconsistent results.

**The right way:** sort partial orders with a topological sort, or extend
the comparator to a total order with a deterministic tie-break.

### In real systems

- **Database ORDER BY** with ties returns rows in an unspecified order unless a unique column breaks the tie, which is exactly choosing a linear extension.
- **Lamport clocks** extend the happened-before partial order to a total order by breaking ties with process IDs.
- **Migration tools** apply schema migrations in a total order that extends their dependency order.

## 9.9 Product Orders

### In one sentence

Two orders combine into an order on pairs by comparing both components,
and the result is usually only partial even when both parts are linear.

### The idea

Given relations R1 on A1 and R2 on A2, the **product relation** on pairs
relates (a1, a2) to (b1, b2) iff a1 R1 b1 and a2 R2 b2. The book observes
that the product of partial orders is a partial order: reflexivity,
transitivity and antisymmetry each hold component by component. But
linearity does not carry over: ≤ on ℕ is linear, yet in the product order
on ℕ × ℕ, (1, 2) and (2, 1) are incomparable.

The book's example compares people by age and height: someone is
"smaller" only if younger and shorter, and many pairs are incomparable.

### Worked example

**Simple: pairs of version numbers.**

```ts
const pairs: [number, number][] = [[1, 2], [2, 1], [2, 3], [1, 1]];
const le = (p: [number, number], q: [number, number]) => p[0] <= q[0] && p[1] <= q[1];
console.log('(1,2) vs (2,1) comparable?', le(pairs[0], pairs[1]) || le(pairs[1], pairs[0]), ' (1,1) ≤ (2,3)?', le(pairs[3], pairs[2]));
```

```text
(1,2) vs (2,1) comparable? false  (1,1) ≤ (2,3)? true
```

**Realistic: Pareto fronts.** When comparing options on two measures, say
latency and cost for cloud instance types, the product order says one
option dominates another only if it is at least as good on both. The
minimal elements under this order (options not dominated by any other)
form the **Pareto front**, and the honest answer to "which is best?" is
usually "any point on the front, depending on what you value". Dilworth's
lemma even says something here: among n options, either there is a long
chain of strictly improving options or a large set of mutually
incomparable trade-offs.

### Step by step

<Trace columns={["property", "why it holds for the product"]} rows={[
  ["reflexive", "a1 R1 a1 and a2 R2 a2, so (a1, a2) relates to itself"],
  ["transitive", "chain each component: a1 R1 b1 R1 c1 and a2 R2 b2 R2 c2"],
  ["antisymmetric", "both directions in each component force a1 = b1 and a2 = b2"],
  ["linear?", "fails: (1, 2) and (2, 1) under ≤ × ≤"],
]} caption="The product inherits every property that is checked one component at a time, and loses linearity, which needs a choice between components." />

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** sorting multi-criteria results by the product order and
expecting a single ranking.

**Why it fails:** the product order is partial; there may be no single
best element.

**The right way:** use a lexicographic order (compare the first component,
break ties with the second), which is linear, or present the Pareto front.

### In real systems

- **Vector clocks** compare event timestamps with the product order on vectors of counters; incomparable vectors mean concurrent events.
- **Multi-objective optimisation** and benchmark reports present Pareto fronts.
- **Type systems:** subtyping of function types and tuples is defined component-wise, a product order (with a twist of direction for function arguments).

## 9.10 Equivalence Relations

### In one sentence

A relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive is an equivalence
relation, and it is the same thing as splitting the set into disjoint
blocks.

### The idea

A relation is **symmetric** if a R b implies b R a. An **equivalence
relation** is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Examples: equality,
congruence mod n (chapter 8), "has the same length as" on strings, "is
in the same connected component as" in an undirected graph.

The book's general recipe: for any function f, "f(a) = f(b)" is an
equivalence relation. In fact every equivalence relation has this form.

#### Equivalence classes

The **equivalence class** of a, written [a], is the set of all elements
related to a. So [7] under congruence mod 3 is the set of integers with
remainder 1. The key facts:

- every element is in its own class (reflexivity);
- if a R b then [a] = [b] (symmetry and transitivity);
- two classes are either equal or disjoint.

So the classes form a **partition** (9.5's definition: non-empty blocks,
each element in exactly one). Conversely, any partition defines an
equivalence relation, "in the same block". And taking f(a) = [a] shows
every equivalence relation is "same f-value" for some f.

]} />

### Worked example

**Simple: classes mod 3.**

```ts
const classes = new Map<number, number[]>(); for (const n of N) (classes.get(n % 3) ?? classes.set(n % 3, []).get(n % 3)!).push(n);
console.log('classes mod 3:', JSON.stringify([...classes.values()]));
```

```text
classes mod 3: [[1,4,7,10],[2,5,8,11],[3,6,9,12]]
```

The key function is n % 3; grouping by key is computing the partition.

**Realistic: deduplicating records.** Customer records are "the same
customer" if they share a normalised email. That is "same f-value", an
equivalence, and grouping by the normalised email partitions the records
cleanly. Now suppose the rule is "same email OR same phone". That
relation is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive: A shares an email
with B, B shares a phone with C, but A and C share nothing. To get
clusters you must take the transitive closure (9.4), which is what
union-find data structures do, and accept that A and C end up merged.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** grouping items by a similarity threshold ("within 5% of
each other") and expecting well-defined groups.

**Why it fails:** thresholds are not transitive; chains of small
differences link very different items, and the result depends on
processing order.

**The right way:** group by a key function (rounding to a bucket, a
canonical form), or explicitly take the transitive closure and accept its
consequences.

### In real systems

- **Hash maps** rely on an equivalence (the equals method) that must be consistent with the hash function: equal keys must have equal hashes.
- **Union-find** maintains a partition under merges, used in Kruskal's spanning tree algorithm and in type inference.
- **Canonicalisation:** URL normalisation, Unicode normalisation and case folding define equivalences by key functions.
- **Compiler optimisations** (global value numbering) partition expressions into classes known to compute the same value.

2. How many equivalence classes does congruence mod 5 have on the integers?

2. Five, one per remainder.

## 9.11 Summary of Relational Properties

### In one sentence

A handful of properties, each checkable pair by pair, classify the
relations that matter: orders, equivalences and their variants.

### The idea

The book closes with the catalogue. For a relation R on A:

- **reflexive:** every a has a R a. The walk relation of every digraph.
- **irreflexive:** no a has a R a. G⁺ of a DAG.
- **symmetric:** a R b implies b R a. Undirected graphs.
- **asymmetric:** a R b implies NOT b R a. Strict orders.
- **antisymmetric:** for distinct a, b, a R b implies NOT b R a. Weak orders.
- **transitive:** a R b and b R c imply a R c. Every walk relation.

And the classes:

- **strict partial order:** transitive and irreflexive (equivalently asymmetric);
- **weak partial order:** transitive, antisymmetric and reflexive;
- **linear order:** a partial order in which all distinct pairs are comparable;
- **equivalence relation:** reflexive, symmetric and transitive.

  ["weak partial order", "transitive, reflexive, antisymmetric"],
  ["linear order", "a partial order with all distinct pairs comparable"],
  ["equivalence relation", "transitive, reflexive, symmetric"],
  ["walk relation of a DAG", "a weak partial order"],

### Worked example

**Simple:** classify "is a substring of" on strings. Reflexive (every
string is a substring of itself), transitive, antisymmetric (two strings
that are substrings of each other have the same length, so are equal).
A weak partial order, not linear ("ab" and "ba" are incomparable).

**Realistic:** classify a comparator before trusting a sort. Suppose a
comparator for tasks says a is before b if a's priority is higher, or the
priorities are equal and a was created earlier. Irreflexive (a task is not
before itself), transitive (check the cases), and any two different tasks
are comparable if creation times are distinct. So it is a strict linear
order, and sorting with it is safe. If creation times can tie, it is only
a strict partial order (a strict weak ordering in library terms), which
sort functions also accept, but tied items may come out in either order.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** proving "reflexive, symmetric, transitive" by checking
a few examples.

**Why it fails:** each property is a for-all statement; examples can only
refute it.

**The right way:** prove each yes in general, find one counterexample for
each no, and use a brute-force checker like the one in 9.6 on small sets
to catch mistakes before writing the proof.

### In real systems

- **Property-based tests** for comparators and equality methods check exactly these laws (reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, antisymmetry) on random inputs.
- **Language specifications** state these laws as contracts: Java's equals and compareTo documentation, for example, requires them.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Directed graphs & Partial Orders">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working idea: a directed graph is a set of things with
arrows between them, and the same information is a relation, a set of
ordered pairs. A walk follows arrows; a path never revisits; a DAG has no
way to come back to where you started.

Try it: draw the dependency graph of five chores (laundry, dry, fold,
shop, cook) and list them in an order that respects every arrow. Then add
an arrow that creates a cycle and see why no order works.

The beginner's mistake is forgetting direction: treating "a reaches b" as
"b reaches a".

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

The working toolkit: adjacency lists for code, adjacency matrices for
counting (A^k counts length-k walks), composition for building
reachability, topological sort for ordering, and depth for parallel
schedules. The critical path is the longest chain and sets the minimum
parallel time.

Practise classifying relations: for each new relation, decide
reflexive, irreflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, asymmetric and
transitive, with a proof or a counterexample for each.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

The advanced view is that orders and DAGs are the same objects: strict
partial orders are positive walk relations of DAGs, weak ones are walk
relations, and any partial order is isomorphic to a family of sets under
inclusion. Dilworth's lemma gives a size trade-off between chains and
antichains, which is the theoretical basis of the "sequential versus
parallel" tension in scheduling.

Linear extensions connect it all: topological sorts are the linear orders
that extend a partial order, and counting or sampling them is a rich
problem in its own right.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **Self-loops:** an edge u → u is a cycle of length 1, so a graph with a self-loop is not a DAG.
- **Length-0 walks:** they make G* reflexive and are the reason G* and G⁺ differ.
- **The empty relation:** it is a strict partial order (vacuously transitive and irreflexive), symmetric and antisymmetric at once.
- **Infinite DAGs:** may have no minimal element (the integers under greater than), so the topological sort theorem needs finiteness.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **Parallel edges:** the book's digraphs have at most one edge per ordered pair; multigraphs need edge identities and walk counts change.
- **Ties in comparators:** a "strict weak ordering" with ties is a strict partial order whose incomparability is an equivalence; sorting is stable only if the algorithm guarantees it.
- **Non-transitive similarity:** "within distance d" is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive, so it is neither an equivalence nor an order.
- **Bounded processors:** with a fixed number of workers, minimum-time scheduling becomes a hard problem, and the depth schedule may not fit.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production code, the graph is usually implicit (imports, service
calls, build targets, table foreign keys), and the first job is to make
it explicit and check it is a DAG. Then topological order gives a safe
execution order, depths give the parallelism, and the critical path tells
you where optimisation pays. Cycle detection should report a short cycle
to the user, which is what the "shortest closed walk is a cycle" lemma
promises can be found.

For comparators and equality methods, test the relational laws: a broken
comparator or an equals that is not transitive produces bugs that look
random.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

Two habits. First, when something "must happen before" something else,
draw the DAG; most scheduling and build problems become obvious once
drawn. Second, before relying on a relation, ask which of the six
properties it has. A surprising number of bugs are a relation assumed to
be transitive or total that is not.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

When designing systems with dependencies (microservices, data pipelines,
module structures), keep the dependency graph acyclic on purpose: layered
architectures are linear extensions of a module order, and a cycle
between services makes independent deployment impossible. Use the
critical path to decide what to parallelise, and partial orders such as
vector clocks to reason about concurrency honestly instead of pretending
events are totally ordered.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

Long-term, this chapter feeds graph algorithms (BFS, DFS, shortest paths,
strongly connected components), concurrency theory (happened-before,
consistency models), databases (recursive queries, join as composition),
and type systems (subtyping orders). My plan is to keep the property
catalogue and the DAG scheduling theorems at hand, and revisit them when
studying distributed systems and compilers.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**Is a digraph really the same thing as a relation?** Yes, for digraphs
with at most one edge per ordered pair: the edge set is the set of
related pairs. The book keeps both vocabularies because each is
convenient in different places.

**What is the difference between minimal and minimum?** Minimal: nothing
is below it. Minimum: it is below everything else. A DAG can have many
minimal vertices but at most one minimum.

**Why does the book care about both strict and weak orders?** They carry
the same information, but some statements are cleaner in one form: "less
than" for strict, "less than or equal" for weak. Moving between them is
just adding or removing self-pairs.

**Is topological sort unique?** Only when the order is linear. Otherwise
there are many, and choosing among them is a design decision.

**Does the depth schedule minimise the number of processors too?** No. It
minimises time. It may use more processors at some steps than a schedule
that delays tasks off the critical path; balancing both is harder.

**Why is matrix multiplication related to walks?** Each entry of a matrix
product sums over a middle index, and a two-part walk is a choice of
middle vertex. The same structure is relation composition with OR and AND.

## Key takeaways

- A digraph is a binary relation on its vertices; in-degree and out-degree sums both equal the number of edges.
- The shortest walk between two vertices is a path, and distances satisfy the triangle inequality but are not symmetric.
- A^k counts walks of length k; the first k with a non-zero entry is the distance.
- Composition of relations is "first S, then R"; G⁺ and G* are unions of powers of G.
- Every finite DAG has a topological sort; the depth schedule is a minimum-time parallel schedule, whose time is the size of the largest chain.
- Dilworth: n vertices force a chain longer than t or an antichain of at least n / t.
- Strict partial orders are transitive and irreflexive; weak ones are transitive, antisymmetric and reflexive; they are the walk relations of DAGs.
- Every partial order is isomorphic to a family of sets under inclusion.
- Linear orders compare every pair; product orders usually do not.
- Equivalence relations, partitions and key functions are three views of the same thing.

## Glossary

- **Digraph:** a set of vertices with directed edges.
- **Tail, head:** the start and end vertex of a directed edge.
- **In-degree, out-degree:** the number of edges entering, leaving a vertex.
- **Walk:** a sequence of vertices joined by edges in the right direction.
- **Path:** a walk with no repeated vertex.
- **Cycle:** a positive-length closed walk with no repeats except the start/end.
- **Distance:** the length of a shortest path.
- **Adjacency matrix:** the 0/1 matrix of edges.
- **Composition R ∘ S:** a relates to c when a S b and b R c for some b.
- **G⁺, G*:** the positive walk relation and the walk relation.
- **DAG:** a directed acyclic graph.
- **Topological sort:** a listing of a DAG's vertices with every edge pointing forward.
- **Minimal, minimum:** nothing below it; below everything.
- **Chain, antichain:** pairwise comparable, pairwise incomparable sets of vertices.
- **Parallel schedule:** a partition of tasks into steps respecting dependencies.
- **Critical path:** a largest chain; it sets the minimum parallel time.
- **Dilworth's lemma:** a chain longer than t or an antichain of size at least n / t.
- **Reflexive, irreflexive, symmetric, asymmetric, antisymmetric, transitive:** the six relational properties.
- **Strict / weak partial order:** transitive and irreflexive / transitive, antisymmetric and reflexive.
- **Isomorphic:** related by a bijection that preserves the relation.
- **Linear order:** a partial order with all distinct pairs comparable.
- **Linear extension:** a linear order containing a given partial order.
- **Product order:** comparison of pairs component by component.
- **Equivalence relation, equivalence class, partition:** reflexive-symmetric-transitive relation; the set related to an element; disjoint blocks covering a set.
