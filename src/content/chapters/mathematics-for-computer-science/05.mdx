Induction is the proof method computer science uses most, because programs
loop and recurse, and loops and recursion are induction in disguise. This
chapter states the rule precisely, gives a template for writing induction
proofs that a reader can check, and shows the classic trap: a proof that
looks right and "proves" something false. It then generalises to strong
induction, compares both with the well ordering principle of chapter 2,
and ends with the most practical idea in Part I: modelling a program as a
state machine and proving it correct with an invariant, and proving it
stops with a decreasing quantity.

## Before you start

**After this chapter you will be able to:**

- State the induction principle and explain why it is valid, using the "chain of implications" picture.
- Write a clean induction proof with a named hypothesis, base case and inductive step.
- Strengthen an induction hypothesis when the plain version is too weak to carry the step.
- Find the broken step in a faulty induction proof.
- Use strong induction, and say when it is more convenient than ordinary induction or well ordering.
- Model a process as a state machine, find an invariant, and use the invariant principle to show a bad state is unreachable.
- Prove partial correctness and termination of a loop, using an invariant for the first and a strictly decreasing derived variable for the second.

## 5.1 Ordinary Induction

### In one sentence

If a property holds for 0, and holding for any n forces it to hold for n + 1,
then it holds for every nonnegative integer.

### The idea

Picture a line of people numbered 0, 1, 2 and so on, and two rules: the
first person gets a ticket, and anyone who gets a ticket passes one to the
next person. Does person 17 get a ticket? Person 0 does by the first rule,
so person 1 does by the second, so person 2 does, and after 17 uses of the
second rule, person 17 does. Nothing special about 17: everybody gets one.
That chain of "so" is all induction is.

#### A rule for ordinary induction

**The induction principle.** Let P be a predicate on the nonnegative
integers. If

- P(0) is true, and
- for every n in ℕ, P(n) IMPLIES P(n + 1),

then P(m) is true for every m in ℕ.

The second bullet is one statement about all n, not infinitely many
separate facts to check. You prove it once, for an arbitrary n, by assuming
P(n) and deriving P(n + 1). The first bullet is the **base case**, the
second is the **inductive step**, and the assumption P(n) inside the step
is the **induction hypothesis**.

Why is this valid? The book treats it as an axiom about ℕ, but it can be
reduced to well ordering: if P failed somewhere, there would be a least m
where it fails. m is not 0 (the base case holds), so m − 1 is in ℕ and P(m
− 1) holds (m was least), and then the step gives P(m), a contradiction.

    ^          ^          ^          ^                    ^

#### A familiar example

The sum of the integers from 1 to n is n(n + 1)/2. Chapter 2 proved this
with well ordering; here it is by induction. Let P(n) be the equation
1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n + 1)/2.

- **Base case:** P(0) says the empty sum (0) equals 0 · 1/2 = 0. True.
- **Inductive step:** assume P(n) for an arbitrary n. Then 1 + ... + n +
  (n + 1) equals n(n + 1)/2 + (n + 1) by the hypothesis, which is (n + 1)(n
  + 2)/2 after factoring out (n + 1). That is exactly P(n + 1).

So P(m) holds for all m.

#### A template for induction proofs

The book gives a fixed shape, and it is worth following every time:

1. **State that the proof uses induction.** The reader then knows what
   structure to expect.
2. **Define the induction hypothesis P(n).** Write the predicate out in
   full. Most bad induction proofs fail here, by never saying exactly what
   P(n) is.
3. **Prove the base case**, usually P(0) (sometimes P(1) or another
   starting value, in which case the conclusion starts there too).
4. **Prove the inductive step**: assume P(n) for an arbitrary n in the range
   and prove P(n + 1), stating where the hypothesis is used.
5. **Invoke induction**: conclude P(m) for all m from the starting value.

#### A clean writeup

A good writeup is short because the template carries the structure. The
book's point is that scratch work and the final proof are different
documents. In scratch work you poke at small cases and algebra until you
see why the step works; the writeup then states the hypothesis, does the
base case in one line, and does the step with each use of the hypothesis
marked. A reader should be able to check each line without reconstructing
your search.

#### A more challenging example

The book's harder example is a tiling puzzle. A square courtyard of side 2ⁿ
is divided into unit squares, and one of the central squares is reserved
for a statue. Can the rest be covered exactly by L-shaped tiles, each
covering three unit squares?

Try induction on n with P(n) as "a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ courtyard with the statue in a
central square can be tiled". The step fails: split the 2ⁿ⁺¹ courtyard into
four 2ⁿ quadrants, and the statue sits at the corner of one quadrant, not
at its centre, so the hypothesis does not apply to any quadrant.

The fix is surprising: prove something *stronger*. Let P(n) say "a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ
courtyard can be tiled with the statue in *any* square". Now the step works:

1. Split into four quadrants. The statue is in one of them; tile that one
   by the hypothesis.
2. Place one L-tile at the centre covering one corner square from each of
   the other three quadrants.
3. Each of those three quadrants now has one covered square, which plays
   the role of a statue. Tile each by the hypothesis.

The stronger statement is easier to prove because the hypothesis you get
to assume is stronger too. This is called **strengthening the induction
hypothesis**, and it is the most useful trick in this chapter.

#### A faulty induction proof

The book's warning example "proves" that all horses are the same colour,
by induction on the size of a set of horses. The base case (one horse) is
fine. The step takes n + 1 horses, removes the first and applies the
hypothesis to the remaining n, removes the last and applies it again, and
argues that the two overlapping groups force everyone to share a colour.

The bug is the overlap. When n + 1 = 2, removing the first leaves one horse
and removing the last leaves the other; the two groups share nobody, so
nothing links their colours. The step "P(1) implies P(2)" is false, so the
chain breaks at its first link, and everything after it is unsupported.
The lesson: the step must be valid for *every* n from the base case on,
including the smallest one. Check the smallest step by hand.

### Worked example

**Simple: test before you prove.** Before proving the sum formula, check it
on many values. A test cannot prove the claim, but it catches a wrong
formula in milliseconds:

```ts
let ok = true, s = 0;
for (let n = 0; n <= 1000; n++) { s += n; if (s !== (n * (n + 1)) / 2) ok = false; }
console.log('sum formula holds for n = 0..1000:', ok);
```

```text
sum formula holds for n = 0..1000: true
```

Notice the loop has the same shape as the induction proof. `s` starts at
the base case, and each iteration takes the sum for n − 1 to the sum for n.
The `if` is checking P(n) at each step.

**Realistic: the tiling proof as a program.** The strengthened proof is
also an algorithm. It recurses on quadrants exactly as the step does, and
places one tile at the centre for the three quadrants without the hole:

```ts
function tile(n: number, holeR: number, holeC: number): number[][] {
  const size = 1 << n; const g = Array.from({ length: size }, () => Array(size).fill(0));
  let id = 0;
  const rec = (r0: number, c0: number, sz: number, hr: number, hc: number) => {
    if (sz === 1) return;                         // base case: 1 × 1 is the hole itself
    const h = sz / 2; const t = ++id;             // the centre tile for this level
    const quads = [[r0, c0], [r0, c0 + h], [r0 + h, c0], [r0 + h, c0 + h]];
    const centre = [[r0 + h - 1, c0 + h - 1], [r0 + h - 1, c0 + h], [r0 + h, c0 + h - 1], [r0 + h, c0 + h]];
    quads.forEach(([qr, qc], i) => {
      const inside = hr >= qr && hr < qr + h && hc >= qc && hc < qc + h;
      if (inside) rec(qr, qc, h, hr, hc);         // hypothesis with the real hole
      else { const [cr, cc] = centre[i]; g[cr][cc] = t; rec(qr, qc, h, cr, cc); }
    });
  };
  rec(0, 0, size, holeR, holeC); g[holeR][holeC] = -1; return g;
}
const g = tile(3, 3, 4);
console.log(g.map((row) => row.map((v) => (v === -1 ? ' B' : String(v).padStart(2))).join(' ')).join('\n'));
console.log('tiles used:', new Set(g.flat().filter((v) => v > 0)).size, 'expected', (64 - 1) / 3);
```

```text
 3  3  4  4  8  8  9  9
 3  2  2  4  8  7  7  9
 5  2  6  6 10 10  7 11
 5  5  6  1  B 10 11 11
13 13 14  1  1 18 19 19
13 12 14 14 18 18 17 19
15 12 12 16 20 17 17 21
15 15 16 16 20 20 21 21
tiles used: 21 expected 21
```

B is the statue, at a central square of the 8 × 8 grid. Each number is
one L-tile; tile 1 is the centre tile placed at the top level, sitting in
the three quadrants that do not contain B. 63 free squares divided by 3
squares per tile is 21 tiles, and the program used exactly 21. The
recursive call is the induction hypothesis, and the reason the recursion is
correct is the reason the proof is correct.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** proving P(n + 1) by starting from P(n + 1) and
"reducing" it until you reach something true, or using the hypothesis
for a value it does not cover (as the horse proof does for n + 1 = 2).

**Why it fails:** an implication from the goal to something true proves
nothing about the goal (chapter 1's warning about working backwards). And a
step valid only for large n leaves a gap that breaks the chain.

**The right way:** in the step, start from the hypothesis and derive the
goal, or write a chain of equalities that are each reversible. Then test
the step by hand at the smallest n it must cover.

### In real systems

- **Loop correctness:** every proof that a loop computes the right thing is induction on the number of iterations, as the sum loop above shows.
- **Recursive algorithms:** divide-and-conquer code such as the tiling, merge sort and quick sort are correct because of an induction on input size, with the recursive call as the hypothesis.
- **Strengthening in practice:** when a recursive function needs extra parameters (an accumulator, a position, a "hole" location) to work, that is the code version of strengthening the hypothesis.
- **Proof assistants** such as Coq, Lean and Isabelle generate the induction principle for each data type automatically, and verified compilers rely on them.

## 5.2 Strong Induction

### In one sentence

Strong induction lets the step assume the property for every value from 0
up to n, not just for n, which makes it the natural tool when n + 1 breaks
into smaller pieces of arbitrary size.

### The idea

Ordinary induction lets you look one step back. Many problems need to look
further: a number factors into two smaller factors of unknown size, an
amount of money is a smaller amount plus a coin, a stack splits into two
smaller stacks. In each case you want the property for the smaller pieces,
whatever they are.

#### A rule for strong induction

**The strong induction principle.** Let P be a predicate on ℕ. If

- P(0) is true, and
- for every n in ℕ, P(0), P(1), ..., P(n) together IMPLY P(n + 1),

then P(m) is true for every m in ℕ.

The only change is the hypothesis: you may assume all earlier cases, not
only the last one. This is not more powerful in what it can prove (5.3
explains why), but it is often far more convenient.

  ["prove P(n + 1)", "prove P(n + 1)"],

  ["example: sum formula", "example: prime factorisation"],

#### Products of primes

Every integer greater than 1 is a product of primes. This was proved with
well ordering in chapter 2; strong induction gives a direct proof. Let P(n)
be "n is a product of primes", for n ≥ 2.

- **Base case:** 2 is prime, so it is a product of one prime.
- **Step:** assume P(k) for every k from 2 to n, and consider n + 1. If n
  + 1 is prime, it is a product of one prime. Otherwise n + 1 = a · b with a
  and b both between 2 and n. By the strong hypothesis, both are products
  of primes, so their product is too.

Ordinary induction would be stuck here: knowing that n is a product of
primes says nothing useful about n + 1, whose factors have nothing to do
with n.

#### Making change

The book imagines a country whose coins are worth 3 and 5 units, and shows
any amount of 8 or more can be paid exactly. Let P(n) be "n + 8 units can be
made from 3s and 5s".

- **Base cases:** 8 = 3 + 5; 9 = 3 + 3 + 3; 10 = 5 + 5.
- **Step:** for n + 1 ≥ 3, the amount (n + 1) + 8 is 3 more than (n − 2) +
  8, and n − 2 lies between 0 and n, so the strong hypothesis covers it.
  Add one 3-coin.

The step reaches back three places, which is why three base cases are
needed. Ordinary induction could only reach one place back.

#### The stacking game

Start with a stack of n boxes. A move splits one stack of a + b boxes into
stacks of a and b, scoring a · b points. The game ends when every stack has
one box. Which strategy scores most?

The answer is that strategy does not matter: every way of playing scores
exactly n(n − 1)/2. The proof is by strong induction on n. One box scores 0,
which matches 1 · 0/2. For n + 1 boxes, the first split into a and b scores
ab, and by the strong hypothesis the two resulting stacks score a(a − 1)/2
and b(b − 1)/2 however they are played. The total is ab + a(a − 1)/2 + b(b
− 1)/2, and with a + b = n + 1 this simplifies to (n + 1)n/2. The split sizes
a and b can be anything, which is exactly why the strong hypothesis is
needed.

### Worked example

**Simple: factorisation as a recursion.** The strong-induction proof is an
algorithm: if n has a factor, factor both parts recursively.

```ts
function factor(n: number): number[] {
  for (let d = 2; d * d <= n; d++) if (n % d === 0) return [...factor(d), ...factor(n / d)];
  return [n];
}
console.log([12, 97, 360, 1001, 2 ** 10].map((n) => `${n} = ${factor(n).join(' × ')}`).join('\n'));
```

```text
12 = 2 × 2 × 3
97 = 97
360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5
1001 = 7 × 11 × 13
1024 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2
```

The recursive calls are on d and n/d, both smaller than n but otherwise of
any size. That is the shape of a strong induction, and it is why the
function terminates and returns only primes.

**Realistic: change-making and the stacking game.** The change proof also
runs as code, with its three base cases and one step:

```ts
function change(n: number): number[] {
  if (n === 8) return [3, 5];
  if (n === 9) return [3, 3, 3];
  if (n === 10) return [5, 5];
  if (n < 8) throw new Error(`cannot make ${n}`);
  return [...change(n - 3), 3];
}
console.log(change(26).join('+'), '=', change(26).reduce((a, b) => a + b));
const bad: number[] = [];
for (let n = 1; n < 30; n++) {
  let can = false;
  for (let a = 0; 3 * a <= n; a++) if ((n - 3 * a) % 5 === 0) can = true;
  if (!can) bad.push(n);
}
console.log('amounts that cannot be made:', bad);
```

```text
3+5+3+3+3+3+3+3 = 26
amounts that cannot be made: [ 1, 2, 4, 7 ]
```

The brute-force check confirms that 7 is the largest impossible amount, so
the theorem's threshold of 8 is the best possible. For the stacking game,
play 10 boxes with three strategies: always halve, always peel one box off,
and 1,000 random games:

```ts
function play(n: number, pick: (h: number) => number): number {
  if (n === 1) return 0;
  const a = pick(n); const b = n - a;
  return a * b + play(a, pick) + play(b, pick);
}
const n = 10;
const rnd = new Set<number>();
for (let i = 0; i < 1000; i++) rnd.add(play(n, (h) => 1 + Math.floor(Math.random() * (h - 1))));
console.log(play(n, (h) => Math.floor(h / 2)), play(n, () => 1), [...rnd], (n * (n - 1)) / 2);
```

```text
45 45 [ 45 ] 45
```

Every game, including all 1,000 random ones, scores 45 = 10 · 9/2. The set
of random scores has a single element. A test like this is how you would
discover the theorem; the strong induction is how you know it holds for
every n and every strategy.

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** using strong induction with too few base cases. In the
change proof, someone proves only 8 and then says "n + 1 is n − 2 plus a
3-coin".

**Why it fails:** for 9 and 10, the amount 3 less is 6 or 7, which are below
8 and not covered (and in fact 7 is impossible). The step silently reaches
outside the range where the hypothesis holds.

**The right way:** check how far back the step reaches (here, 3), and make
sure every value in the first stretch is a base case or reaches back only
into proven values. A quick loop over small n catches this.

### In real systems

- **Recursion on sub-structures** such as divide and conquer, parsing nested expressions, and tree algorithms all recurse on pieces of unknown size, so their correctness proofs are strong inductions.
- **Dynamic programming** fills a table where entry n depends on several earlier entries. Its correctness is strong induction on n, and the base cases are the table's initial rows. The coin-change problem is the classic example.
- **Amortised analysis:** the stacking game's "every strategy costs the same" is the kind of invariant that potential-function arguments use to bound the total cost of a sequence of operations.

## 5.3 Strong Induction vs. Induction vs. Well Ordering

### In one sentence

Ordinary induction, strong induction and well ordering prove exactly the
same theorems, so the choice between them is about which proof reads most
clearly.

### The idea

Strong induction looks more powerful, but it can be turned into ordinary
induction by a trick: replace P(n) with Q(n) = "P(0) and P(1) and ... and
P(n)". An ordinary induction on Q has a hypothesis Q(n), which is all of
P(0) to P(n), exactly what strong induction would have assumed. So any
strong induction proof is an ordinary induction proof of a stronger
predicate.

Well ordering is the third face of the same fact. A well ordering proof
says "suppose there is a counterexample; take the least one; derive a
smaller counterexample or a contradiction". That is the contrapositive of a
strong induction step: if P holds below n, it holds at n.

<Layers caption="Three ways of saying the same thing about ℕ. Each can be derived from the others, so they prove the same theorems." layers={[
  { title: "Well ordering", body: "every nonempty set of naturals has a least element; argue from a least counterexample" },
  { title: "Strong induction", body: "if P holds for all values below n, it holds at n" },
  { title: "Ordinary induction", body: "if P holds at n, it holds at n + 1; strong induction is ordinary induction on 'P up to n'" },
]} />

The book's advice on choosing:

- Use **ordinary induction** when n + 1 is naturally built from n. It is the
  simplest and the most familiar to readers.
- Use **strong induction** when n + 1 breaks into smaller pieces of
  arbitrary size.
- Use **well ordering** when a minimal counterexample is the natural thing
  to think about, or when the proof is easier as a contradiction.

The reason to know all three is not logical power but communication. A
proof written in the frame that matches the problem is shorter and easier
to check.

### Worked example

**Simple: one claim, three proofs.** "Every amount of 8 or more is a sum of
3s and 5s."

- Strong induction: as in 5.2.
- Ordinary induction on Q(n) = "every amount from 8 to n + 8 can be made":
  the step uses the case three below, which Q includes.
- Well ordering: suppose some amount of at least 8 cannot be made, and take
  the least one, m. m is not 8, 9 or 10 (those can be made). So m − 3 is at
  least 8 and smaller than m, so it can be made; add a 3-coin to make m.
  Contradiction.

All three are correct, and the arithmetic in each is the same. The well
ordering version is perhaps the shortest; the strong induction version has
the clearest structure for a program.

**Realistic: matching proofs to code.** The recursive `change` function in
5.2 is the strong-induction proof. A bottom-up dynamic-programming version
that fills an array `canMake[0..n]` from left to right is the ordinary
induction on Q(n): at each step, the whole prefix of the array is the
hypothesis. A search for the smallest amount that fails, which returns
nothing because there is none, is the well ordering proof turned into a
test. One fact, three programs.

  ["4", "P(n + 1) holds", "the strong step, using line 3"],
  ["5", "Q(n + 1) = Q(n) AND P(n + 1) holds", "lines 3 and 4"],
  ["6", "Q(m) for all m, so P(m) for all m", "ordinary induction, and Q(m) includes P(m)"],
]} caption="The strong step is reused unchanged in line 4. Nothing new is proved; the bookkeeping just moves into the predicate." />

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** believing strong induction can prove things ordinary
induction cannot, and reaching for it by default "to be safe".

**Why it fails:** it does no harm logically, but it hides what the step
actually uses. A reader of a strong-induction proof has to check whether
the step reaches back one value or many, and what base cases that needs.

**The right way:** use the weakest frame that works. If the step only uses
P(n), say so with ordinary induction. Use strong induction when the step
genuinely uses earlier values.

### In real systems

- **Recursion versus iteration:** a recursive function and its iterative, table-filling version are the strong and ordinary forms of the same proof, which is why compilers and programmers can convert between them safely.
- **Minimal counterexamples in testing:** property-based testing tools shrink a failing input to a minimal one. That is the well ordering principle used constructively.
- **Termination proofs** for real programs use well ordering on a measure, as 5.4 shows.

## 5.4 State Machines

### In one sentence

Model a process as states and transitions, find a property that the start
state has and every transition preserves, and you have proved the property
for every state the process can ever reach.

### The idea

Many things we want to prove are about processes, not formulas: a counter
never exceeds its limit, a robot never reaches a certain square, a loop
returns the right answer, a protocol never deadlocks. State machines give
one model for all of them, and the invariant principle gives one proof
method.

#### States and transitions

A **state machine** is a binary relation on a set (chapter 4's idea again),
with new names: the elements are **states**, the relation is the
**transition relation**, each arrow q → r is a **transition**, and one
state is the **start state**. The machine can be deterministic (at most
one transition out of each state, so the relation is a function) or
nondeterministic (several choices).

The book's first example is a bounded counter: states 0 to 99 plus an
overflow state, a transition from each n to n + 1, from 99 to overflow, and
overflow stays put. Its state graph is a straight line ending in a loop.

An **execution** is a sequence of states starting at the start state where
each consecutive pair is a transition. A state is **reachable** if some
execution reaches it.

#### Invariant for a diagonally-moving robot

A robot starts at the origin of an infinite grid and each step moves
diagonally: x changes by 1 and y changes by 1, each up or down. Can it ever
reach (1, 0)?

Look at x + y. A diagonal move changes x by ±1 and y by ±1, so x + y
changes by −2, 0 or +2. Its parity never changes. At the start it is 0,
even. So every reachable state has x + y even, and (1, 0), with sum 1, is
unreachable. The property "x + y is even" is the **invariant**, and it
answers a question about infinitely many possible executions with a
two-line argument.

#### The invariant principle

A **preserved invariant** is a predicate P on states such that whenever P(q)
holds and q → r is a transition, P(r) holds. The book is careful with the
word "preserved": the property only has to survive transitions, not hold
everywhere.

**The invariant principle.** If a preserved invariant holds at the start
state, it holds at every reachable state.

This is induction on the number of steps. The base case is the start state;
the step is "preserved". The principle was formulated by Robert Floyd in
the 1960s, and it is the foundation of how programs are proved correct.

A subtle point the book makes: to show a property holds at all reachable
states, it is often easier to find a *stronger* preserved invariant than
to work with the property itself. "x ≠ 1 or y ≠ 0" is true at every
reachable state, but it is not preserved: the state (0, 1) satisfies it,
yet one diagonal move takes it to (1, 0). (0, 1) is itself unreachable,
but the predicate alone cannot tell you that. The parity invariant is stronger and preserved. This is the
state-machine version of strengthening the induction hypothesis.

#### The Die Hard example

In an action film, the heroes must measure exactly 4 gallons of water using
only a 3-gallon jug and a 5-gallon jug. The state is the pair (a, b) of
amounts in the two jugs, starting at (0, 0). Transitions: fill a jug,
empty a jug, or pour one into the other until the source is empty or the
destination is full.

With 3 and 5 it can be done, as the search below finds. The book then
changes the puzzle: jugs of 3 and 9. Now the invariant "both amounts are
multiples of 3" holds at (0, 0), and every transition preserves it
(filling gives 3 or 9, emptying gives 0, and pouring moves a multiple of 3
between amounts that are multiples of 3, with capacities that are
multiples of 3). So 4 gallons is unreachable, and no amount of trying will
find it. In general, the reachable amounts are multiples of the greatest
common divisor of the capacities, which chapter 8 proves.

#### Fast exponentiation

Robert Floyd split program correctness into two parts:

- **Partial correctness:** if the program stops, its answer is right.
- **Termination:** the program always stops.

The book's example is fast exponentiation, which computes aᵇ in about log₂
b multiplications. With registers x, y, z starting at a, 1, b, it repeats:
if z is 0, return y; otherwise let r be z mod 2, halve z (rounding down),
multiply y by x if r is 1, and square x.

**Partial correctness** comes from the invariant y · xᶻ = aᵇ. At the start it
reads 1 · aᵇ = aᵇ. If z is even, the step squares x and halves z, so xᶻ is
unchanged. If z is odd, z becomes (z − 1)/2, x becomes x² and y becomes xy,
and the product becomes xy · x^(z − 1) = y · xᶻ, unchanged. When the loop
stops, z = 0, so y · x⁰ = y = aᵇ.

**Termination** comes from z: it is a nonnegative integer and strictly
decreases at every step where z is positive (halving a positive integer and
rounding down makes it smaller). By well ordering it cannot decrease
forever, so the loop stops.

#### Derived variables

A **derived variable** is any function from states to numbers, such as x +
y for the robot or z for fast exponentiation. It is **strictly decreasing**
if every transition makes it smaller, and **weakly decreasing** if no
transition makes it larger.

**Termination theorem.** If a derived variable takes values in ℕ (or any
well ordered set) and is strictly decreasing, then every execution is
finite.

A weakly decreasing variable is not enough: the machine might take
infinitely many steps without changing it.

The book's stretch example shows why "well ordered set" and not just ℕ
matters. A robot lives at points (x, y) with x and y in ℕ. While not at the
origin it must move either one step west (if x is positive) or one step
south while jumping any distance east. It must get stuck at the origin,
but there is no bound on how long that takes, since every southward step
can jump arbitrarily far east. No ℕ-valued variable bounds it. The trick is
the derived variable v = y + x/(x + 1). Moving west lowers the fraction
part; moving south lowers y by 1, and the fraction, whatever x becomes,
stays below 1, so v still drops. Its values lie in a well ordered set of
fractions (chapter 2 showed such sets exist), so it cannot decrease forever.

### Worked example

**Simple: invariants by brute force.** Search the robot's states for six

**Realistic: the jugs, solved and refuted.** A breadth-first search over

```ts
function reachable(capA: number, capB: number) {
  const key = (a: number, b: number) => `${a},${b}`;
  const seen = new Map<string, string | null>([[key(0, 0), null]]); const q: P[] = [[0, 0]];
  while (q.length) {
    const [a, b] = q.shift()!;
    const pourAB = Math.min(a, capB - b), pourBA = Math.min(b, capA - a);
    const moves: P[] = [[capA, b], [a, capB], [0, b], [a, 0], [a - pourAB, b + pourAB], [a + pourBA, b - pourBA]];
    for (const [na, nb] of moves) if (!seen.has(key(na, nb))) { seen.set(key(na, nb), key(a, b)); q.push([na, nb]); }
  }
  return seen;
}
```

state space is finite, so the search alone is a proof; the invariant is

**Fast exponentiation with the invariant as an assertion.** Putting the
invariant into the loop as a runtime check turns the proof into a test:

```ts
function fastExp(a: number, b: number) {
  let x = a, y = 1, z = b, steps = 0;
  while (z !== 0) {
    if (y * x ** z !== a ** b) throw new Error('invariant broken');
    const r = z % 2; z = Math.floor(z / 2); if (r === 1) y = x * y; x = x * x; steps++;
  }
  return { y, steps };
}
console.log(fastExp(3, 13), 3 ** 13, fastExp(2, 30), fastExp(5, 0));
const bigExp = (a: bigint, b: bigint) => {
  let x = a, y = 1n, z = b;
  while (z > 0n) { if (z & 1n) y *= x; z >>= 1n; x *= x; }
  return y;
};
console.log(bigExp(7n, 100n) === 7n ** 100n);
```

```text
{ y: 1594323, steps: 4 } 1594323 { y: 1073741824, steps: 5 } { y: 1, steps: 0 }
true
```

Thirteen is 1101 in binary, so the loop runs 4 times, once per bit; 30 has
5 bits. The step count is the number of bits of b, not b itself. The BigInt
version computes 7¹⁰⁰ exactly, which is where the speed matters in
practice (modular exponentiation in RSA uses this loop with a `mod` after
each multiply).

### Common mistake

**The wrong way:** using as the invariant the property you want ("the
robot is never at (1, 0)", "the jug never holds 4") and trying to show it is
preserved.

**Why it fails:** the goal property is usually not preserved on its own:
there are states satisfying it that step into a bad state. Those states
happen to be unreachable, but proving that needs more information than the
goal contains.

**The right way:** look for a stronger property that rules out the bad
states and is preserved: parity for the robot, divisibility by 3 for the
jugs. Running a search on a small instance and looking at what all
reachable states have in common is the fastest way to find it.

### In real systems

- **Loop invariants** are how program verifiers (Dafny, Why3, Frama-C) and careful code reviews establish correctness. The fast exponentiation invariant is the textbook case.
- **Model checking** tools such as TLA+ and SPIN search the reachable states of a protocol model, exactly like the jug search, and check invariants such as "at most one leader" or "no lost writes" at each state.
- **Termination measures:** proof assistants and compilers that require total functions (Lean, Agda, Idris) ask for a decreasing measure, which is a derived variable.
- **Assertions and runtime checks:** putting the invariant in an `assert` inside a loop, as `fastExp` does, catches bugs the moment the invariant breaks rather than at the end.
- **Modular exponentiation** in RSA, Diffie-Hellman and signature schemes is the fast exponentiation loop with a reduction modulo n after each multiplication.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Induction">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working idea: to prove something for every n, prove it for 0
and prove that it carries from any n to n + 1. Write P(n) out in full
before anything else.

Try it on "2⁰ + 2¹ + ... + 2ⁿ = 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1". P(0) says 1 = 1. For the step,
add 2ⁿ⁺¹ to both sides of P(n): the right side becomes 2 · 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1 =
2ⁿ⁺² − 1, which is P(n + 1).

The beginner's mistake is skipping the hypothesis statement and then
assuming the thing being proved. If you cannot point to the exact line
where P(n) is used, the step is not a proof.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

The working skill is choosing the predicate. Many claims are not stated in
terms of n at all ("all horses", "every stack", "the robot never"), and the
first job is to find the number to induct on: the size of a set, the
height of a stack, the number of steps taken.

Then pick the frame. If n + 1 comes from n, use ordinary induction; if it
splits into pieces, use strong induction; if it is a process, use an
invariant. Write the five-part template every time until it is automatic.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

The advanced move is strengthening: when the step fails, prove something
stronger so that the hypothesis is stronger too. The tiling puzzle (hole
anywhere), the robot (parity rather than "not at (1, 0)"), and loop
invariants that carry an extra term (y · xᶻ rather than "y will be aᵇ") are
all the same move.

The second advanced skill is separating correctness from termination.
Prove each with its own tool: an invariant for "if it stops, the answer is
right", and a strictly decreasing measure in a well ordered set for "it
stops".

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Base cases at the edge of the range need care. If the theorem starts at n
= 1 or n = 8, the base case and the conclusion both start there. If the
step reaches back k values, k base cases are needed.

The smallest step is the most dangerous. The horse proof fails only at the
step from 1 to 2. Always run the step by hand at the smallest n it must
cover; most faulty induction proofs die there.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Nondeterministic machines need invariants preserved by *every* possible
transition, not only by the ones a sensible run would take. A protocol
proof that checks only the happy path proves nothing about the reordering
or retry that breaks it.

Termination over infinite branching is another corner. The far-east robot
has no bound on its running time, yet always stops. ℕ-valued measures
cannot prove that; a measure in a richer well ordered set can.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In real code, invariants belong in the code: as comments that state them,
as assertions in debug builds, and as properties in property-based tests.
A data structure's representation invariant (a heap's ordering, a balanced
tree's heights, a cache's size limit) is a preserved invariant of the
structure viewed as a state machine whose transitions are its operations.

Termination matters in production as timeouts, retry limits and bounded
queues. A retry loop with a counter that strictly decreases is a
termination proof you can read at a glance.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

Ask "what stays the same?" whenever a process changes things. The answer is
usually the key to proving something about the process, and finding it is
the creative part; checking it is mechanical.

Also: test, then prove. Run small cases, look at what all of them share,
and only then write the induction. The search finds the claim, and the proof
makes it certain.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

Distributed protocols are state machines with many nondeterministic
transitions (messages arriving in any order, nodes crashing). Their safety
properties are invariants, and designs such as Raft and Paxos are
presented together with the invariants that make them safe. TLA+ makes
this explicit: you write the state machine and the invariant, and the model
checker searches small instances for violations.

Designing with invariants first (what must always be true?) and
transitions second (what operations keep it true?) leads to systems that
are easier to reason about than designing operations first.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

Induction is the one proof technique I expect to use every week, mostly

is right, I am doing an induction. Making that explicit is what turns

## Questions people ask

**Is induction circular? It assumes P(n) to prove P(n + 1).** No. The step
proves an implication, "if P(n) then P(n + 1)", for an arbitrary n. It
never assumes P holds for all n. The base case then sets off the chain.

**Why is induction valid at all?** For the book, it is a basic property of
ℕ, equivalent to well ordering. Intuitively, every natural number is
reached from 0 by finitely many +1 steps, and the step covers each one.

**Do I always need P(0) as the base case?** No. Start wherever the claim
starts, and the conclusion then holds from that value on.

**When do I need strong induction instead of ordinary?** When the step needs
the claim at values other than n, typically because n + 1 splits into
smaller parts of unknown size.

**What is the difference between an invariant and a preserved invariant?**
The book uses "preserved invariant" for a predicate that every transition
preserves. Some texts use "invariant" for a predicate true at all
reachable states. A preserved invariant true at the start is the method
for proving the second kind.

**How does this relate to loop invariants in programming?** A loop is a
state machine whose transition is one iteration. A loop invariant is a
preserved invariant; checking it before the loop and after each iteration
is the invariant principle.

## Key takeaways

- Induction: prove P(0) and that P(n) implies P(n + 1) for arbitrary n; then P holds for all n.
- Always write the induction hypothesis out in full, and mark where the step uses it.
- When the step fails, try proving a stronger statement.
- Check the step at the smallest n it must cover; the horse paradox breaks there.
- Strong induction assumes all earlier cases; use it when n + 1 splits into arbitrary smaller pieces.
- Ordinary induction, strong induction and well ordering prove the same theorems; choose the clearest.
- A state machine is a set of states, a transition relation and a start state.
- Invariant principle: a preserved invariant true at the start is true at every reachable state.
- Partial correctness comes from an invariant; termination comes from a strictly decreasing measure in a well ordered set.
- Fast exponentiation takes a number of steps equal to the number of bits of the exponent.

## Glossary

- **Induction principle:** P(0) and (P(n) implies P(n + 1) for all n) together give P(m) for all m.
- **Base case:** the first value, proved directly.
- **Inductive step:** the proof that P(n) implies P(n + 1) for arbitrary n.
- **Induction hypothesis:** the assumption P(n) (or P(0) to P(n)) in the step.
- **Strengthening the hypothesis:** proving a stronger claim so the step has more to work with.
- **Strong induction:** induction where the step may assume every earlier case.
- **State machine:** states, a transition relation on them, and a start state.
- **Transition:** one arrow q → r of the transition relation.
- **Execution:** a sequence of states from the start, each step a transition.
- **Reachable state:** a state appearing in some execution.
- **Preserved invariant:** a predicate on states that every transition carries from q to r.
- **Invariant principle:** a preserved invariant true at the start holds at every reachable state.
- **Partial correctness:** if the process stops, its result is right.
- **Termination:** the process always stops.
- **Derived variable:** a function from states to numbers or another ordered set.
- **Strictly / weakly decreasing:** every transition makes it smaller / no transition makes it larger.
