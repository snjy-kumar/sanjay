Chapter 6 counted things directly: multiply choices, choose subsets, divide out symmetry. Many counting
problems resist that. The number of ways to climb stairs, the cost of merge sort, the number of ways to
make change, the number of onto functions: each is easier to describe by how it relates to smaller cases,
or by what it must exclude. This chapter gives the three big tools for those problems. Recurrence
relations describe a count in terms of smaller counts, and we learn to solve them in closed form.
Generating functions encode a whole sequence as one algebraic object. Inclusion–exclusion counts a union
by correcting for overlaps. Together they are the mathematics behind dynamic programming and the running
times of divide-and-conquer algorithms.

## 8.1 Applications of recurrence relations

### In one sentence

A recurrence relation expresses each term of a sequence through earlier terms, and many counting
problems, growth models and algorithms are described most naturally that way, which is also the basis of
dynamic programming.

### The idea

#### What a recurrence relation is

A **recurrence relation** for a sequence a0, a1, a2, ... is an equation that gives aₙ in terms of one or
more earlier terms, for all n from some point on. Together with **initial conditions**, the first few
terms, it determines the sequence completely. A **solution** is a sequence, often a formula in n, that
satisfies the recurrence and the initial conditions.

Chapter 5 met recursive definitions; this section is about building recurrences from problems. The
skill is the same each time: look at the last step, or the first, and see how a problem of size n reduces
to problems of smaller sizes.

#### Climbing stairs

How many ways are there to climb n stairs if each step goes up one stair or two? Look at the last step.
Either it was a single stair, after climbing n minus 1 stairs in any of the s(n minus 1) ways, or it was a
double, after climbing n minus 2 stairs in s(n minus 2) ways. These cases are disjoint and cover
everything, so s(n) equals s(n minus 1) plus s(n minus 2), with s(0) equal to 1 (the empty climb) and s(1)
equal to 1.

That is the Fibonacci recurrence, shifted by one. Node computes s(10) as 89 and s(20) as 10,946. The
count grows exponentially, which is why listing the climbs is hopeless and a recurrence is the only
sensible description.

#### Bit strings with no two consecutive 1s

How many bit strings of length n have no two adjacent 1s? Look at the last bit. If it is 0, the first n
minus 1 bits can be any valid string. If it is 1, the bit before it must be 0, and the first n minus 2
bits can be any valid string. So b(n) equals b(n minus 1) plus b(n minus 2), with b(1) equal to 2 and b(2)
equal to 3.

A brute-force loop over all 2ⁿ strings, testing whether m AND (m shifted right by 1) is zero, gives 2, 3,
5, 8, 13 for n from 1 to 5, and 144 for n equal to 10. Same recurrence, different starting values.
Recognising a familiar recurrence in a new problem is half the work of solving it.

#### The Tower of Hanoi

Move a stack of n discs from one peg to another, one disc at a time, never placing a larger disc on a
smaller. To move n discs, move the top n minus 1 to the spare peg, move the largest, then move the n minus
1 back on top. So H(n) equals 2H(n minus 1) plus 1, with H(1) equal to 1. Iterating gives 1, 3, 7, 15,
..., and H(n) equals 2ⁿ minus 1, which induction confirms. For 64 discs that is 18,446,744,073,709,551,615
moves.

The same "double plus one" shape appears whenever a task splits into two copies of a smaller task plus a
constant amount of work, such as the number of nodes in a perfect binary tree of height n.

#### Growth with payments

Recurrences model money too. A loan of 5,000 at 1 percent interest a month, repaid at 100 a month,
satisfies d(n) equal to 1.01·d(n minus 1) minus 100, with d(0) equal to 5,000. A loop finds the balance
first reaches zero in month 70. Section 8.2 solves this recurrence exactly: the balance is 10,000 minus
5,000·1.01ⁿ, which reaches zero when 1.01ⁿ equals 2, at n about 69.7.

#### Catalan numbers

How many ways can n pairs of parentheses be arranged so they balance? Any balanced string starts with an
opening parenthesis whose matching close splits the rest into a balanced string inside, using k pairs,
and a balanced string after, using n minus 1 minus k pairs. So Cₙ is the sum over k from 0 to n minus 1 of
Cₖ·Cₙ₋₁₋ₖ, with C0 equal to 1.

These are the **Catalan numbers**: 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, 1430, 4862, 16796. They also count binary
trees with n internal nodes, ways to parenthesise a product of n plus 1 factors, and ways to triangulate a
polygon. The closed form is C(2n, n)/(n plus 1), which generating functions can derive.

#### Dynamic programming

A recurrence that refers to the same smaller cases many times can be computed bottom-up, storing each
answer once. That is **dynamic programming**. The recursion tells you what to compute; the table tells
you in what order.

Counting ways to make an amount from coins is the standard example. Let w(v) be the number of ways to make
v from the coins considered so far. Processing coins one at a time, adding coin c updates w(v) by adding
w(v minus c), for v from c upward. With coins 1, 2 and 5, there are 10 ways to make 10. With coins 1, 5, 10
and 25, there are 242 ways to make 100. Processing coin by coin counts combinations rather than ordered
sequences, because each combination is built in one coin order.

Dynamic programming also finds optima. The fewest coins to make 12 from coins 1, 6 and 9 is 2 (6 plus 6).
The greedy rule, always take the largest coin that fits, picks 9 and then three 1s, using 4. A recurrence
over all last coins, m(v) equal to 1 plus the minimum of m(v minus c), considers every option and cannot
be fooled.

### Worked example

**Simple: the stairs recurrence, bottom-up.**

```ts
const stairs = (n: number) => {
  let a = 1, b = 1;               // s(0), s(1)
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) [a, b] = [b, a + b];
  return a;
};
console.log([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20].map(stairs));
```

```text
[ 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 89, 10946 ]
```

Two variables are enough because each term depends only on the previous two. A naive recursive version
recomputes the same values exponentially many times.

**Realistic: counting and minimising coins.**

```ts
function ways(t: number, coins: number[]) {
  const w = Array(t + 1).fill(0); w[0] = 1;
  for (const c of coins) for (let v = c; v <= t; v++) w[v] += w[v - c];
  return w[t];
}
function minCoins(t: number, coins: number[]) {
  const m = Array(t + 1).fill(Infinity); m[0] = 0;
  for (let v = 1; v <= t; v++)
    for (const c of coins) if (c <= v) m[v] = Math.min(m[v], m[v - c] + 1);
  return m[t];
}
console.log(ways(10, [1, 2, 5]), ways(100, [1, 5, 10, 25]), minCoins(12, [1, 6, 9]));
```

```text
10 242 2
```

needs big integers sooner.

### Common mistake

**Overlapping or missing cases.** The wrong way: counting stair climbs by "the first step is 1 or 2, or
the last step is 1 or 2", mixing two splits. Why it fails: the cases overlap and the count double-counts.
The fix is to split on one feature (the last step) into cases that are disjoint and exhaustive, and say
so.

**Wrong initial conditions.** The no-11 strings and the stairs share a recurrence but start differently;
using the wrong start shifts every answer. Check the recurrence's first few values against brute force.

**Ordered versus unordered in coin problems.** Swapping the loops, iterating amounts outside and coins
inside, counts ordered sequences of coins, so 1 plus 2 and 2 plus 1 count twice. Both are legitimate
questions; know which one you are answering.

### In real systems

Dynamic programming runs diff tools (longest common subsequence), spell checkers (edit distance),
routing (shortest paths in chapter 10), text layout (optimal line breaking), and query optimisers
(join ordering). Recurrences model resource growth: a queue's backlog, a cache's population, a loan, a
retry budget. Whenever code has "compute this from smaller versions of itself", a recurrence is the
specification and memoisation or a table is the implementation.

## 8.2 Solving linear recurrence relations

### In one sentence

A linear homogeneous recurrence with constant coefficients is solved by finding the roots of its
characteristic equation and combining powers of those roots, and a nonhomogeneous one adds a particular
solution to that general solution.

### The idea

#### Which recurrences this covers

A **linear homogeneous recurrence relation of degree k with constant coefficients** has the form aₙ equal
to c1aₙ₋₁ plus c2aₙ₋₂ plus ... plus cₖaₙ₋ₖ, with real constants c1 to cₖ and cₖ not zero. Each word
matters. **Linear**: earlier terms appear to the first power, not squared or multiplied together.
**Homogeneous**: no extra term that does not involve earlier aⱼ. **Constant coefficients**: the cᵢ do not
depend on n. **Degree k**: aₙ looks back k terms, so k initial conditions fix the sequence.

Fibonacci is degree 2. aₙ equal to 2aₙ₋₁ plus 1 is not homogeneous. aₙ equal to n·aₙ₋₁ does not have
constant coefficients. aₙ equal to aₙ₋₁² is not linear.

#### Guess a power

Try aₙ equal to rⁿ. Substituting into aₙ equal to c1aₙ₋₁ plus c2aₙ₋₂ and dividing by rⁿ⁻² gives r²
equal to c1r plus c2, the **characteristic equation**. Its solutions are the **characteristic roots**.
Any root r makes rⁿ a solution.

Because the recurrence is linear, any combination of solutions is also a solution. So if r1 and r2 are
distinct roots, every aₙ equal to α1r1ⁿ plus α2r2ⁿ satisfies the recurrence. The theorem says that every
solution has this form; the two constants are fixed by the two initial conditions.

#### Distinct roots, worked

Solve aₙ equal to aₙ₋₁ plus 6aₙ₋₂ with a0 equal to 3 and a1 equal to 4. The characteristic equation is r²
minus r minus 6 equal to 0, which factors as (r minus 3)(r plus 2), so the roots are 3 and minus 2.
Then aₙ equals α·3ⁿ plus β·(minus 2)ⁿ. From a0: α plus β equals 3. From a1: 3α minus 2β equals 4. Adding
twice the first, 5α equals 10, so α is 2 and β is 1.

The solution is aₙ equal to 2·3ⁿ plus (minus 2)ⁿ. Node computed the first eight terms both ways and got 3,
4, 22, 46, 178, 454, 1522, 4246 from each.

#### Fibonacci in closed form

For Fibonacci, r² equals r plus 1, with roots φ equal to (1 plus √5)/2 and ψ equal to (1 minus √5)/2.
Fitting f0 equal to 0 and f1 equal to 1 gives fₙ equal to (φⁿ minus ψⁿ)/√5, **Binet's formula**. Since
|ψ| is less than 1, ψⁿ/√5 is tiny and fₙ is the nearest integer to φⁿ/√5. For n equal to 30 both give
832,040.

This explains the growth seen in 8.1: the stairs count, the no-11 strings and Fibonacci all grow like φⁿ,
about 1.618ⁿ. The largest root in absolute value controls the growth rate of any such sequence.

#### Repeated roots

If the characteristic equation has a double root r, then rⁿ alone gives only one solution, and we need
two. The second is n·rⁿ. The general solution is (α plus βn)rⁿ.

Solve aₙ equal to 4aₙ₋₁ minus 4aₙ₋₂ with a0 equal to 1 and a1 equal to 6. The characteristic equation r²
minus 4r plus 4 is (r minus 2)², a double root 2. From a0, α is 1. From a1, 2(1 plus β) equals 6, so β is
2. The solution is aₙ equal to (1 plus 2n)2ⁿ: 1, 6, 20, 56, 144, ..., and Node agrees for eight terms.

In general, a root of multiplicity m contributes rⁿ, n·rⁿ, ..., nᵐ⁻¹rⁿ.

#### Higher degree

Degree 3 works the same way. For aₙ equal to 6aₙ₋₁ minus 11aₙ₋₂ plus 6aₙ₋₃, the characteristic equation
r³ minus 6r² plus 11r minus 6 factors as (r minus 1)(r minus 2)(r minus 3). With a0, a1, a2 equal to 2, 5,
15, solving three linear equations gives aₙ equal to 1 minus 2ⁿ plus 2·3ⁿ. Check n equal to 3: 1 minus 8
plus 54 is 47, matching the recurrence's next term.

Finding roots of high-degree polynomials can be hard; in practice, degree 2 and 3 cover most problems by
hand, and for anything larger a computer either finds roots numerically or simply iterates.

#### Nonhomogeneous recurrences

A **linear nonhomogeneous recurrence with constant coefficients** adds a term F(n) that depends only on n:
aₙ equal to c1aₙ₋₁ plus ... plus cₖaₙ₋ₖ plus F(n). Dropping F(n) gives the **associated homogeneous
recurrence**.

The key theorem: if pₙ is any one solution of the nonhomogeneous recurrence, a **particular solution**,
then every solution is pₙ plus a solution of the associated homogeneous recurrence. The difference of two
solutions of the full recurrence cancels F(n), so it solves the homogeneous one.

To find a particular solution, guess a form like F(n). If F(n) is a constant, try a constant. If F(n) is
a polynomial of degree d, try a polynomial of degree d. If F(n) is sⁿ, try c·sⁿ, unless s is a
characteristic root of multiplicity m, in which case try c·nᵐ·sⁿ.

#### Nonhomogeneous, worked

Solve aₙ equal to 2aₙ₋₁ plus 3 with a0 equal to 1. Try a constant c: c equals 2c plus 3, so c is minus 3.
The homogeneous part is α·2ⁿ. So aₙ is α·2ⁿ minus 3, and a0 equal to 1 gives α equal to 4. The answer is
4·2ⁿ minus 3: 1, 5, 13, 29, 61, ...

Solve aₙ equal to 3aₙ₋₁ plus 2ⁿ with a0 equal to 0. Since 2 is not a characteristic root (the root is 3),
try c·2ⁿ: c·2ⁿ equals 3c·2ⁿ⁻¹ plus 2ⁿ; dividing by 2ⁿ⁻¹, 2c equals 3c plus 2, so c is minus 2. Then aₙ is
α·3ⁿ minus 2·2ⁿ, and a0 equal to 0 gives α equal to 2. The answer is 2·3ⁿ minus 2ⁿ⁺¹: 0, 2, 10, 38, 130,
... Node agrees for eight terms.

The loan from 8.1 is the same shape: d(n) equals 1.01·d(n minus 1) minus 100. The constant particular
solution is c equal to 1.01c minus 100, so c is 10,000. Then d(n) is α·1.01ⁿ plus 10,000, and d(0) equal
to 5,000 gives α equal to minus 5,000.

### Worked example

**Simple: check a closed form against the recurrence.** The habit I use for every solution: compute the
recurrence directly and compare.

```ts
const terms = (rec: (a: number[], i: number) => number, init: number[], n: number) => {
  const a = [...init];
  for (let i = init.length; i < n; i++) a.push(rec(a, i));
  return a;
};
const r = terms((a, i) => a[i - 1] + 6 * a[i - 2], [3, 4], 8);
const f = [...Array(8).keys()].map(n => 2 * 3 ** n + (-2) ** n);
console.log(r.join(' '));
console.log(f.join(' '));
```

### Common mistake

**Fitting constants before adding the particular solution.** The wrong way: solve the homogeneous part,
fit α to a0, then add the particular solution. Why it fails: the sum no longer matches a0. The fix:
write the full general solution, homogeneous plus particular, and only then fit constants.

**Forgetting the resonance case.** If F(n) is 2ⁿ and 2 is also a characteristic root, the guess c·2ⁿ
gives 0 equal to something non-zero. Multiply the guess by n (or nᵐ for multiplicity m).

**Trusting the algebra without checking.** Always compute five terms from the recurrence and five from
the formula. Sign errors with negative roots, such as (minus 2)ⁿ, are especially common.

### In real systems

Closed forms let you reason about growth without simulating: a cache warming curve, a backlog under
constant arrivals, a compounding cost. Linear recurrences also appear in signal processing (IIR filters
are linear recurrences), pseudorandom generators (linear feedback shift registers), and population or
traffic models. The characteristic root with the largest absolute value tells you whether a system
grows, decays or oscillates, which is the same question control engineers ask about stability.

## 8.3 Divide-and-conquer algorithms and recurrence relations

### In one sentence

A divide-and-conquer algorithm splits a problem of size n into a subproblems of size n/b and combines
their answers with g(n) extra work, so its cost satisfies f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus g(n), and the master
theorem reads off the growth rate by comparing a with bᵈ when g(n) grows like nᵈ.

### The idea

#### Divide-and-conquer recurrences

A **divide-and-conquer algorithm** solves a problem by dividing it into smaller instances of the same
problem, solving those recursively, and combining the results. If each step makes a subproblems, each of
size n/b, and the dividing and combining cost g(n), then the total cost f(n) satisfies the
**divide-and-conquer recurrence** f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus g(n).

When n/b is not an integer, the subproblem sizes are floors and ceilings. The growth rate is the same,
so analyses usually assume n is a power of b and then extend.

#### Binary search

Binary search compares the target with the middle element and continues in one half. So a is 1, b is 2,
and g(n) is a constant (one or two comparisons): f(n) equals f(n/2) plus 2. Iterating gives f(n) about 2
log₂ n, so O(log n). A sorted list of a million items needs at most 20 probes, since 2²⁰ is just over a
million.

#### Merge sort

Merge sort splits the list in half, sorts each half recursively, and merges the sorted halves with at
most n minus 1 comparisons. So M(n) equals M(floor of n/2) plus M(ceiling of n/2) plus n minus 1, with
M(1) equal to 0. Computing the recurrence exactly gives 8,977 comparisons for 1,000 elements and about
18.95 million for a million; n log₂ n is 9,966 and about 19.93 million. The solution is Θ(n log n).

#### Fast multiplication

Multiplying two n-digit numbers by the school method costs n² digit products. **Karatsuba's
algorithm** splits each number into high and low halves, x equal to x1·B plus x0 and y equal to y1·B plus
y0, and notices that the middle coefficient x1y0 plus x0y1 equals (x1 plus x0)(y1 plus y0) minus x1y1
minus x0y0. So three half-size products suffice instead of four: K(n) equals 3K(n/2) plus O(n).

The solution is Θ(n^(log₂ 3)), about n^1.585. For 1,024-digit numbers, the recurrence counts 59,049
single-digit products against 1,048,576 for the school method; for a million digits the gap is more
than 300 times. The same "fewer subproblems" trick gives Strassen's matrix multiplication: 7 half-size
products instead of 8, so Θ(n^(log₂ 7)), about n^2.807.

#### Fast exponentiation

Computing bⁿ by repeated squaring uses bⁿ equal to (b^(n/2))² for even n, and b times that for odd n. So
P(n) equals P(n/2) plus at most 2 multiplications, giving O(log n). Computing 7 to the 1,000 mod 13 this
way takes 16 multiplications and gives 9. This is the modular exponentiation chapter 4 used for RSA.

#### The master theorem

For f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus c·nᵈ, with a at least 1, b greater than 1, c positive and d at least 0,
the **master theorem** says:

- if a is less than bᵈ, f(n) is O(nᵈ): the combining work at the top dominates;
- if a equals bᵈ, f(n) is O(nᵈ log n): every level does about the same work;
- if a is greater than bᵈ, f(n) is O(n^(log_b a)): the leaves dominate.

Check it against the examples. Binary search: a 1, b 2, d 0, so a equals bᵈ, giving O(log n). Merge sort:
a 2, b 2, d 1, equal again, giving O(n log n). Karatsuba: a 3, b 2, d 1, and 3 is greater than 2, giving
n^(log₂ 3).

#### Why the three cases

Draw the recursion tree. Level j has aʲ subproblems of size n/bʲ, each costing c(n/bʲ)ᵈ, so the level
costs c·nᵈ·(a/bᵈ)ʲ. The total is c·nᵈ times a geometric series in a/bᵈ with about log_b n terms. If the
ratio is less than 1, the series is bounded by a constant, and the top level dominates. If it is exactly
1, every level costs the same, and there are log_b n levels. If it exceeds 1, the last term dominates,
and it works out to n^(log_b a).

#### Closest pair and other examples

The closest pair of points in the plane can be found by sorting by x, splitting at the median, solving
each half, and checking a thin strip around the dividing line. A geometric argument shows each point in
the strip needs to be compared with only a constant number of others, so the combine step is O(n) and the
total is O(n log n), against O(n²) for checking every pair.

The maximum-sum contiguous subarray has a divide-and-conquer solution in O(n log n), splitting at the
middle and combining the best left, best right and best crossing sums. Kadane's linear scan, a dynamic
programming method, does better at O(n). On the array 3, minus 5, 4, minus 1, 2, 6, minus 8, 3, 1, both
find 11, from 4, minus 1, 2, 6. Divide and conquer is a strategy, not always the best one.

### Common mistake

## 8.4 Generating functions

### In one sentence

A generating function packages a whole sequence a0, a1, a2, ... as the coefficients of a power series,
so that counting problems become multiplying polynomials and recurrences become algebra on functions.

### The idea

#### Definition

The **generating function** for a sequence a0, a1, ..., aₖ, ... is G(x) equal to a0 plus a1x plus a2x²
plus ..., the sum of aₖxᵏ. We treat it as a formal object: x is a placeholder whose powers keep the terms
apart, and we rarely care what number x is.

The constant sequence 1, 1, 1, ... has generating function 1/(1 minus x), since 1 plus x plus x² plus ...
sums to that. The sequence C(n, 0), C(n, 1), ..., C(n, n) has generating function (1 plus x)ⁿ, by the
binomial theorem. The sequence 1, a, a², ... has 1/(1 minus ax).

#### Multiplying generating functions

The product of a0 plus a1x plus ... and b0 plus b1x plus ... has coefficient of xᵏ equal to the sum of
aᵢbₖ₋ᵢ over i. In counting terms: if aᵢ counts ways to pick i items of one kind and bⱼ counts ways to pick
j of another, the coefficient of xᵏ in the product counts ways to pick k items in total.

That is the whole trick. Each choice contributes a factor; the exponent records how much it contributes;
multiplying adds the exponents across all combinations.

#### Counting solutions with constraints

How many solutions does e1 plus e2 plus e3 equal to 10 have in integers with 2 ≤ e1 ≤ 5, 0 ≤ e2 ≤ 4 and
e3 at least 1? Each variable gets a factor listing its allowed values as exponents: (x² plus x³ plus x⁴
plus x⁵) for e1, (1 plus x plus x² plus x³ plus x⁴) for e2, and (x plus x² plus ...) for e3. The answer is
the coefficient of x¹⁰ in the product. Node multiplied the polynomials and got 20; a brute-force loop
over e1 and e2 also gets 20.

#### Making change again

The number of ways to make n from coins 1, 2 and 5 is the coefficient of xⁿ in 1/((1 minus x)(1 minus
x²)(1 minus x⁵)), because each coin can be used any number of times, and using the 2-coin k times
contributes x²ᵏ. The coefficients for n from 0 to 12 are 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13. The
value 10 for n equal to 10 matches the dynamic programming count in 8.1. In fact the DP loop is the
polynomial multiplication, done one coin at a time.

#### The extended binomial theorem

For any real u and non-negative integer k, the **extended binomial coefficient** C(u, k) is u(u minus
1)...(u minus k plus 1)/k!, and C(u, 0) is 1. The **extended binomial theorem** says (1 plus x)ᵘ is the sum
over k of C(u, k)xᵏ, for |x| less than 1.

For negative integers, C(minus n, k) equals (minus 1)ᵏ·C(n plus k minus 1, k). So (1 minus x)⁻ⁿ has
coefficient of xᵏ equal to C(n plus k minus 1, k), which is the stars-and-bars count from chapter 6:
choosing k items from n kinds with repetition. For example, the coefficient of x⁵ in (1 minus x)⁻³ is
C(7, 2), which is 21.

#### Partitions

A **partition** of n writes it as a sum of positive integers, ignoring order. The generating function
for partitions is the product over k of 1/(1 minus xᵏ). For partitions into distinct parts, it is the
product of (1 plus xᵏ); for partitions into odd parts, the product over odd k of 1/(1 minus xᵏ).

Node counted partitions of 10: 42 in total, 10 into distinct parts, and 10 into odd parts. The equality of
the last two holds for every n, and the generating function proof is two lines: (1 plus xᵏ) equals (1
minus x²ᵏ)/(1 minus xᵏ), and in the product the even factors cancel.

#### Solving recurrences

To solve aₖ equal to 3aₖ₋₁ with a0 equal to 2, let G(x) be the sum of aₖxᵏ. Then G(x) minus 3xG(x) equals
a0, since every other term cancels by the recurrence. So G(x) equals 2/(1 minus 3x), which expands to
the sum of 2·3ᵏxᵏ. Hence aₖ is 2·3ᵏ.

The same method handles Fibonacci: G(x) equals x/(1 minus x minus x²), and partial fractions recover
Binet's formula. For linear recurrences, generating functions and characteristic roots are two views of
the same algebra; the denominator of G is the characteristic polynomial with its coefficients reversed.

#### Proving identities

Generating functions prove identities by comparing coefficients. From (1 plus x)²ⁿ equal to ((1 plus
x)ⁿ)², the coefficient of xⁿ on the left is C(2n, n); on the right it is the sum of C(n, k)C(n, n minus k),
which is the sum of C(n, k)². So the sum of the squares of row n of Pascal's triangle is C(2n, n).

### Worked example

**Simple: multiply polynomials to count.**

```ts
function mul(a: number[], b: number[], N: number) {
  const c = Array(N + 1).fill(0);
  for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++)
    for (let j = 0; j < b.length && i + j <= N; j++) c[i + j] += a[i] * b[j];
  return c;
}
const N = 12;
const e1 = [0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1];          // x^2..x^5
const e2 = [1, 1, 1, 1, 1];             // 1..x^4
const e3 = [0, ...Array(N).fill(1)];    // x^1 and up
console.log(mul(mul(e1, e2, N), e3, N)[10]);
```

```text

```

Each array is a polynomial's coefficient list. Truncating at degree N is exact for the coefficients we
need, because higher powers can never come back down.

**Realistic: partitions of 10.**

```ts
function parts(n: number, allowed: (k: number) => boolean, distinct: boolean) {
  const w = Array(n + 1).fill(0); w[0] = 1;
  for (let k = 1; k <= n; k++) {
    if (!allowed(k)) continue;
    if (distinct) for (let v = n; v >= k; v--) w[v] += w[v - k];
    else for (let v = k; v <= n; v++) w[v] += w[v - k];
  }
  return w[n];
}

```


The distinct-parts loop runs downward so each part is used at most once, which is multiplication by (1
plus xᵏ); the upward loop allows repeats, which is multiplication by 1/(1 minus xᵏ).

extended binomial theorem, where |x| less than 1 justifies the algebra.

### In real systems

combinatorial enumeration in compilers and test generators (counting trees of a grammar up to a given
size, then sampling uniformly). Polynomial multiplication with the fast Fourier transform turns some
counting problems into O(n log n) computations, which is how large convolutions are done in practice.

## 8.5 Inclusion–exclusion

### In one sentence

To count the elements in a union of sets, add the sizes of the sets, subtract the sizes of all pairwise
intersections, add back the triple intersections, and keep alternating, so that every element is counted
exactly once.

### The idea

#### Two sets

Chapter 6's subtraction rule is the two-set case: |A ∪ B| equals |A| plus |B| minus |A ∩ B|. Adding |A|
and |B| counts the elements in both sets twice; subtracting the intersection fixes that.

Example: of 1,000 numbers from 1 to 1,000, 333 are divisible by 3 and 200 by 5. Those divisible by both,
that is by 15, number 66. So 333 plus 200 minus 66, which is 467, are divisible by 3 or 5.

#### Three sets

For three sets, |A ∪ B ∪ C| equals |A| plus |B| plus |C| minus |A ∩ B| minus |A ∩ C| minus |B ∩ C| plus
|A ∩ B ∩ C|. An element in exactly one set is counted once. An element in exactly two is counted twice and
subtracted once. An element in all three is counted three times, subtracted three times, and added back
once.

Extending the example to 7: the counts divisible by 3, 5 and 7 are 333, 200 and 142; by 15, 21 and 35 are
66, 47 and 28; by 105 is 9. So the union is 333 plus 200 plus 142 minus 66 minus 47 minus 28 plus 9, which
is 543. A loop over 1 to 1,000 agrees: 543 divisible by at least one, and 457 by none.

#### The general principle

For finite sets A1, ..., Aₙ, the size of the union is the sum of the |Aᵢ|, minus the sum of |Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ|
over pairs, plus the sum over triples, and so on, with the sign of each k-fold intersection term equal to
(minus 1)ᵏ⁺¹, ending with (minus 1)ⁿ⁺¹|A1 ∩ ... ∩ Aₙ|. With n sets there are 2ⁿ minus 1 terms.

#### Why every element is counted once

Take an element in exactly r of the sets, with r at least 1. It appears in C(r, 1) single sets, C(r, 2)
pairwise intersections, C(r, 3) triples, and so on. Its total count is C(r, 1) minus C(r, 2) plus C(r, 3)
minus ... plus or minus C(r, r). The binomial theorem with x equal to minus 1 says that C(r, 0) minus C(r,
1) plus C(r, 2) minus ... is (1 minus 1)ʳ, which is 0. So C(r, 1) minus C(r, 2) plus ... equals C(r, 0),
which is 1. An element in none of the sets contributes 0. That is the proof.

#### A survey count

Suppose a product team counts users of three features over a month: 120 used search, 90 used export, 70
used sharing; 40 used search and export, 30 search and sharing, 25 export and sharing, and 10 used all
three. The number who used at least one is 120 plus 90 plus 70 minus 40 minus 30 minus 25 plus 10, which is
195. If 500 users were active, 305 used none of the three, a number that matters more than any single
feature count.

The arithmetic is only as good as the intersections. Getting "used both" right requires counting distinct
users, not events, which is where real analytics pipelines go wrong.

#### Bounded variables

How many solutions does x1 plus x2 plus x3 equal to 12 have in non-negative integers each at most 5?
Without bounds, stars and bars gives C(14, 2), which is 91. Let Aᵢ be the solutions with xᵢ at least 6.
Substituting xᵢ' equal to xᵢ minus 6 leaves a sum of 6, so |Aᵢ| is C(8, 2), 28. Two variables at least 6
leave a sum of 0, one solution, so each pairwise intersection is 1. Three variables at least 6 would need
18, impossible.

So the bad solutions number 3·28 minus 3·1 plus 0, which is 81, and the good ones 91 minus 81, which is 10.
A double loop confirms 10. The generating function (1 plus x plus ... plus x⁵)³ gives the same answer as
its coefficient of x¹², which shows the two methods are two views of one count.

### Worked example

**Simple: divisibility by inclusion–exclusion and by brute force.**

subsets with a bitmask, multiplying the divisors in each subset and alternating the sign by the subset's
size.

```ts
function countDivisibleByAny(N: number, ds: number[]) {
  let total = 0;
  for (let mask = 1; mask < 1 << ds.length; mask++) {
    let prod = 1, bits = 0;
    ds.forEach((d, i) => { if (mask >> i & 1) { prod *= d; bits++; } });
    total += (bits % 2 ? 1 : -1) * Math.floor(N / prod);
  }
  return total;
}

```


divisors that share factors, the intersection is divisibility by the least common multiple.

## 8.6 Applications of inclusion–exclusion

### In one sentence

The complementary form of inclusion–exclusion counts the elements having none of a list of properties,
and that one formula counts primes with the sieve of Eratosthenes, onto functions, and derangements.

### The idea

#### The alternative form

Let P1, ..., Pₙ be properties an element of a set of size N may have, and let N(Pᵢ Pⱼ) be the number with
both Pᵢ and Pⱼ, and so on. The number with **none** of the properties, written N(P1' ... Pₙ'), is N minus
the sum of N(Pᵢ) plus the sum of N(PᵢPⱼ) minus ... plus (minus 1)ⁿN(P1...Pₙ). It is just N minus the size
of the union, so it is the same principle seen from the complement.

Most applications ask for "none of the bad things", which is why this form is the useful one.

#### The sieve of Eratosthenes

A composite number at most 100 has a prime factor at most √100, which is 10, so it is divisible by 2, 3, 5
or 7. The numbers from 2 to 100 that are divisible by none of these are exactly the primes greater than
7, plus nothing else. Inclusion–exclusion over the 15 non-empty subsets of 2, 3, 5, 7 counts 78 numbers
from 1 to 100 divisible by at least one of them. So 100 minus 78, which is 22, are divisible by none; one
of those is 1, which is not prime, leaving 21 primes from 11 to 97. Adding back 2, 3, 5 and 7 gives 25
primes up to 100.

The **sieve of Eratosthenes** is the algorithm version: cross out multiples of each prime up to √n, and the
survivors are prime. Inclusion–exclusion counts the survivors without listing them.

#### Euler's phi function

The number of integers from 1 to n that are coprime to n, written φ(n), follows from the same idea. If
the distinct primes dividing n are p1, ..., pₖ, inclusion–exclusion gives φ(n) equal to n times (1 minus
1/p1) times ... times (1 minus 1/pₖ). For 360, which is 2³·3²·5, φ is 360·(1/2)·(2/3)·(4/5), which is 96.
A gcd loop over 1 to 360 also finds 96. This is the φ used in RSA in chapter 4.

#### Onto functions

How many onto functions are there from a set of m elements to a set of n elements? Count all functions,
nᵐ, and subtract those missing at least one target. Let Pᵢ be "target i is never hit". Functions missing
a particular set of k targets map into the remaining n minus k, so there are (n minus k)ᵐ of them, and
there are C(n, k) ways to choose which k are missed. So the number of onto functions is the sum over k
from 0 to n of (minus 1)ᵏ·C(n, k)·(n minus k)ᵐ.

From 6 elements onto 3: 3⁶ minus 3·2⁶ plus 3·1⁶ minus 0, which is 729 minus 192 plus 3, which is 540. A
brute-force loop over all 729 functions counts 540 that hit every target. From 5 onto 3 the count is 150;
from 7 onto 4 it is 8,400.

In job terms: the ways to assign 6 distinct tasks to 3 distinct workers so that every worker gets at
least one task is 540. Dividing by 3! gives 90, the number of ways to split 6 tasks into 3 unlabelled
non-empty groups, a Stirling number of the second kind.

#### Derangements

A **derangement** is a permutation that leaves no element in its original place. Let Pᵢ be "element i is
fixed". Permutations fixing a particular set of k elements number (n minus k)!, and there are C(n, k)
such sets, so the number of derangements is Dₙ equal to the sum over k of (minus 1)ᵏ·C(n, k)·(n minus k)!,
which simplifies to n! times (1 minus 1/1! plus 1/2! minus ... plus (minus 1)ⁿ/n!).

The values are 1, 0, 1, 2, 9, 44, 265, 1854, 14833, 133496, 1334961 for n from 0 to 10. They also satisfy
Dₙ equal to (n minus 1)(Dₙ₋₁ plus Dₙ₋₂), which is how Node computed them.

The bracketed sum is the start of the series for e⁻¹. So the probability that a random permutation is a
derangement approaches 1/e, about 0.3679, and gets there very fast: for 6 elements it is already 0.3681,
and for 10 it agrees with 1/e to six decimals.

A secret gift exchange among 10 people, where names are drawn from a hat, gives nobody their own name only
about 37 percent of the time. Organisers who redraw until it works expect to draw about e, roughly 2.7,
times.

### Worked example

**Simple: onto functions by formula and brute force.**

```ts
const C = (n: number, k: number) => {
  let r = 1;
  for (let i = 0; i < k; i++) r = r * (n - i) / (i + 1);
  return Math.round(r);
};
const onto = (m: number, n: number) => {
  let t = 0;
  for (let k = 0; k <= n; k++) t += (k % 2 ? -1 : 1) * C(n, k) * (n - k) ** m;
  return t;
};
let brute = 0;
for (let x = 0; x < 3 ** 6; x++) {
  const hit = new Set<number>();
  let y = x;
  for (let i = 0; i < 6; i++) { hit.add(y % 3); y = Math.floor(y / 3); }
  if (hit.size === 3) brute++;
}
console.log(onto(6, 3), brute, onto(5, 3), onto(7, 4));
```

```text
540 540 150 8400
```

The brute-force loop reads each number x as six base-3 digits, one target per element, which enumerates
every function from 6 elements to 3 exactly once.

### Common mistake

**Using C(n, k)·(n minus k)ᵐ without the alternating sign.** The wrong way: counting onto functions as nᵐ
minus n(n minus 1)ᵐ. Why it fails: functions missing two targets are subtracted twice. The fix: carry
the alternating sum all the way to k equal to n.

**Forgetting the number 1 in the sieve count.** 1 is divisible by none of the small primes and is not
prime; leaving it in overcounts by one.

### In real systems

Onto-function counts give the number of ways a hash or scheduler can leave no bucket or worker empty,
which matters for load balancing analyses. Derangements model random reassignments (reviewers to papers
where nobody reviews their own, shuffled test fixtures where nothing stays in place). The sieve of
Eratosthenes, in segmented form, is still how prime tables are generated, and the probability form of
inclusion–exclusion bounds the chance that at least one of several failure events happens, which
reliability engineers use when events are not independent.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Advanced Counting Techniques">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version of this chapter is one habit: when a count is hard, ask how a problem of
size n relates to smaller ones. Look at the last step, the last bit, or the last coin, split into
disjoint cases, and write the recurrence. Then compute the first few values by brute force and check the
recurrence reproduces them.

Example: how many ways can you tile a 2-by-n strip with 1-by-2 dominoes? Look at the left edge. Either one
domino stands vertically, leaving a 2-by-(n minus 1) strip, or two lie horizontally stacked, leaving a
2-by-(n minus 2) strip. So t(n) equals t(n minus 1) plus t(n minus 2), with t(1) equal to 1 and t(2) equal
to 2: Fibonacci once more.

The beginner mistake is to write the recurrence and never check it. Two minutes of brute force for n up
to 6 catches wrong cases, wrong initial values and off-by-one shifts. Fix it by making "brute force agrees
for small n" part of the definition of done.

For inclusion–exclusion, the beginner version is the two-set rule, and the habit is to draw the Venn
diagram before writing a single number.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level the recurrences become algorithms. Dynamic programming is a recurrence plus a table, and
most DP problems in interviews and production code are two-dimensional: the answer for a prefix of one
input and a prefix of another.

Edit distance is the standard example. The fewest single-character insertions, deletions and
substitutions turning string a into string b satisfies d(i, j) equal to the minimum of d(i minus 1, j)
plus 1, d(i, j minus 1) plus 1, and d(i minus 1, j minus 1) plus 0 or 1, depending on whether the
characters match.

```ts
function ed(a: string, b: string) {
  const d = Array.from({ length: a.length + 1 }, (_, i) =>
    Array.from({ length: b.length + 1 }, (_, j) => (i ? (j ? 0 : i) : j)));
  for (let i = 1; i <= a.length; i++)
    for (let j = 1; j <= b.length; j++)
      d[i][j] = Math.min(d[i - 1][j] + 1, d[i][j - 1] + 1,
        d[i - 1][j - 1] + (a[i - 1] === b[j - 1] ? 0 : 1));
  return d[a.length][b.length];
}
console.log(ed('deploy', 'deplete'), ed('branch', 'brunch'), ed('commit', 'comet'));
```

```text
3 1 2
```

The intermediate mistake is recursing without memoising. A naive recursive Fibonacci for n equal to 30
makes 2,692,537 calls; the memoised version makes 59. Fix it by storing each subproblem's answer the first
time it is computed, or by filling a table in dependency order.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath, the chapter's methods are linear algebra. A linear recurrence of degree k is a k-by-k matrix
acting on a vector of the last k terms. Its characteristic roots are the matrix's eigenvalues, repeated
roots correspond to matrices that cannot be diagonalised, and the general solution is the matrix power
applied to the initial vector. That is why repeated squaring of the matrix computes the nth term in O(k³
log n) operations.

Generating functions are the same structure seen as rational functions: a linear recurrence's generating
function is a polynomial divided by the reversed characteristic polynomial, and partial fractions are
the eigen-decomposition in disguise.

The master theorem is a statement about geometric series, and the more general Akra–Bazzi method extends
it to unequal splits, such as f(n) equal to f(n/3) plus f(2n/3) plus n, which is Θ(n log n).

The advanced mistake is treating these as separate tricks. The fix is to solve one recurrence three
ways, by characteristic roots, by generating function and by matrix power, and watch them meet.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **Degree 0 and empty inputs.** Many recurrences need a value at n equal to 0 that seems meaningless,
  such as one way to climb zero stairs or one way to make zero change. Getting it wrong shifts the whole
  table.
- **Complex roots.** r² plus 1 equal to 0 has roots i and minus i; the solution is still real and
  oscillates with period 4. Combine the conjugate terms into cosines and sines.
- **Zero roots.** A characteristic root of 0 means cₖ is 0, so the recurrence really has lower degree.
- **Non-power-of-b sizes.** The master theorem's answer holds with floors and ceilings, but exact counts
  differ, as merge sort's 8,977 against 9,966 shows.
- **Empty intersections.** In inclusion–exclusion many high-order terms are zero; skipping them is
  correct, but only once you have shown they are zero.
- **Bounded variables with large bounds.** If a bound exceeds the total, it never binds and its property
  contributes nothing.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **Overflow.** Fibonacci passes 2⁵³ near n equal to 79, and Binet's formula rounded in doubles is first
  wrong at n equal to 71, by my check. Use BigInt or modular arithmetic.
- **Cancellation in alternating sums.** Inclusion–exclusion adds and subtracts huge numbers to get a small
  one; in floating point the result can be garbage. Use exact integers.
- **Recursion depth.** A top-down memoised DP on n equal to a million overflows the call stack in Node;
  bottom-up iteration does not.
- **Greedy that happens to work.** Greedy coin change is optimal for some coin systems, such as 1, 5, 10,
  25, and not others, such as 1, 6, 9. Testing only on the first set hides the bug.
- **Order of loops in DP.** Swapping loops changes combinations into sequences; both compile, and only
  one answers the question.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, recurrences describe how systems evolve, and solving them predicts behaviour. A queue
that loses 10 percent of its backlog each minute to processing and gains 50 new jobs has q(n) equal to
0.9q(n minus 1) plus 50. The particular solution is 500, so q(n) is 500(1 minus 0.9ⁿ) from empty: about
439 after 20 minutes, and never more than 500. A capacity plan reads that steady state straight off the
recurrence.

Retries are a recurrence too. If each of 4 service layers retries a failed call 3 times, one user request
can become 4⁴, which is 256, calls at the bottom layer during an outage. That multiplication is why retry
budgets and retries only at the edge are standard practice.

Divide-and-conquer costs show up in fan-out: a request that fans out to a shards, each of which fans out
again, has the same recursion tree as the master theorem, and tail latency grows with the number of
leaves. The production mistake is to analyse the average path only. The fix is to count the leaves.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is "describe, then solve". Most of the difficulty in a counting or cost problem is finding
the right description: the right last step, the right subproblem, the right properties to exclude. Once
it is described, solving is mechanical, and a computer can check the solution.

The second habit is to distrust closed forms you have not checked. I compute five terms from the
recurrence and five from the formula every time, because sign errors and index shifts are the normal
failure, not the exception.

The third is to ask what grows fastest. In a linear recurrence, the largest root; in a divide-and-conquer
recurrence, top work against leaf count. That one question is usually enough for engineering decisions.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In a larger system, these tools size things. Divide and conquer is the shape of distributed query
engines: split the data across machines, compute partial answers, combine. The master theorem's three
cases become three design questions. Is the combine step the bottleneck (a central coordinator merging
everything)? Is every level equally loaded (a balanced tree of aggregators)? Or do the leaves dominate
(many shards doing heavy scans)?

Concrete system: a search service with 64 shards, each answering in 20 milliseconds, and a merge step
that sorts 64 lists of 10 results. The leaves dominate the work, but the merge dominates nothing; tail
latency is set by the slowest of 64 leaves, which is why such systems hedge requests and use replicas.

Inclusion–exclusion sizes caches and audiences: how many distinct keys across three hot sets, how many
users in at least one segment. Getting intersections wrong misestimates memory or reach.

The system design mistake is to analyse one level in isolation. The fix is to draw the recursion tree
for the whole system and put a cost on each level.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

I learn this chapter by pairing every formula with code. For each recurrence, a brute-force counter, a
table-driven DP and a closed form, all printing the same numbers. For each inclusion–exclusion problem,
the formula and a brute-force loop.

Then I solve one recurrence by all three methods of the advanced level, and apply the master theorem to
every recursive algorithm I already know: binary search, merge sort, quicksort's best case, Karatsuba,
Strassen, tree traversals.

Done when I can turn a new counting problem into a recurrence in a few minutes, solve linear recurrences
of degree 2 by hand including repeated roots, apply the master theorem without notes, and count onto
functions and derangements from the alternative form on a blank page.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**is dynamic programming just memoised recursion?**

Top-down DP is exactly that. Bottom-up DP fills the same table in an order that respects dependencies,
which avoids recursion depth limits and often allows keeping only the last few rows.

**when should I solve a recurrence instead of just computing it?**

When you need growth rates, need values for huge n, or want to compare designs. For a specific small n,
just compute it.

**do I need generating functions if I know characteristic roots?**

For linear recurrences, no. Generating functions earn their place in constrained counting, partitions
and identities, where characteristic roots do not apply.

**does the master theorem cover quicksort?**

Only its best case, where splits are even. The average case needs the probabilistic analysis of chapter
7, and the worst case, f(n) equal to f(n minus 1) plus n, is quadratic.

**how is inclusion–exclusion related to probability?**

Dividing every count by the size of the sample space gives the probability form: p of a union of events
is the alternating sum of probabilities of intersections. It holds whether or not the events are
independent.

## Key takeaways

- A recurrence plus initial conditions defines a sequence; split on the last step into disjoint cases.
- Dynamic programming is a recurrence evaluated once per subproblem, in dependency order.
- Linear homogeneous recurrences with constant coefficients are solved by characteristic roots; repeated
  roots contribute n·rⁿ terms.
- Nonhomogeneous recurrences are a particular solution plus the homogeneous general solution; fit
  constants last.
- f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus c·nᵈ grows like nᵈ, nᵈ log n, or n^(log_b a), as a is less than, equal to
  or greater than bᵈ.
- A generating function turns sequences into power series; products count combined choices.
- Inclusion–exclusion counts unions by alternating sums of intersections; its complement form counts
  elements with none of a set of properties.
- Onto functions, primes by the sieve, Euler's φ and derangements are all the complement form.

## Glossary

- **Recurrence relation**: an equation giving aₙ from earlier terms.
- **Initial conditions**: the first terms, which fix the sequence.
- **Dynamic programming**: solving a recurrence by storing each subproblem's answer once.
- **Catalan numbers**: 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, ...; count balanced parentheses and binary trees.
- **Linear homogeneous recurrence with constant coefficients**: aₙ a fixed linear combination of the
  previous k terms.
- **Characteristic equation and roots**: rᵏ equal to c1rᵏ⁻¹ plus ... plus cₖ, and its solutions.
- **Binet's formula**: Fibonacci's closed form in terms of φ and ψ.
- **Nonhomogeneous recurrence**: a linear recurrence with an extra term F(n).
- **Particular solution**: any one solution of the nonhomogeneous recurrence.
- **Divide-and-conquer recurrence**: f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus g(n).
- **Master theorem**: the three-case growth rule for f(n) equal to a·f(n/b) plus c·nᵈ.
- **Karatsuba's algorithm**: multiplication with three half-size products.
- **Generating function**: the power series whose coefficients are a sequence.
- **Extended binomial coefficient**: C(u, k) for any real u.
- **Partition**: a way to write n as an unordered sum of positive integers.
- **Inclusion–exclusion**: the alternating sum for the size of a union.
- **Alternative form**: the count of elements with none of the given properties.
- **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: crossing out multiples of small primes to find primes.
- **Euler's φ function**: the count of integers up to n coprime to n.
- **Onto function count**: the alternating sum of C(n, k)(n minus k)ᵐ.
- **Derangement**: a permutation with no fixed points; Dₙ is about n!/e.
- **Stirling number of the second kind**: ways to split a set into a given number of non-empty unlabelled
  blocks.
