A graph is the simplest picture of connection: dots, and lines between some of them. That picture fits
an astonishing range of problems. Road maps, computer networks, social networks, call graphs, package
dependencies, state machines, timetables, circuit layouts and molecules are all graphs, and questions
about them (can I get there, what is the shortest way, can I visit everything once, how few colours
separate conflicts) are graph questions. This is the longest chapter of the book's middle, and for a
software developer one of the most directly useful. It builds the vocabulary, the representations, and
the classic results and algorithms: the handshake theorem, bipartite tests, Hall's marriage theorem,
connectivity and cut vertices, Euler's and Hamilton's tours, Dijkstra's shortest paths, Euler's formula
for planar graphs and graph colouring.

## 10.1 Graphs and graph models

### In one sentence

A graph is a set of vertices and a set of edges joining pairs of them, and choosing whether edges are
directed, whether parallel edges and loops are allowed, and what the vertices and edges stand for is the
modelling step that turns a real problem into a graph problem.

### The idea

#### The definition

A **graph** G equal to (V, E) consists of a non-empty set V of **vertices** (or nodes) and a set E of
**edges**, each edge having one or two vertices, its **endpoints**. An edge **connects** its endpoints.
We consider finite graphs, where V and E are finite.

The definition is about structure, not pictures. A drawing is just one way to show the structure; the
same graph can be drawn in many ways, and much of this chapter is about which properties survive any
redrawing.

#### Kinds of graph

The basic choices are whether edges have direction and whether repeats are allowed.

- A **simple graph** has undirected edges, no two edges joining the same pair, and no loops. A friendship
  network is simple.
- A **multigraph** allows several edges, called multiple or parallel edges, between the same pair. Two
  cities joined by three separate fibre links form a multigraph.
- A **pseudograph** also allows **loops**, edges from a vertex to itself.
- A **directed graph**, or digraph, has directed edges, each an ordered pair (u, v) from its **initial
  vertex** u to its **terminal vertex** v. "Follows" on a social network is directed.
- A **directed multigraph** allows parallel directed edges, such as several flights from one airport to
  another.
- A **mixed graph** has both kinds, such as a street map with one-way and two-way streets.

#### Graph models

A **graph model** chooses what the vertices and edges represent so that the question becomes a question
about the graph. A good model throws away everything irrelevant and keeps exactly the structure the
question needs.

- **Computer networks**: vertices are machines or routers, edges are links. Multiple links give a
  multigraph; one-way links a digraph.
- **Social networks**: vertices are people; edges are friendships (undirected), follows (directed) or
  messages (a directed multigraph, one edge per message).
- **The web**: vertices are pages, directed edges are links. Ranking algorithms, such as PageRank, work on
  this directed graph.
- **Call graphs**: vertices are functions, a directed edge from f to g means f calls g. A cycle means
  recursion.
- **Dependency graphs**: vertices are packages or tasks, edges are "depends on". A topological sort
  (chapter 9) gives a build order.
- **Road networks**: vertices are intersections, edges are road segments, weighted by length or travel
  time.
- **Tournaments**: vertices are teams, a directed edge from a to b means a beat b, one edge per pair.
- **Niche overlap and conflict graphs**: an edge joins two things that compete or conflict, such as two
  exams with a shared student, which is the start of graph colouring.

#### Weights and labels

Many models attach numbers to edges: distance, latency, cost, capacity. A graph with such numbers is
**weighted**. Section 10.6 uses weights for shortest paths; chapter 11 uses them for minimum spanning
trees. Vertices can carry labels too, such as a machine's region or a person's role.

### Worked example

**Simple: modelling service calls.** Five services: gateway calls auth and orders; orders calls payments
and inventory; payments calls auth. Model them as a directed graph with an edge from caller to callee.

```ts
const calls: Record<string, string[]> = {
  gateway: ['auth', 'orders'], orders: ['payments', 'inventory'],
  payments: ['auth'], auth: [], inventory: [],
};
const edges = Object.values(calls).reduce((s, xs) => s + xs.length, 0);
const callers = (v: string) => Object.keys(calls).filter(u => calls[u].includes(v));
console.log(edges, callers('auth'));
```

```text
5 [ 'gateway', 'payments' ]
```

Already the model answers questions: auth has two callers, so an auth outage affects both; inventory and
auth have no outgoing edges, so they are leaves of the call graph.

**Realistic: choosing the model for a question.** For "which flights connect two airports", a directed
multigraph keeps every flight. For "is there any route", a simple directed graph is enough: collapse
parallel flights into one edge. For "what is the cheapest route", a weighted simple directed graph with
the cheapest fare per pair is enough. Same data, three graphs, each the smallest that answers its
question.

### Common mistake

**Losing information by choosing too simple a graph.** The wrong way: modelling message traffic as a
simple undirected graph, then asking who sends the most messages. Why it fails: direction and multiplicity
were thrown away. The fix: decide the questions first, then pick the kind of graph that keeps what they
need.

**Mistaking the drawing for the graph.** Two drawings that look different can be the same graph, and edges
that cross in a drawing do not meet at a vertex.

### In real systems

Graphs are the data model behind network routing, service meshes, social features (people you may know is
a graph query), recommendation (users and items as a bipartite graph), fraud detection (rings of
accounts), knowledge graphs, build systems, garbage collectors (reachability from roots), and compilers
(control-flow graphs). Graph databases store them natively, and most relational systems hold them as edge
tables.

## 10.2 Graph terminology and special types of graphs

### In one sentence

The degree of a vertex counts its edges, the degrees always sum to twice the number of edges, and a few
special families (complete graphs, cycles, wheels, cubes and bipartite graphs) recur so often that they
have names, with bipartite graphs supporting the theory of matchings.

### The idea

#### Adjacency and degree

Two vertices of an undirected graph are **adjacent**, or neighbours, if an edge joins them; the edge is
**incident** with them. The **neighbourhood** N(v) is the set of neighbours of v. The **degree** deg(v) is
the number of edges incident with v, with a loop counting twice. A vertex of degree 0 is **isolated**; one
of degree 1 is **pendant**.

In the graph with edges a–b, a–c, b–c, b–d, c–e, d–e, d–f and e–f, the degrees are 2, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2 for a
to f.

#### The handshake theorem

In any undirected graph with m edges, the sum of the degrees is 2m. Each edge has two ends, and each end
adds one to some vertex's degree. In the example, the degrees sum to 16 and there are 8 edges.

A consequence: **every undirected graph has an even number of vertices of odd degree**. The sum of all
degrees is even; the even degrees sum to something even; so the odd degrees must also sum to something
even, which needs an even number of them. This is why a party cannot have exactly one person who shook an
odd number of hands.

#### Directed degree

In a directed graph, the **in-degree** deg⁻(v) counts edges ending at v and the **out-degree** deg⁺(v)
counts edges starting at v. Each edge adds one to an out-degree and one to an in-degree, so the in-degrees
and out-degrees each sum to the number of edges. Removing directions gives the **underlying undirected
graph**.

#### Degree sequences

The **degree sequence** lists the degrees in non-increasing order. Not every sequence is a degree
sequence of a simple graph: 3, 3, 3, 1 fails, since the handshake sum is fine (10) but the vertex of degree
1 can be adjacent to only one of the three degree-3 vertices, each of which needs all three others.

The Havel–Hakimi test decides it: remove the largest degree d, subtract 1 from the next d degrees, and
repeat; the sequence is graphic exactly if you reach all zeros without a negative. Node finds 3, 3, 2, 2,
2 graphic, and 3, 3, 3, 1 and 4, 4, 4, 1, 1 not.

#### Special simple graphs

- **Complete graph Kₙ**: every pair of n vertices adjacent; n(n minus 1)/2 edges, so 45 for K10.
- **Cycle Cₙ**, for n at least 3: vertices in a ring, each adjacent to the next; n edges, all degrees 2.
- **Wheel Wₙ**: Cₙ plus a hub adjacent to every cycle vertex; 2n edges.
- **n-cube Qₙ**: vertices are the 2ⁿ bit strings of length n, adjacent when they differ in one bit; n·2ⁿ⁻¹
  edges, so 32 for Q4. Hypercube networks connect processors this way, so any two are at most n hops
  apart.

#### Bipartite graphs

A simple graph is **bipartite** if its vertices split into two sets V1 and V2 so that every edge joins a
vertex of V1 to one of V2. Users and products with "bought" edges; students and courses; jobs and
machines: all bipartite.

The **complete bipartite graph** K(m, n), usually written K_m,n, has all m·n edges between the parts;
K3,4 has 12.

**Theorem**: a graph is bipartite exactly when its vertices can be coloured with two colours so that no
edge joins two vertices of the same colour, and exactly when it has no cycle of odd length. A
breadth-first search tests it: colour the start 0, its neighbours 1, their neighbours 0, and so on; a
conflict means an odd cycle. The example graph above is not bipartite, because the search finds b and c,
adjacent, with the same colour: a, b, c is a triangle. The cycle C6 is bipartite, with colours
alternating.

#### Matchings

A **matching** in a graph is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex. In a bipartite graph of
people and tasks, a matching assigns tasks to people, at most one each. A **maximum matching** has as many
edges as possible. A matching of a bipartite graph with parts V1 and V2 is **complete from V1 to V2** if
every vertex of V1 is matched.

**Hall's marriage theorem**: a bipartite graph has a complete matching from V1 to V2 if and only if every
subset A of V1 has at least |A| neighbours in V2. The condition is obviously necessary: if some group of
people can do fewer tasks between them than there are people, they cannot all be matched. Hall's theorem
says it is also sufficient.

Example: Asha can do api or db; Ravi only api; Meera db or ui; Kiran ui or ops. Every group of k people
can do at least k tasks between them, so a complete matching exists. The augmenting-path algorithm finds
one: Ravi api, Asha db, Meera ui, Kiran ops. When Ravi arrives after Asha has taken api, the algorithm
moves Asha to db, the augmenting step.

#### Subgraphs and new graphs from old

A **subgraph** of G uses some of its vertices and edges; it is **proper** if it is not G itself. The
**subgraph induced** by a vertex set W keeps W and every edge of G between vertices of W. Removing a vertex
removes its edges too; removing an edge keeps its endpoints. The **union** of two graphs takes the union
of vertex sets and of edge sets.

### Worked example

**Simple: degrees and the handshake theorem.**

```ts
const E: [string, string][] = [['a','b'],['a','c'],['b','c'],['b','d'],['c','e'],['d','e'],['d','f'],['e','f']];

for (const [u, v] of E) { deg[u] = (deg[u] ?? 0) + 1; deg[v] = (deg[v] ?? 0) + 1; }

```


Four vertices have odd degree, an even number, as the theorem requires.

**Realistic: a bipartite test with breadth-first search.**

```ts
function bipartite(adj: Record<string, string[]>) {
  const colour: Record<string, number> = {};
  for (const s of Object.keys(adj)) {
    if (s in colour) continue;
    colour[s] = 0;
    const queue = [s];
    while (queue.length) {
      const u = queue.shift()!;
      for (const w of adj[u]) {
        if (!(w in colour)) { colour[w] = 1 - colour[u]; queue.push(w); }
        else if (colour[w] === colour[u]) return { ok: false, conflict: [u, w] };
      }
    }
  }
  return { ok: true, colour };
}
```

Run on the example graph, it returns not bipartite with conflict b and c; on C6 it returns colours
alternating 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1. The outer loop matters: a graph with several components needs a search from
each.

**Realistic: matching people to tasks.** The augmenting-path method tries each person in turn; if their
preferred task is taken, it tries to move the current holder to another of their tasks, recursively.

```ts
const can: Record<string, string[]> = {
  Asha: ['api', 'db'], Ravi: ['api'], Meera: ['db', 'ui'], Kiran: ['ui', 'ops'],
};
const match: Record<string, string> = {};
const tryAssign = (p: string, seen: Set<string>): boolean => {
  for (const t of can[p]) {
    if (seen.has(t)) continue;
    seen.add(t);
    if (!(t in match) || tryAssign(match[t], seen)) { match[t] = p; return true; }
  }
  return false;
};
let size = 0;
for (const p in can) if (tryAssign(p, new Set())) size++;
console.log(size, match);
```

```text
4 { api: 'Ravi', db: 'Asha', ui: 'Meera', ops: 'Kiran' }
```

### Common mistake

**Counting a loop once.** The wrong way: a vertex with one loop and one other edge has degree 2. Why it
fails: a loop has both ends at the vertex, so it adds 2, giving degree 3. The fix: count edge ends, not
edges, and the handshake theorem stays true.

**Testing only one component for bipartiteness.** A disconnected graph is bipartite only if every
component is. Start a search from every uncoloured vertex.

### In real systems

Degree distributions describe networks: the web and social graphs have a few hubs of huge degree, which
shapes caching, sharding and fan-out costs. Bipartite graphs model recommendations (users and items),
ad matching, and job scheduling; matching algorithms assign drivers to riders, reviewers to papers and
residents to hospitals. Hypercube structure appears in parallel computing interconnects and in the bit
flipping of Gray codes.

## 10.3 Representing graphs and graph isomorphism

### In one sentence

Graphs are stored as adjacency lists, adjacency matrices or incidence matrices, each suiting different
sizes and queries, and two graphs are isomorphic when a one-to-one correspondence of their vertices
preserves adjacency, which invariants can disprove quickly but which in general is hard to confirm.

### The idea

#### Adjacency lists

An **adjacency list** records, for each vertex, the vertices adjacent to it (for a digraph, the
terminal vertices of edges leaving it). It uses space proportional to the number of vertices plus edges,
lists a vertex's neighbours in time proportional to its degree, and is the standard choice for large
sparse graphs. Checking whether a particular edge exists takes time proportional to the degree, unless
each list is a hash set.

#### Adjacency matrices

The **adjacency matrix** of a graph with vertices v1, ..., vₙ is the n-by-n matrix A with aᵢⱼ equal to 1 if
vᵢ and vⱼ are adjacent and 0 otherwise. For an undirected graph it is symmetric. For multigraphs, aᵢⱼ is
the number of edges between vᵢ and vⱼ, and a loop puts 1 on the diagonal (by the usual convention). For a
digraph, aᵢⱼ is 1 when there is an edge from vᵢ to vⱼ.

The matrix uses n² entries whatever the number of edges, answers "is there an edge?" in constant time, and
supports algebra: section 10.4 uses its powers to count paths. A graph with n vertices has at most n(n
minus 1)/2 edges; it is **sparse** when it has far fewer, and **dense** when it has close to that many.
For sparse graphs, most of the matrix is zeros.

#### Incidence matrices

The **incidence matrix** of a graph with vertices v1, ..., vₙ and edges e1, ..., eₘ is the n-by-m matrix M
with mᵢⱼ equal to 1 if edge eⱼ is incident with vᵢ. Each column has two 1s (one for a loop). Incidence
matrices are less common in software, but they appear in network flow formulations and circuit analysis,
where edges carry quantities.

#### Isomorphism

Simple graphs G1 equal to (V1, E1) and G2 equal to (V2, E2) are **isomorphic** if there is a bijection f
from V1 to V2 such that a and b are adjacent in G1 exactly when f(a) and f(b) are adjacent in G2. The
bijection is an **isomorphism**: a renaming of vertices that turns one edge set into the other. Isomorphic
graphs are the same graph drawn and labelled differently.

#### Invariants

A **graph invariant** is a property preserved by isomorphism: the number of vertices, the number of edges,
the degree sequence, whether the graph is connected or bipartite, the number of cycles of each length,
and so on. If two graphs differ in any invariant, they are not isomorphic.

Invariants can disprove isomorphism but agreeing on a few does not prove it. The cycle C6 and two
disjoint triangles both have 6 vertices, 6 edges and every degree equal to 2, but C6 is connected and the
triangles are not, so they are not isomorphic.

#### Testing isomorphism

To show two graphs are isomorphic, exhibit the bijection and check every pair. Brute force tries all n!
bijections, which is hopeless beyond small n. In practice algorithms prune by invariants: a vertex of
degree 3 must map to a vertex of degree 3, its neighbours' degrees must match, and so on. Practical tools
handle huge graphs well, and theoretical work has shown isomorphism can be decided in quasi-polynomial
time, but no polynomial-time algorithm is known for the general case.

### Worked example

**Simple: build all three representations.** For the four-vertex graph with edges e1 equal to 1–2, e2 equal
to 2–3, e3 equal to 3–4, e4 equal to 4–1 and e5 equal to 1–3:

| vertex | adjacency list | matrix row | incidence row (e1 to e5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2, 4, 3 | 0 1 1 1 | 1 0 0 1 1 |
| 2 | 1, 3 | 1 0 1 0 | 1 1 0 0 0 |
| 3 | 2, 4, 1 | 1 1 0 1 | 0 1 1 0 1 |
| 4 | 3, 1 | 1 0 1 0 | 0 0 1 1 0 |

Row sums of the adjacency matrix are the degrees: 3, 2, 3, 2. Each incidence column has exactly two 1s.

**Realistic: brute-force isomorphism on small graphs.**

```ts
type E = [number, number][];
const key = (E: E) => new Set(E.map(([a, b]) => Math.min(a, b) + ',' + Math.max(a, b)));
function iso(A: E, B: E, n: number) {
  const kb = key(B), p = [...Array(n).keys()];
  let found: number[] | null = null, tried = 0;
  const go = (k: number) => {
    if (found) return;
    if (k === n) {
      tried++;
      if (A.every(([a, b]) => kb.has(Math.min(p[a], p[b]) + ',' + Math.max(p[a], p[b])))) found = [...p];
      return;
    }
    for (let i = k; i < n; i++) { [p[k], p[i]] = [p[i], p[k]]; go(k + 1); [p[k], p[i]] = [p[i], p[k]]; }
  };
  go(0);
  return { found, tried };
}
console.log(iso([[0,1],[1,2],[2,3],[3,0],[0,2]], [[0,1],[1,2],[2,3],[3,0],[1,3]], 4));
```

```text
{ found: [ 1, 0, 3, 2 ], tried: 8 }
```

Both graphs are a 4-cycle with one diagonal. The map sends 0 to 1, 1 to 0, 2 to 3 and 3 to 2, carrying the
diagonal 0–2 onto 1–3. The search needed 8 of the 24 permutations. Pruning by degree would have tried only
the maps sending degree-3 vertices to degree-3 vertices: 2·2, which is 4.

## 10.4 Connectivity

### In one sentence

A path is a sequence of edges joining vertices end to end, a graph is connected when every pair of
vertices is joined by a path, and cut vertices, bridges, strong components and path counts from matrix
powers measure how robustly a graph holds together.

### The idea

#### Paths and circuits

A **path** of length n from u to v in an undirected graph is a sequence of n edges e1, ..., eₙ with
associated vertices x0 equal to u, x1, ..., xₙ equal to v, each eᵢ joining xᵢ₋₁ and xᵢ. In a simple graph we
just list the vertices. A path is a **circuit** if it begins and ends at the same vertex and has length
at least 1. It is **simple** if it does not repeat an edge.

Different books and libraries use "path" differently (some require no repeated vertices), so it is
worth checking definitions. Here, a path may repeat vertices; a simple path does not repeat edges.

#### Connected graphs and components

An undirected graph is **connected** if there is a path between every pair of distinct vertices. A
theorem worth knowing: in a connected graph there is a simple path, indeed one with no repeated vertices,
between every pair, obtained by cutting out any loops from a path.

A **connected component** is a maximal connected subgraph. Every graph is the disjoint union of its
components, and "is joined by a path to" is an equivalence relation whose classes are the components'
vertex sets: chapter 9 meeting chapter 10. Breadth-first or depth-first search from each unvisited vertex
finds the components in time proportional to vertices plus edges.

#### Distance

The **distance** between two vertices of a connected graph is the length of a shortest path between them,
and breadth-first search computes distances from a source in an unweighted graph. In the network formed
by triangle a, b, c, triangle d, e, g, the edge c–d and a pendant f on e, the distances from a are 1 to b
and c, 2 to d, 3 to e and g, and 4 to f. The largest distance between any pair is the **diameter**.

#### Cut vertices and bridges

Removing a vertex and its edges can disconnect a graph. Such a vertex is a **cut vertex**, or
articulation point. An edge whose removal disconnects the graph is a **cut edge**, or **bridge**.

In the network above, c, d and e are cut vertices: removing c separates a and b from the rest; removing e
isolates f. The bridges are c–d and e–f. Edges within a triangle are never bridges, because the other two
edges of the triangle still join their endpoints.

A graph with no cut vertices is **nonseparable**. The **vertex connectivity** κ(G) is the fewest vertices
whose removal disconnects G or leaves one vertex; the **edge connectivity** λ(G) is the fewest edges whose
removal disconnects it. Always κ(G) ≤ λ(G) ≤ the minimum degree. A network with λ equal to 1 has a single
point of failure among its links.

Tarjan's algorithm finds all cut vertices and bridges in one depth-first search, by tracking for each
vertex the earliest vertex reachable from its subtree using one back edge.

#### Directed connectivity

A directed graph is **strongly connected** if there is a directed path from every vertex to every other,
and **weakly connected** if its underlying undirected graph is connected. Its **strongly connected
components** are the maximal strongly connected subgraphs; contracting each to a single vertex leaves an
acyclic graph, the condensation, which can be topologically sorted.

The web graph has a giant strongly connected core, pages that can reach it, and pages reachable from it,
plus tendrils; that structure is why crawlers start from many seeds.

#### Paths and isomorphism

Paths give invariants: the existence of a simple circuit of a particular length is preserved by
isomorphism, which is how the triangle check in 10.3 worked.

#### Counting paths

**Theorem**: if A is the adjacency matrix of a graph (with the vertices ordered), the number of different
paths of length r from vᵢ to vⱼ is the (i, j) entry of Aʳ, using ordinary integer multiplication. The proof
is induction on r: a path of length r plus 1 from vᵢ to vⱼ is a path of length r from vᵢ to some vₖ followed
by an edge from vₖ to vⱼ, which is exactly the sum defining the (i, j) entry of Aʳ·A.

## 10.5 Euler and Hamilton paths

### In one sentence

An Euler circuit uses every edge exactly once and exists in a connected graph exactly when every degree
is even, which is easy to check and easy to construct, while a Hamilton circuit visits every vertex
exactly once and has no known efficient test, which makes it the gateway to NP-completeness.

### The idea

#### Euler circuits and paths

An **Euler circuit** in a graph is a simple circuit containing every edge. An **Euler path** is a simple
path containing every edge. The question goes back to the walk across the seven bridges of Königsberg:
can you cross every bridge once and return home? Model each land mass as a vertex and each bridge as an
edge, and the question becomes whether a multigraph has an Euler circuit.

**Theorem**: a connected multigraph with at least two vertices has an Euler circuit if and only if every
vertex has even degree. It has an Euler path that is not a circuit if and only if exactly two vertices
have odd degree, and then the path starts at one and ends at the other.

The Königsberg multigraph has four vertices, all of odd degree, so no such walk exists. One theorem, and a
question people walked around for years is settled.

#### Why the condition is necessary

Follow an Euler circuit. Every time it passes through a vertex it enters on one edge and leaves on
another, using two edges at that vertex. The start vertex is left at the beginning and re-entered at the
end, another pair. Since every edge is used exactly once, each degree is a sum of 2s, so even.

#### Why it is sufficient

Start anywhere and walk along unused edges. Because every degree is even, whenever you enter a vertex
other than the start, an unused edge remains to leave by, so you can only get stuck back at the start.
That gives a circuit. If edges remain, some unused edge touches the circuit (the graph is connected);
start a new walk there, which also closes up, and splice it into the first circuit. Repeat until all
edges are used. This is **Hierholzer's algorithm**, and with a stack it runs in time proportional to the
number of edges.

**Fleury's algorithm** is the other classic construction: walk, never crossing a bridge of the remaining
graph unless there is no other choice. It is easier to follow by hand but slower, since it needs a bridge
test at each step.

#### Hamilton circuits and paths

A **Hamilton path** visits every vertex exactly once; a **Hamilton circuit** does that and returns to the
start. The definitions look like Euler's with "vertex" for "edge", but the problems behave completely
differently. No simple degree condition characterises Hamilton circuits, and deciding whether one exists
is **NP-complete**: no polynomial-time algorithm is known, and finding one would give polynomial
algorithms for thousands of other hard problems.

Some facts still help:

- A graph with a vertex of degree 1 has no Hamilton circuit (the circuit needs two edges at every
  vertex).
- If a vertex has degree 2, both its edges must be in any Hamilton circuit.
- A Hamilton circuit uses exactly n edges, and once two edges at a vertex are chosen, the rest at that
  vertex are out.
- **Dirac's theorem**: a simple graph with n at least 3 vertices, each of degree at least n/2, has a
  Hamilton circuit.
- **Ore's theorem**: a simple graph with n at least 3 vertices where deg(u) plus deg(v) is at least n for
  every pair of non-adjacent u and v has a Hamilton circuit.

Both theorems are sufficient, not necessary: the cycle C10 has a Hamilton circuit with every degree only
2.

#### Gray codes

The cube Qₙ always has a Hamilton circuit, and walking it lists every n-bit string with consecutive strings
(and last and first) differing in one bit. That list is a **Gray code**. The reflected Gray code for 3 bits
is 000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111, 101, 100. Rotary encoders use Gray codes so a sensor reading taken between
two positions is wrong by at most one step instead of by many bits at once.

### Worked example

**Simple: decide by degrees.** The bowtie (triangle a, b, c and triangle c, d, e sharing c) has degrees 2, 2,
4, 2, 2: all even, so it has an Euler circuit. The house (square a, b, c, d plus a roof vertex e joined to a
and b) has degrees a 3, b 3, c 2, d 2, e 2: exactly two odd, so it has an Euler path from a to b but no
circuit. That is the classic "draw it without lifting your pen" puzzle, and the theorem says start at one
of the two vertices where the roof meets the square.

## 10.6 Shortest-path problems

### In one sentence

In a weighted graph the length of a path is the sum of its edge weights, Dijkstra's algorithm finds
shortest paths from one vertex when weights are non-negative by repeatedly finalising the closest
unfinished vertex, and the travelling salesperson problem, the cheapest Hamilton circuit, is NP-hard.

### The idea

#### Weighted graphs

A **weighted graph** assigns a number, its weight, to each edge: distance, travel time, cost, latency or
bandwidth. The **length** of a path is the sum of the weights of its edges. A shortest path between two
vertices is one of minimum length. In an unweighted graph every weight is 1, and breadth-first search
already solves the problem.

#### Dijkstra's algorithm

Dijkstra's algorithm computes shortest distances from a source a to every vertex when all weights are
non-negative.

1. Set the label L(a) to 0 and every other label to infinity. No vertex is finalised.
2. Repeatedly pick the unfinalised vertex u with the smallest label and finalise it.
3. For each neighbour v of u, if L(u) plus w(u, v) is less than L(v), set L(v) to that and record u as v's
   predecessor.
4. Stop when every vertex (or the target) is finalised.

Following predecessors back from a vertex gives a shortest path. The **invariant**: when a vertex is
finalised, its label is its true shortest distance. With a binary heap the algorithm runs in time
proportional to (vertices plus edges) times log vertices.

#### Why it is correct

Suppose u is the next vertex finalised with label L(u), and some path P to u were shorter. P starts inside
the finalised set and leaves it at some edge x to y, with y not finalised. The part of P up to y has length
at least L(y) (y's label was relaxed through x), and L(y) is at least L(u) because u was the minimum.
Since weights are non-negative, the rest of P adds nothing negative, so P has length at least L(u). That
contradicts P being shorter.

The "non-negative" assumption is used in the last step. With a negative edge, a later detour can be
cheaper, and Dijkstra can finalise too early. **Bellman–Ford** handles negative weights (and detects
negative cycles) by relaxing every edge n minus 1 times; **Floyd–Warshall** computes all-pairs distances
with the same triple loop as Warshall's algorithm in chapter 9, with min and plus in place of or and and.

#### The travelling salesperson problem

The **travelling salesperson problem** asks for a Hamilton circuit of minimum total weight in a complete
weighted graph. Brute force checks (n minus 1)!/2 distinct tours, which for 25 cities is about 3 × 10²³.
The problem is NP-hard. In practice, heuristics (nearest neighbour, 2-opt swaps) and exact branch-and-cut
solvers handle very large instances, and for distances obeying the triangle inequality there are
approximation algorithms with guaranteed ratios.

```ts
    if (c < best) { best = c; bt = t; }
    return;
  }
  for (let i = k; i < a.length; i++) { [a[k], a[i]] = [a[i], a[k]]; perm(a, k + 1); [a[k], a[i]] = [a[i], a[k]]; }
};
perm([1, 2, 3, 4], 0);
console.log('optimal', best, bt.map(i => C[i]).join('-'));
let cur = 0, cost = 0;
const seen = new Set([0]), tour = ['P'];
while (seen.size < 5) {
  let nb = -1;
  for (let j = 0; j < 5; j++) if (!seen.has(j) && (nb < 0 || d[cur][j] < d[cur][nb])) nb = j;
  cost += d[cur][nb]; cur = nb; seen.add(nb); tour.push(C[nb]);
}
cost += d[cur][0]; tour.push('P');
console.log('nearest neighbour', cost, tour.join('-'));
```

```text
optimal 32 P-R-Q-S-T-P
nearest neighbour 38 P-T-Q-R-S-P
```

Nearest neighbour grabs P–T (8), T–Q (2) and Q–R (3), then is forced into R–S (6) and the expensive S–P
(19). Greedy local choices left the worst edge for last. The optimum gives up the cheap T–Q and uses P–R,
R–Q, Q–S, S–T and T–P: 10 plus 3 plus 7 plus 4 plus 8, which is 32.

### Common mistake

**Running Dijkstra with negative weights.** The wrong way: using it on a graph with refunds or credits
modelled as negative costs. Why it fails: the proof's last step needs non-negative weights. With edges A–B
1, A–C 2 and C–B minus 2 (directed), Dijkstra finalises B at 1, but A–C–B costs 0. The fix: Bellman–Ford,
or reweighting as in Johnson's algorithm.

**Trusting greedy tours.** Nearest neighbour above is 19% worse than optimal. Use it as a starting point
for improvement, not as an answer.

### In real systems

Map routing runs Dijkstra-like searches with heuristics (A star) and precomputed hierarchies (contraction
hierarchies) to answer continental queries in milliseconds. Link-state routing protocols such as OSPF run
Dijkstra on each router over the advertised topology. Vehicle routing and delivery planning solve
travelling-salesperson variants every day with heuristics. Build systems and project planners compute
longest paths in acyclic graphs (the critical path), which is shortest path with signs flipped, safe only
because the graph has no cycles.

## 10.7 Planar graphs

### In one sentence

A graph is planar when it can be drawn in the plane with no edges crossing, every connected planar
drawing satisfies Euler's formula v minus e plus f equals 2, and that single formula gives edge bounds
proving that K5 and K3,3 are not planar, which by Kuratowski's theorem are the only two obstructions.

### The idea

#### Planarity

A graph is **planar** if it can be drawn in the plane without any edges crossing; such a drawing is a
**planar representation**. Planarity is a property of the graph, not of a drawing: K4 is usually drawn
with its diagonals crossing, but moving one vertex inside the triangle formed by the other three removes
the crossing, so K4 is planar. Q3 is planar too: draw a small square inside a large square and join the
corners.

The classic puzzle asks whether three houses can each be joined to three utilities (water, gas,
electricity) without lines crossing. That is asking whether K3,3 is planar, and the answer is no.

#### Faces and Euler's formula

A planar representation splits the plane into **regions** or **faces**, including one unbounded outer
face. **Euler's formula**: a connected planar simple graph with e edges and v vertices has exactly f equal
to e minus v plus 2 faces in any planar representation. So the number of faces does not depend on how the
graph is drawn.

Check on familiar shapes. The cube graph Q3 has 8 vertices, 12 edges and 6 faces: 8 minus 12 plus 6 is 2.
The dodecahedron has 20, 30 and 12; the icosahedron 12, 30 and 20. Both give 2. A tree with n vertices has n
minus 1 edges and a single face: n minus (n minus 1) plus 1 is 2.

#### The degree of a face

The **degree of a face** is the number of edges on its boundary, with an edge counted twice if the face
lies on both sides of it (a bridge, say). Every edge contributes exactly 2 to the sum of face degrees, so
the face degrees add up to 2e: a handshake theorem for faces.

#### Edge bounds

If a connected planar simple graph has v at least 3, every face has degree at least 3 (no loops, no
parallel edges), so 2e is at least 3f. Substituting f equal to e minus v plus 2 gives the bound
**e ≤ 3v minus 6**.

If, in addition, the graph has no triangles (no circuits of length 3), every face has degree at least 4,
so 2e is at least 4f, which gives **e ≤ 2v minus 4**.

Two consequences:

- **K5 is not planar**: it has 5 vertices and 10 edges, but 3·5 minus 6 is 9.
- **K3,3 is not planar**: it is bipartite, so it has no triangles, and has 6 vertices and 9 edges, but 2·6
  minus 4 is 8.

The bound e ≤ 3v minus 6 also shows that a planar simple graph has a vertex of degree at most 5. If every
degree were at least 6, the handshake theorem would give 2e at least 6v, so e at least 3v, contradicting e
at most 3v minus 6. This small fact drives the colouring results in 10.8.

#### Kuratowski's theorem

An **elementary subdivision** removes an edge u–v and adds a new vertex w with edges u–w and w–v: it puts a
vertex in the middle of an edge. Graphs are **homeomorphic** if they can be obtained from the same graph by
subdivisions. Subdividing does not change planarity.

**Kuratowski's theorem**: a graph is non-planar if and only if it contains a subgraph homeomorphic to K5
or K3,3. These two are the only fundamental obstructions. The Petersen graph is non-planar because it
contains a subdivided K3,3 (it does not contain a subdivided K5, since it has no vertex of degree 4).

Note that the edge bounds are necessary conditions only. A graph can satisfy e ≤ 3v minus 6 and still be
non-planar: K3,3 satisfies it (9 is at most 12), and it took the stronger triangle-free bound to rule it out.

#### Testing planarity

Linear-time planarity tests exist (Hopcroft and Tarjan gave the first; modern libraries use simpler
variants). They either produce a planar embedding or a Kuratowski subgraph as a certificate of
non-planarity.

### Worked example

**Simple: count faces without drawing.** A connected planar graph has 10 vertices, each of degree 3. Then 2e
is 30, so e is 15, and f is 15 minus 10 plus 2, which is 7 faces in every planar drawing. (The Petersen
graph also has 10 vertices of degree 3, but it is not planar, so the formula says nothing about it.)

**Simple: rule out planarity.** A simple graph with 8 vertices and 19 edges cannot be planar, because 3·8
minus 6 is 18. A triangle-free graph with 8 vertices and 13 edges cannot be planar, because 2·8 minus 4 is
12.

**Realistic: a check for small cases.**

```ts
function planarBounds(v: number, e: number, triangleFree: boolean) {
  const limit = triangleFree ? 2 * v - 4 : 3 * v - 6;
  return { v, e, limit, verdict: e > limit ? 'not planar' : 'bound inconclusive' };
}
console.log(planarBounds(5, 10, false));   // K5
console.log(planarBounds(6, 9, true));     // K3,3
console.log(planarBounds(8, 12, true));    // Q3
console.log(planarBounds(10, 15, false));  // Petersen
```

```text
{ v: 5, e: 10, limit: 9, verdict: 'not planar' }
{ v: 6, e: 9, limit: 8, verdict: 'not planar' }
```

which is 13.3, would rule it out). Bounds prove non-planarity; they never prove planarity.

algorithms exploit for faster preprocessing. Map colouring, next, is a question about planar graphs.

## 10.8 Graph coloring

### In one sentence

A colouring assigns colours to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ, the chromatic number is the
fewest colours possible, every planar graph can be coloured with four colours, and computing the
chromatic number in general is NP-hard, so practical systems use greedy orderings.

### The idea

#### Colourings and chromatic number

A **colouring** of a simple graph assigns a colour to each vertex so that no two adjacent vertices get the
same colour. The **chromatic number** χ(G) is the least number of colours in a colouring.

Map colouring is the origin: colour the regions of a map so that regions sharing a border differ. Build
the **dual graph**, with a vertex per region and an edge per shared border; it is planar, and colouring
the map is colouring the dual graph.

#### Small cases

- χ(Kn) is n: every pair is adjacent.
- χ(G) is 1 exactly when G has no edges, and at most 2 exactly when G is bipartite.
- χ(Cn) is 2 for even n and 3 for odd n.
- χ(Km,n) is 2.
- The wheel Wn (a hub joined to Cn) needs one colour more than its rim: 3 for even n, 4 for odd n.
- The Petersen graph has chromatic number 3.

To show χ(G) equals k, do two things: give a colouring with k colours (upper bound) and show that k minus 1
colours cannot work (lower bound), usually by finding a subgraph that needs k, like a triangle or an odd
cycle or a clique.

#### The four colour theorem

**Four colour theorem**: the chromatic number of a planar graph is at most 4. It was conjectured in the
nineteenth century and proved in 1976 by Appel and Haken with substantial computer checking of
configurations, the first major theorem to depend on a computer; a simpler computer-assisted proof
appeared in the 1990s and a fully machine-checked proof was completed later.

The **five colour theorem** has a short proof using the degree-5 fact from 10.7, and the **six colour
theorem** is easier still: by induction, remove a vertex of degree at most 5, colour the rest with six
colours, and at most five colours are used on its neighbours, leaving one for it.

#### Greedy colouring

Greedy colouring takes the vertices in some order and gives each the smallest colour not used by its
already-coloured neighbours. It never uses more than Δ plus 1 colours, where Δ is the maximum degree,
because a vertex has at most Δ coloured neighbours. The order matters enormously: on the crown graph (two
rows u1 to u4 and v1 to v4, with uᵢ adjacent to vⱼ whenever i differs from j), which is bipartite and so
2-colourable, an alternating order makes greedy use 4 colours.

The **Welsh–Powell** heuristic orders vertices by decreasing degree, which often helps. No polynomial
algorithm computes χ in general unless P equals NP; even deciding 3-colourability is NP-complete.

#### The chromatic polynomial

The **chromatic polynomial** P(G, k) counts colourings with at most k colours. For a path or tree with n
vertices it is k(k minus 1)ⁿ⁻¹; for a cycle it is (k minus 1)ⁿ plus (−1)ⁿ(k minus 1). χ(G) is the least k
with P(G, k) positive. For C5 and k equal to 3, the formula gives 32 minus 2, which is 30, and brute-force
counting agrees.

### Worked example

**Realistic: exam scheduling.** Five exams (maths, physics, chemistry, computing, biology). Two exams
conflict if a candidate takes both: maths–physics, maths–computing, physics–chemistry,
computing–chemistry, computing–biology, chemistry–biology and maths–biology. Exams with the same colour can
share a slot.

another), and the triangle shows two would not. Mobile networks assign frequencies so neighbouring cells
differ; timetabling, Sudoku and map rendering are colouring problems; and distributed systems colour
conflict graphs to decide which tasks can run in parallel.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 10, Graphs">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version of this chapter: a graph is vertices and edges, and most questions are about
degrees and paths. Count degrees, use the handshake theorem (degrees add to twice the edges), and run
breadth-first search to see what is reachable and how far.

Example: in a team chat where an edge means "has messaged", five people with degrees 3, 3, 2, 2, 2 give 12,
so there are 6 conversations. A claim that seven people each messaged exactly three others is false at
once: 21 is odd.

The beginner mistake is to trust the picture. Two drawings that look different can be the same graph, and
a drawing with crossings can still be planar. Fix it by reasoning from the vertex and edge lists, not the
layout.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level graphs become data structures and algorithms. You choose adjacency lists for sparse graphs
and matrices for dense ones, find components with search, test bipartiteness with two-colouring, run
Dijkstra for weighted shortest paths and Hierholzer for Euler circuits, each in near-linear time.

Example: a delivery service with 40,000 road junctions and 100,000 road segments stores adjacency lists
(about 240,000 entries counting both directions and the vertex array) rather than a 1.6-billion-entry
matrix, and answers a shortest-route query with a heap-based Dijkstra.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

The advanced view is about which problems are easy and which are hard. Euler circuits, shortest paths,
bipartiteness, planarity, 2-colouring and connectivity are polynomial. Hamilton circuits, the travelling
salesperson, 3-colouring and maximum clique are NP-complete or NP-hard. Isomorphism sits in between:
quasi-polynomial in theory, fast in practice.

Example: Euler and Hamilton questions look alike, but genome assemblers switched from finding Hamilton
paths in overlap graphs to Euler paths in de Bruijn graphs precisely to land on the easy side.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases live at the boundaries of definitions. Is a single vertex connected? Yes. Does an Euler
circuit theorem apply to a graph with isolated vertices? Only after ignoring them. Does K2 have a Hamilton
circuit? Not as a simple graph: it would use the one edge twice. Is the empty graph bipartite? Yes,
vacuously.

Example: a graph with vertices a, b, c, d where only a–b and b–a (a multigraph pair) exist and c, d are
isolated has every degree even, but "connected multigraph" fails unless c and d are dropped. Code that
checks degrees without checking connectivity will report an Euler circuit that does not touch c.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases combine conventions. A loop adds 2 to a degree but 1 to the diagonal of the adjacency matrix,
so row sums equal degrees only if loops are counted twice. Parallel edges break "list the vertices to
name the path". Negative weights break Dijkstra; negative cycles make "shortest path" undefined.

Example: a currency graph with edge weights minus log(exchange rate) has a negative cycle exactly when an
arbitrage loop exists. Bellman–Ford finds it; Dijkstra would quietly return wrong prices.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, graphs are large, change over time and live in memory layouts that matter. Compressed
sparse rows, batched breadth-first search, bidirectional and goal-directed search, precomputed hierarchies
and incremental connectivity are the tools. Recursion limits, integer overflow in path counts and
floating-point ties in weights are real failure modes.

Example: counting paths with Aⁿ overflows 64-bit integers quickly on dense graphs (K10's path counts grow
like 9ⁿ), so production code uses BigInt, modular counts or logarithms, depending on what the count is
for.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is to ask "what is the graph?" before "what is the algorithm?". Decide what the vertices are,
what an edge means, whether it has a direction and a weight, and whether multiplicity matters. A good
model usually makes the algorithm obvious.

Example: "can these meetings fit into three rooms?" is a colouring question if meetings are vertices and
overlaps are edges; for intervals on a line that graph is special (an interval graph), and sorting by start
time colours it optimally, which a generic colouring heuristic would not guarantee.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design, graphs describe dependencies, networks and data flow, and graph properties are design
properties. Cut vertices and bridges are single points of failure; strongly connected components in a
dependency graph are cycles that block independent deployment; the diameter bounds how many hops a
message needs.

Example: a service graph with one cut vertex (an authentication gateway every request passes) has vertex
connectivity 1. Adding a second gateway on a separate path raises it to 2, and the analysis in 10.4 tells
you which single failures still partition the system.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategic view is to recognise the classic problem under the disguise and know its complexity class.
If it is an easy problem, use the standard algorithm. If it is a hard one, change the problem: restrict the
input (planar, bipartite, interval, small treewidth), accept an approximation, or use a solver and a time
limit.

Example: a team facing a 3,000-stop delivery routing problem does not search for an exact travelling
salesperson tour. It uses a construction heuristic plus local improvement and checks the result against a
lower bound, accepting a tour a few percent above optimal in seconds.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**is a tree a graph?**

Yes: a tree is a connected graph with no simple circuits. Chapter 11 studies trees in depth.

**when should I use an adjacency matrix?**

When the graph is small or dense, when you need constant-time edge tests, or when you want matrix algebra
such as counting paths with powers.

**why is Euler easy and Hamilton hard?**

Euler has a local characterisation (every degree even, plus connectivity), and a walk can be built greedily
and spliced. Hamilton has no known local test; whether a vertex can be used depends globally on the rest of
the path.

**does Dijkstra work on directed graphs?**

Yes, unchanged, provided all weights are non-negative. Relax only edges leaving the finalised vertex.

**is the four colour theorem useful in practice?**

Mostly as a guarantee. Real map and scheduling problems use heuristics; the theorem says four colours
always suffice for planar maps, but finding a 4-colouring efficiently uses a separate quadratic algorithm.

**how can I tell if two large graphs are isomorphic?**

Compare cheap invariants first (sizes, degree sequences, triangle counts). If all agree, use a canonical
labelling tool, which handles very large practical graphs quickly.

## Key takeaways

- A graph is vertices and edges; directions, multiplicities, loops and weights are modelling choices.
- The handshake theorem: degrees add to 2e, so the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
- Kn, Cn, Wn, Qn and Km,n are the standard families; a graph is bipartite exactly when it has no odd cycle.
- Adjacency lists suit sparse graphs; matrices suit dense ones and matrix algebra.
- Invariants can prove non-isomorphism; only an explicit bijection proves isomorphism.
- Connectivity, components, cut vertices and bridges measure robustness; strong components do it for
  digraphs; entries of Aʳ count paths of length r.
- Euler circuits exist exactly when the graph is connected with all degrees even; Hamilton circuits have
  no such test and are NP-complete.
- Dijkstra finds shortest paths with non-negative weights; the travelling salesperson problem is NP-hard.
- Euler's formula v minus e plus f equals 2 gives e ≤ 3v minus 6 and e ≤ 2v minus 4; K5 and K3,3 are the only
  obstructions to planarity.
- Planar graphs are 4-colourable; computing χ is hard in general; greedy colouring depends on order.

## Glossary

- **Graph, vertex, edge**: a set of vertices with edges joining pairs of them.
- **Simple graph, multigraph, pseudograph**: no parallel edges or loops; parallel edges allowed; loops too.
- **Directed graph**: edges are ordered pairs.
- **Adjacent, incident**: joined by an edge; an edge touches a vertex.
- **Degree, in-degree, out-degree**: edges at a vertex, loops twice; edges into and out of a vertex.
- **Handshake theorem**: degrees add to twice the number of edges.
- **Complete graph Kn**: every pair adjacent.
- **Cycle Cn, wheel Wn, cube Qn**: a ring; a ring plus a hub; bit strings differing in one bit.
- **Bipartite graph, Km,n**: vertices split in two parts, edges only across; all edges across.
- **Matching, perfect matching**: edges with no shared endpoints; one covering every vertex.
- **Hall's theorem**: a bipartite graph has a matching covering one side exactly when every set on that side
  has at least as many neighbours.
- **Subgraph, induced subgraph, union**: part of a graph; all edges among chosen vertices; combine graphs.
- **Adjacency list, adjacency matrix, incidence matrix**: neighbours per vertex; n-by-n 0–1 table;
  vertex-by-edge table.
- **Isomorphism, invariant**: an adjacency-preserving bijection; a property it preserves.
- **Path, circuit, simple path**: a walk along edges; one that returns; one without repeated edges.
- **Connected, component**: every pair joined by a path; a maximal connected subgraph.
- **Cut vertex, bridge**: a vertex or edge whose removal disconnects the graph.
- **Vertex and edge connectivity**: fewest vertices or edges whose removal disconnects.
- **Strongly, weakly connected**: directed paths both ways; connected ignoring directions.
- **Euler circuit, Euler path**: uses every edge once, returning or not.
- **Hamilton circuit, Hamilton path**: visits every vertex once, returning or not.
- **Gray code**: a cyclic list of bit strings with neighbours differing in one bit.
- **Weighted graph, length**: edges carry numbers; a path's length is the sum.
- **Dijkstra's algorithm**: shortest paths from one source with non-negative weights.
- **Travelling salesperson problem**: the cheapest Hamilton circuit.
- **Planar graph, face**: drawable without crossings; a region of such a drawing.
- **Euler's formula**: v minus e plus f equals 2 for connected planar graphs.
- **Elementary subdivision, homeomorphic**: put a vertex in an edge; related by subdivisions.
- **Kuratowski's theorem**: non-planar exactly when it contains a subdivided K5 or K3,3.
- **Colouring, chromatic number**: adjacent vertices differ; the fewest colours needed.
- **Four colour theorem**: planar graphs have chromatic number at most 4.
- **Chromatic polynomial**: the number of colourings with at most k colours.

**Self-check.**

**1. Why must every graph have an even number of odd-degree vertices?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The degrees add to 2e, which is even, and the even degrees contribute an even amount, so the odd degrees
must be even in number.

</details>

**2. Which graphs are bipartite?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exactly those with no circuit of odd length.

</details>

**3. What does the (i, j) entry of A cubed count?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The paths of length 3 from vᵢ to vⱼ.

</details>

**4. When does a connected multigraph have an Euler path but no Euler circuit?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

When exactly two vertices have odd degree; the path runs between them.

</details>

**5. What assumption does Dijkstra's correctness proof use?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That all edge weights are non-negative, so extending a path never makes it shorter.

</details>

**6. Why is K3,3 not planar?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It has no triangles, so a planar drawing would need e ≤ 2v minus 4, which is 8, but it has 9 edges.

</details>

**7. What is the chromatic number of a bipartite graph with at least one edge?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

2.

</details>
