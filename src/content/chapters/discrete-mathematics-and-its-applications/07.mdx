Probability is counting with a purpose: how likely is an outcome, what should we expect on average, and
how far from average can things go? This chapter starts from "favourable outcomes over all outcomes",
builds conditional probability and Bayes' theorem, and ends with expected value and variance, the tools
behind randomised algorithms, hash table analysis and every alerting threshold.

## 7.1 An introduction to discrete probability

### In one sentence

When every outcome of an experiment is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of
outcomes in it divided by the total number of outcomes.

### The idea

#### Experiments, sample spaces and events

An **experiment** is a procedure with a set of possible results. The **sample space** S is the set of all
possible outcomes. An **event** is a subset of S. Rolling one die: S is 1 to 6, and "even" is the event 2,
4, 6.

#### Laplace's definition

If S is finite, non-empty and all outcomes are equally likely, the **probability** of event E is p(E)
equal to |E| / |S|. So probability is between 0 and 1, an impossible event has probability 0, and a
certain event has probability 1.

Two dice summing to 8: the outcomes (2, 6), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 3), (6, 2), 5 of 36, so 5/36, about 0.139.
Note (3, 5) and (5, 3) are different outcomes, because the dice are distinguishable even if they look
alike.

#### Complements and unions

The **complement** of E, written Ē, is S minus E, and p(Ē) equals 1 minus p(E). This is the counting
complement from chapter 6 again: "at least one" is easiest as 1 minus "none".

For two events, p(E1 ∪ E2) equals p(E1) plus p(E2) minus p(E1 ∩ E2), the subtraction rule divided by |S|.

#### Probability by counting

Most problems in this section are counting problems from chapter 6 with a division at the end. A lottery
that draws 6 numbers from 49: C(49, 6), which is 13,983,816, equally likely draws, so one ticket wins with
probability about 7.2 in 100 million. A full house in poker: 3,744 of C(52, 5) hands, about 0.0014.

#### Monty Hall

A game show has three doors, one hiding a car. You pick a door; the host, who knows where the car is,
opens another door with a goat, and offers a switch. Switching wins with probability 2/3. Your first pick
is right with probability 1/3, and the host's action does not change that; switching wins in every other
case.

#### Probability is a model

A probability is not a property of the world on its own; it is a property of a model of the world. When I
say a die shows 3 with probability 1/6, I am choosing a sample space of six outcomes and declaring them
equally likely. If the die is loaded, the model is wrong, and every calculation built on it inherits the
error.

This matters in software because the models are often invisible. "Each request fails independently with
probability 0.01" is a model. So is "hash values are uniform" and "users arrive at random". Each one is
an assumption that can be tested against logs, and each one fails in a recognisable way: correlated
outages, adversarial keys, traffic spikes at the top of the hour.

The discipline the chapter teaches is to write the model down before computing anything. Name the
experiment, list or describe the sample space, say which outcomes are equally likely or what the weights
are, and only then define the event you care about. Most probability mistakes are modelling mistakes, not
arithmetic ones.

#### Ordered or unordered

Laplace's definition needs a sample space whose outcomes really are equally likely. For two dice, the 36
ordered pairs are equally likely; the 21 unordered pairs are not, because a double such as (3, 3) arises
one way and a mixed pair such as (2, 5) arises two ways. Counting unordered pairs and dividing by 21 gives
wrong answers.

For card hands, unordered hands are equally likely, so counting combinations works. The probability that
a 5-card hand holds exactly two aces is C(4, 2)·C(48, 3) divided by C(52, 5), about 0.040: choose the two
aces, choose the three non-aces, divide by all hands. Either ordered or unordered counting works for
hands, as long as numerator and denominator use the same convention.

### Worked example

**Simple: dice and complements.**

```ts
let s8 = 0;
for (let a = 1; a <= 6; a++) for (let b = 1; b <= 6; b++) if (a + b === 8) s8++;
console.log(s8, s8 / 36);
console.log('at least one six in 4 rolls:', 1 - (5 / 6) ** 4);
```

```text
5 0.1388888888888889
at least one six in 4 rolls: 0.5177469135802468
```

**Realistic: Monty Hall by simulation.** Simulation is the honest way to check an argument that feels
wrong.

```ts
let seed = 12345;
const rnd = () => (seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2147483648) / 2147483648;
let stay = 0, sw = 0; const T = 100000;
for (let t = 0; t < T; t++) {
  const car = Math.floor(rnd() * 3), pick = Math.floor(rnd() * 3);
  const open = [0, 1, 2].find(d => d !== car && d !== pick)!;
  const other = [0, 1, 2].find(d => d !== pick && d !== open)!;
  if (pick === car) stay++;
  if (other === car) sw++;
}
console.log(stay / T, sw / T);
```

```text
0.32792 0.67208
```

The seeded generator is the linear congruential method from chapter 4, so the run is reproducible.

### Common mistake

**Treating unequal outcomes as equally likely.** The wrong way: "the sum of two dice is one of 11 values
from 2 to 12, so each has probability 1/11." Why it fails: 7 can happen 6 ways and 2 only 1 way.

The fix is to use a sample space of equally likely outcomes, here the 36 ordered pairs, and count.

**Ignoring how information was revealed.** In Monty Hall the host's choice depends on where the car is.
That dependence is the whole puzzle; 7.2's conditional probability makes it precise.

### In real systems

Uniform random choice is everywhere: load balancers picking a random server, sampling 1 percent of
requests for tracing, shuffling test order, A/B test assignment. Each relies on outcomes being equally
likely, which is why biased shortcuts such as `Math.floor(Math.random() * n)` are fine but `rand() % n`
with a small generator range is subtly not.

## 7.2 Probability theory

### In one sentence

A probability distribution assigns a non-negative weight to each outcome so the weights sum to 1, and
conditional probability, independence, Bernoulli trials and random variables let us reason about
outcomes that are not equally likely.

### The idea

#### Probability distributions

For a sample space S, finite or countable, a **probability distribution** assigns each outcome s a number
p(s) with 0 at most p(s) at most 1, and the p(s) summing to 1. The probability of an event E is the sum of
p(s) over s in E. A **uniform distribution** gives every outcome 1/|S|, recovering Laplace's definition.

A loaded die where 3 is twice as likely as each other face: weights 1/7 for each other face and 2/7 for 3.
The probability of an odd number is 1/7 plus 2/7 plus 1/7, which is 4/7.

For pairwise disjoint events E1, E2, ..., the probability of their union is the sum of their
probabilities.

#### Conditional probability

The **conditional probability** of E given F, for p(F) greater than 0, is p(E | F) equal to p(E ∩ F) /
p(F). It restricts the sample space to F and rescales.

A random 4-bit string starts with 0. What is the chance it contains two consecutive 0s? Of the 8 strings
starting with 0, 5 contain 00, so 5/8.

#### Independence

E and F are **independent** if p(E ∩ F) equals p(E)p(F), equivalently p(E | F) equals p(E): knowing F
tells you nothing about E. Events are **mutually independent** if every subcollection satisfies the
product rule; pairwise independence is not enough.

Two coin flips are independent. "The first flip is heads" and "the two flips match" are also independent,
each with probability 1/2 and both together 1/4, which surprises people.

#### Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution

A **Bernoulli trial** has two outcomes, success with probability p and failure with probability q equal
to 1 minus p. In n mutually independent trials, the probability of exactly k successes is C(n, k)pᵏqⁿ⁻ᵏ.
As a function of k this is the **binomial distribution**, and by the binomial theorem the probabilities
sum to (p plus q)ⁿ, which is 1.

#### Random variables

A **random variable** is a function from the sample space to the real numbers: it assigns a number to
each outcome. It is neither random nor a variable, despite the name. The **distribution** of a random
variable X is the set of pairs (r, p(X equal to r)).

For two dice, the sum is a random variable taking values 2 to 12 with probabilities 1/36, 2/36, ..., 6/36,
..., 1/36.

#### The birthday problem

How many people must be in a room before the probability that two share a birthday exceeds 1/2? Assume
365 equally likely birthdays. The probability that n people all differ is (365/365)(364/365)...((365 minus
n plus 1)/365); one minus that passes 1/2 at n equal to 23.

#### Monte Carlo algorithms

A **probabilistic algorithm** makes random choices. A **Monte Carlo** algorithm always runs fast but may
answer wrongly with small probability, which shrinks with repetition. Testing a batch of items by sampling
k at random, when a tenth are defective, misses a bad batch with probability 0.9ᵏ: 0.35 for 10 tests,
0.005 for 50. Miller–Rabin from chapter 4 errs with probability at most (1/4)ᵏ after k rounds.

#### The probabilistic method

To prove an object with some property exists, show a random object has it with positive probability.
Erdős used this to show the Ramsey number R(k, k) is greater than 2^(k/2): a random colouring of a smaller
complete graph has no monochromatic k-clique with positive probability, without anyone exhibiting one.

#### Conditioning shrinks the sample space

A useful way to see p(E | F) is as a new experiment: throw away every outcome outside F, and
renormalise what remains so it sums to 1. For three fair coins, conditioning on "at least one head"
leaves 7 of the 8 outcomes. Three of them have exactly two heads, so the conditional probability is 3/7,
about 0.43, compared with 3/8 unconditionally.

This view also explains why conditioning can raise or lower a probability. Learning that F happened
removes outcomes, and whether E becomes more or less likely depends on whether the removed outcomes were
mostly in E or mostly outside it.

#### Sampling with and without replacement

The binomial distribution assumes independent trials with a fixed success probability. That holds for
flipping a coin, or for drawing with replacement. It fails when you draw without replacement from a
finite batch, because each draw changes what is left.

Take a batch of 100 parts with 10 defective, and inspect 5 chosen at random without replacement. The
probability none is defective is C(90, 5)/C(100, 5), about 0.584. The binomial with p equal to 0.1 gives
0.9⁵, about 0.590. Close, because 5 is small next to 100; as the sample grows relative to the batch, the
gap widens. The exact distribution without replacement is called hypergeometric, and it is just Laplace's
definition applied to subsets.

#### Small failure rates add up

For n independent components each failing with probability p, the chance of no failure is (1 minus p)ⁿ.
With p equal to 0.02 and n equal to 50, that is about 0.364: a fleet of individually reliable parts is
more likely than not to have at least one failure. The chance of at most one failure is about 0.736.

The same arithmetic drives availability. Five independent dependencies at 99.9 percent each give about
99.5 percent together, because a request needs all five. This is why architects count dependencies on the
critical path and why each one added costs reliability.

#### Where the randomness comes from

Every simulation in this chapter depends on a source of random numbers, and computers produce
pseudorandom ones: a deterministic function of a seed that only looks random. A linear congruential
generator, from chapter 4, computes the next state as a times the state plus c, modulo m. It is fast and
reproducible, but its low bits cycle with short periods, and using them to flip coins biases the results.

For simulations and tests, prefer a well-studied generator such as PCG or xoshiro, seeded explicitly so a
failing run can be replayed. For anything security-related, such as tokens, keys and nonces, use the
platform's cryptographic generator, which in Node is `crypto.randomInt` or `crypto.getRandomValues`.
`Math.random()` is neither seedable nor suitable for secrets.

#### Hash collisions in practice

The birthday bound is the reason identifier lengths are chosen the way they are. With 100,000 random
32-bit values, the chance of at least one collision is about 0.69, which my Node loop computed from the
product of (1 minus i/2³²). A 32-bit identifier is therefore unsafe for even a modest table. With 128-bit
random identifiers, such as version 4 UUIDs with 122 random bits, the chance of any collision stays
negligible for any realistic number of identifiers, which is why systems generate them without
coordination.

### Worked example

**Simple: conditional probability by enumeration.**

```ts
let a = 0, b = 0;
for (let m = 0; m < 16; m++) {
  const s = m.toString(2).padStart(4, '0');
  if (s[0] === '0') { b++; if (s.includes('00')) a++; }
}
console.log(a, b, a / b);
```

```text
5 8 0.625
```

**Realistic: the binomial distribution for failures.** A fleet of 20 servers, each failing on a given day
independently with probability 0.05.

```ts
const C = (n: number, r: number) => { let x = 1; for (let i = 1; i <= r; i++) x = (x * (n - r + i)) / i; return x; };
const binom = (n: number, k: number, p: number) => C(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k);
console.log(binom(20, 3, 0.05), binom(20, 0, 0.05), 1 - binom(20, 0, 0.05));
```

```text
0.05958214776873275 0.35848592240854193 0.6415140775914581
```

Exactly 3 fail with probability about 6 percent; at least one fails on 64 percent of days. Small
per-machine risks add up across a fleet.

**Realistic: the birthday problem and hash collisions.**

```ts
const bday = (n: number, d = 365) => { let p = 1; for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) p *= (d - i) / d; return 1 - p; };
console.log([10, 23, 30, 50, 70].map(n => bday(n).toFixed(4)).join(' '));
```

```text

```

With d possible values, collisions become likely after about √(2 ln 2 · d) items. For 32-bit hashes that
is about 77,000 items, which Node confirmed by stepping n upward.

### Common mistake

**Assuming independence.** The wrong way: two disks in the same server each fail with probability 0.01
per year, so both fail with probability 0.0001. Why it fails: they share power, firmware, age and
workload, so failures are correlated, and real double-failure rates are much higher.

The fix is to check independence, not assume it. Put replicas in different racks, zones and batches so
the assumption is closer to true.

**Confusing p(E | F) with p(F | E).** That is the subject of 7.3.

### In real systems

Availability calculations, replication factors, and SLOs are binomial calculations under independence
assumptions. Sampling in observability, canary releases and randomised load balancing ("power of two
choices") all use conditional probability and independence. UUIDs and content hashes rely on the birthday
bound: 122 random bits make collisions negligible even at trillions of IDs.

## 7.3 Bayes' theorem

### In one sentence

Bayes' theorem turns the probability of evidence given a cause into the probability of the cause given
the evidence, and it shows why a positive result from an accurate test for a rare condition is still
usually a false alarm.

### The idea

#### The theorem

Suppose E and F are events with p(E) and p(F) non-zero. Then p(F | E) equals p(E | F)p(F) divided by
p(E | F)p(F) plus p(E | F̄)p(F̄). The denominator is just p(E), split by whether F happened.

In words: the chance of the cause given the evidence is the chance of the evidence under the cause, times
the prior chance of the cause, divided by the total chance of the evidence.

#### Where it comes from

By the definition of conditional probability, p(F | E)p(E) and p(E | F)p(F) are both p(E ∩ F). Divide by
p(E). Then expand p(E) as p(E ∩ F) plus p(E ∩ F̄), each written as a conditional times a prior. That
expansion is the **law of total probability**.

#### The generalised form

If F1, ..., Fₙ partition the sample space, p(Fⱼ | E) equals p(E | Fⱼ)p(Fⱼ) divided by the sum over i of
p(E | Fᵢ)p(Fᵢ). This chooses among several causes, such as which of several servers produced an error.

#### Base rates

The prior p(F) is the **base rate**. When it is small, even strong evidence leaves the posterior modest.
Ignoring it, the **base rate fallacy**, is the commonest probabilistic error people make.

#### Bayesian spam filters

A **Bayesian spam filter** estimates, from training mail, p(w | spam) and p(w | not spam) for each word
w. For a new message containing w, Bayes gives p(spam | w). With several words, a naive filter assumes
words occur independently given the class and multiplies their probabilities. The assumption is false but
the filter works well, because it only has to rank spam above ham.

#### Choosing among several causes

The generalised form is how a system decides which of several explanations best fits an observation.
Suppose three servers take 50, 30 and 20 percent of traffic, and their error rates are 1, 2 and 5
percent. An error arrives. Which server probably produced it?

The total error rate is 0.5·0.01 plus 0.3·0.02 plus 0.2·0.05, which is 0.021. The posteriors are 0.005,
0.006 and 0.010 divided by 0.021: about 0.24, 0.29 and 0.48. The smallest server is the likeliest source,
because its error rate outweighs its small share of traffic. The prior and the likelihood both matter, and
neither alone gives the answer.

#### Updating in sequence

Bayes' theorem can be applied repeatedly: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. This is what made the
second test in the step-by-step trace so powerful. It is also what a spam filter does word by word, and
what a monitoring system does as more evidence arrives.

The catch is independence. Updating twice on two pieces of evidence is only correct if they are
conditionally independent given the cause. Two tests that share a flaw, or two alerts driven by the same
metric, are not two pieces of evidence; treating them as such overstates confidence.

#### Odds form

Bayes' theorem is often easier in odds. The posterior odds of F against F̄ equal the prior odds times the
likelihood ratio p(E | F)/p(E | F̄). For the test, prior odds are 1 to 99, the likelihood ratio is
0.98/0.05, which is 19.6, so posterior odds are 19.6 to 99, about 1 to 5. That is the same 16.5 percent,
reached with one multiplication. In log-odds, each piece of evidence adds a number, which is exactly how a
naive Bayes filter is implemented.

#### Comparing two variants

Bayes' theorem is also a way to compare hypotheses about data, which is the core of Bayesian A/B testing.
Instead of asking "how surprising is this data if there is no difference", the Bayesian view asks "given
this data, how probable is it that variant B is better than A". The machinery for continuous rates goes
beyond this chapter, but the discrete idea is the same: prior times likelihood, normalised.

A discrete version I can compute by hand: suppose a conversion rate is either 2 percent or 3 percent, with
equal prior probability, and 10 visitors produce 1 conversion. The likelihoods are the binomial
probabilities C(10, 1)·p·(1 minus p)⁹. The likelihoods are about 0.167 for 2 percent and 0.228 for 3 percent, so the posterior
probability of the higher rate is 0.228 divided by 0.395, about 0.58. The data only slightly favours the
higher rate, because 10 visitors are too few to say much. With 1,000 visitors and 30 conversions, the
likelihood ratio is about 9 to 1 and the posterior for 3 percent rises to about 0.90, by my Python check.
A larger sample would push it further toward certainty. Nothing about the method
changed; only the amount of evidence did. This is a useful corrective to dashboards that declare a winner
after a few dozen visits: the arithmetic of Bayes makes it plain how little a small sample can move a
reasonable prior, and how a large one can overwhelm almost any prior. In practice, decide the sample size
before the experiment starts, and resist stopping the moment the numbers look good, since repeatedly
peeking and stopping on a favourable result inflates false wins; that is the same lesson as the base rate, seen from the side of sample size.

### Worked example

**Simple: a rare condition.** A condition affects 1 percent of people. A test detects it 98 percent of
the time (sensitivity) and correctly clears 95 percent of healthy people (specificity).

```ts
const sens = 0.98, spec = 0.95, prev = 0.01;
const pPos = sens * prev + (1 - spec) * (1 - prev);
console.log('P(+):', pPos.toFixed(4), 'P(D | +):', (sens * prev / pPos).toFixed(4));
```

```text
P(+): 0.0593 P(D | +): 0.1653
```

Only about 17 percent of positives have the condition. Counting people makes it obvious: of 10,000, 100
have it and 98 test positive; of the 9,900 healthy, 495 test positive anyway. 98 of 593 is about 17
percent.

**Realistic: an alert.** An alert fires in 99 percent of minutes with a real incident and in 2 percent
of normal minutes. Incidents occur in 0.5 percent of minutes. Then p(incident | alert) is 0.99·0.005
divided by (0.99·0.005 plus 0.02·0.995), about 0.20. Four of five pages are false alarms, which is why
alert fatigue is a base-rate problem.

**Realistic: a naive spam filter.** Suppose training gives these word probabilities.

| Word | p(word, spam) | p(word, not spam) |
|---|---|---|
| offer | 0.20 | 0.01 |
| invoice | 0.05 | 0.04 |
| meeting | 0.01 | 0.08 |

```ts
const sp: Record<string, [number, number]> = {
  offer: [0.2, 0.01], invoice: [0.05, 0.04], meeting: [0.01, 0.08],
};
function spamProb(words: string[], prior = 0.5) {
  let s = prior, h = 1 - prior;
  for (const w of words) { s *= sp[w][0]; h *= sp[w][1]; }
  return s / (s + h);
}
for (const ws of [['offer'], ['offer', 'invoice'], ['offer', 'meeting'], ['meeting']])
  console.log(ws.join(' + '), spamProb(ws).toFixed(4));
```

```text
offer 0.9524
offer + invoice 0.9615
offer + meeting 0.7143
meeting 0.1111
```

### Common mistake

**Confusing p(E | F) with p(F | E).** The wrong way: "the test is 98 percent accurate, so a positive

out of 10,000.

**Multiplying in naive Bayes without logs.** Products of many small probabilities underflow to 0. Add
logarithms instead, and smooth zero counts so one unseen word does not veto everything.

### In real systems

diagnosis tools all run Bayes' theorem. In operations, it is the reason to measure an alert's precision,
not just its recall: if incidents are rare, a detector needs a very low false positive rate to be useful.

## 7.4 Expected value and variance

### In one sentence

The expected value of a random variable is its probability-weighted average, which adds across sums of
variables whether or not they are independent, and the variance measures spread, bounding how far values
stray from the mean through Chebyshev's inequality.

### The idea

#### Expected value

The **expected value** or mean of a random variable X on sample space S is E(X), the sum over outcomes s
of p(s)X(s), or equivalently the sum over values r of r·p(X equal to r). For a fair die it is 3.5, which is
never rolled: an expectation is an average, not a likely value.

For a loaded die with 3 twice as likely, E(X) is (1 plus 2 plus 6 plus 4 plus 5 plus 6)/7, which is 24/7,
about 3.43.

#### Linearity of expectation

For any random variables X1, ..., Xₙ and constants a and b, E(X1 plus ... plus Xₙ) is E(X1) plus ... plus
E(Xₙ), and E(aX plus b) is aE(X) plus b. No independence is needed. This is the most useful fact in the
chapter.

The expected number of successes in n Bernoulli trials with probability p is np: write the count as a sum
of n indicator variables, each with expectation p.

#### Indicator variables

An **indicator random variable** is 1 if an event happens and 0 otherwise; its expectation is the event's
probability. Counting problems become expectation problems: write the count as a sum of indicators and
add their probabilities.

The expected number of people who get their own hat back when n hats are returned at random is 1, for
every n: each person has probability 1/n, and there are n people. Node averaged over all 120 permutations
of 5 and got exactly 1. The expected number of inversions in a random permutation is n(n minus 1)/4,
which is 5 for n equal to 5.

#### Average-case complexity

Chapter 3's average case is an expected value. For linear search on n elements where x is present with
probability p, at a uniform position, the expected number of comparisons is p(n plus 1)/2 plus (1 minus
p)n. For n equal to 100 and p equal to 0.6, that is 70.3.

#### The geometric distribution

If each attempt succeeds independently with probability p, the number of attempts until the first
success X has p(X equal to k) equal to (1 minus p)ᵏ⁻¹p, the **geometric distribution**, and E(X) is 1/p.
Waiting for a six takes 6 rolls on average. A request that succeeds 80 percent of the time needs 1.25
attempts on average, and more than 3 attempts with probability 0.2³, which is 0.008.

#### Independent random variables

X and Y are **independent** if p(X equal to r1 and Y equal to r2) is p(X equal to r1)p(Y equal to r2) for
all r1, r2. Then E(XY) is E(X)E(Y). Without independence this fails: for a die X, E(X·X) is 91/6, about
15.17, but E(X)² is 12.25.

#### Variance and standard deviation

The **variance** of X is V(X), the expected value of (X minus E(X))², and the **standard deviation** σ(X)
is its square root. A shortcut: V(X) is E(X²) minus E(X)². For a die, 91/6 minus 49/4 is 35/12, about
2.92.

**Bienaymé's formula**: if X1, ..., Xₙ are pairwise independent, the variance of the sum is the sum of the
variances. For n Bernoulli trials, V is npq. For 10 trials with p equal to 0.3, V is 2.1.

#### Chebyshev's inequality

For any random variable X and positive r, p(|X minus E(X)| at least r) is at most V(X)/r². It needs
nothing but the variance, so it is weak but universal.

For 100 fair flips, E is 50 and V is 25. Chebyshev bounds the chance of 10 or more away from 50 by
25/100, which is 0.25. The true probability, from the binomial, is about 0.057.

#### Expected cost

Expectations are the language of cost models. A cache with a 90 percent hit rate, 1 millisecond hits and
20 millisecond misses has expected latency 0.9·1 plus 0.1·20, which is 2.9 milliseconds. Raising the hit
rate to 95 percent drops it to 1.95. The expectation tells you where tuning pays.

It also hides things. The 2.9 millisecond average says nothing about the one request in ten that takes
20; for that you need the distribution, or at least the variance and the tail percentiles. This is the
reason latency dashboards show p50, p99 and p99.9 instead of just the mean.

#### Variance of the geometric distribution

The number of attempts until success, with success probability p, has variance (1 minus p)/p². For p
equal to 0.8 this is 0.3125, so attempts cluster tightly around the mean of 1.25. For p equal to 0.1, the
mean is 10 and the variance 90: retries become not just slower on average but much less predictable.

#### Markov's inequality

Chebyshev comes from a simpler fact. For a non-negative random variable X and positive a, p(X at least a)
is at most E(X)/a. If mean latency is 50 milliseconds, at most 10 percent of requests can take 500 or
more. Applying Markov's inequality to the non-negative variable (X minus E(X))² gives Chebyshev's
inequality directly, which is how the book's proof goes in spirit.

#### Expected running time of randomised algorithms

A randomised algorithm makes random choices, so its running time on a fixed input is a random variable.
Its expected running time is the expectation over those choices, for the worst input. This is a stronger
guarantee than average-case analysis, which averages over inputs: no input is bad for randomised
quicksort, only unlucky coin flips.

For quicksort with a random pivot, the expected number of comparisons comes from linearity and
indicators. Let the indicator for elements of rank i and j (with i less than j) be 1 if they are ever
compared. They are compared exactly when one of them is the first pivot chosen among ranks i to j, which
has probability 2/(j minus i plus 1). Summing over all pairs gives about 2n ln n. No independence was
needed anywhere, only linearity.

A Las Vegas algorithm, like randomised quicksort, always gives the right answer and has a random running
time. A Monte Carlo algorithm, like Miller–Rabin from 7.2, runs in bounded time and has a small chance of
a wrong answer. With 10 independent Miller–Rabin rounds, the error probability is at most 4⁻¹⁰, about
one in a million.

### Worked example

**Simple: expectations directly.**

```ts
const E = (xs: number[], ps: number[]) => xs.reduce((a, x, i) => a + x * ps[i], 0);
console.log(E([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], Array(6).fill(1 / 6)));
console.log(E([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], [1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 1].map(x => x / 7)));
let em = 0;
for (let a = 1; a <= 6; a++) for (let b = 1; b <= 6; b++) em += Math.max(a, b) / 36;
console.log('E(max of two dice):', em.toFixed(4));
```

```text
3.5
3.4285714285714284
E(max of two dice): 4.4722
```

**Realistic: the coupon collector.** How many random draws, each uniformly one of n types, until you have
seen every type? Once you have i minus 1 types, the wait for a new one is geometric with success
probability (n minus i plus 1)/n, so expected n/(n minus i plus 1) draws. Summing by linearity gives n(1
plus 1/2 plus ... plus 1/n). For 10 types, about 29.3 draws. This is how long random load-balancing
takes to hit every server, or random testing to hit every branch.

**Realistic: Chebyshev versus the truth.** 1,000 requests each fail independently with probability 0.01.
The mean is 10 and the variance 9.9. Chebyshev says 30 or more failures has probability at most 9.9/400,
about 0.025. The binomial gives about 2 in 10 million. Chebyshev is a guarantee, not an estimate.

### Common mistake

**Adding variances of dependent variables.** The wrong way: the variance of X plus X for a die is 2V(X).
Why it fails: X plus X is 2X, whose variance is 4V(X). Bienaymé's formula needs independence.

The fix: expectations always add; variances add only for (pairwise) independent variables.

**Treating the expected value as typical.** The expected loss of a rare large failure can be small even
when the failure itself is catastrophic. Look at the distribution, not just the mean.

### In real systems

Expected values drive capacity planning (average load), cost models (expected retries, expected cache
hit cost), and algorithm choice: quicksort's expected comparisons are about 2n ln n, roughly 13,800 for
1,000 elements, which is why it is used despite a quadratic worst case. Variance drives tail latency and
SLOs; Chebyshev-style bounds justify sizing for the mean plus a few standard deviations.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 7, Discrete Probability">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version: list the equally likely outcomes, count the ones you want, divide. For
anything with "at least one", compute 1 minus the chance of none.

Worked example: the chance at least one of 3 independent services is down, each down 1 percent of the
time, is 1 minus 0.99³, about 3 percent. Three times the risk of one service, roughly.

The beginner mistake is assuming outcomes are equally likely when they are not, like sums of dice. The
fix is to go back to a sample space where they are, such as ordered pairs.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level you combine tools: conditional probability to update on information, the binomial for
repeated independent trials, and expectations to summarise.

Example: a retry policy with 3 attempts, each succeeding with probability 0.8 independently. The request
fails only if all 3 fail: 0.2³, which is 0.008. The expected number of attempts is 1 plus 0.2 plus 0.04,
which is 1.24.

```ts
const p = 0.8;
console.log((1 - p) ** 3, 1 + (1 - p) + (1 - p) ** 2);
```

```text
0.008000000000000002 1.24
```

The mistake is assuming retries are independent when the failure is a dependency outage; then all three
fail together. The fix is backoff, jitter and circuit breakers, and measuring correlation.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath, a probability space is a sample space with a distribution, events are subsets, and random
variables are functions. Every identity in the chapter is a statement about sums over outcomes, which is
why proofs are short: linearity splits a sum, Bayes rearranges a definition, Chebyshev bounds a sum term
by term.

The tools generalise: Markov's inequality (p(X at least a) at most E(X)/a for non-negative X) is the
parent of Chebyshev, and Chernoff bounds give exponentially small tails for sums of independent
variables, which is what randomised algorithm analysis uses.

The mistake is applying a theorem without its hypothesis: Bienaymé without independence, Chebyshev's
square without a variance. The fix is to name the hypothesis every time.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **p(F) equal to 0.** p(E | F) is undefined; code must not divide by zero.
- **Empty events.** Probability 0; certain events have probability 1.
- **One trial.** The binomial reduces to Bernoulli.
- **p equal to 0 or 1.** The geometric distribution's mean 1/p is infinite at p equal to 0: the success
  never comes.
- **Small samples.** A spam filter that has seen a word once has wildly unreliable probabilities;
  smoothing adds a pseudo-count.
- **Ties and equal priors.** Bayes with prior 0.5 reduces to comparing likelihoods.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **Pairwise but not mutual independence.** Three events can be pairwise independent but not mutually
  independent: two fair coins and the event "they match".
- **Underflow.** Products of hundreds of small probabilities round to 0; sum logs.
- **Pseudorandom bias.** A weak generator skews simulations: a quick LCG simulation of 100 fair flips
  averaged 51.2 heads instead of 50 in my run, which is enough to mislead.
- **Sampling with and without replacement.** Drawing without replacement makes trials dependent; the
  binomial becomes hypergeometric.
- **Conditioning on a selected subset.** Survivorship bias: analysing only completed requests hides the
  ones that timed out.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, probability shows up as SLOs, error budgets, sampling and alerting. A 99.9 percent
availability target allows about 43 minutes of downtime a month; a chain of five independent 99.9 percent
dependencies gives about 99.5 percent. Design for independence (separate zones, staggered deploys) and
measure correlation.

Alerts should be judged by precision as well as recall. Bayes says a detector for rare events needs a
very low false positive rate, or on-call engineers learn to ignore it.

The mistake is using `Math.random()` for anything that needs to be unpredictable or reproducible. Use a
cryptographic generator for security and a seeded high-quality generator such as PCG for simulations and
tests.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit is to ask "out of how many?" and "given what?" for every probability you hear. "98 percent
accurate" means nothing until you know the base rate and which conditional it is.

The second habit is to simulate when intuition and calculation disagree. Monty Hall, the birthday problem
and the hat-check problem all feel wrong; a hundred thousand simulated runs settle it in seconds.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In a larger system, probability shapes redundancy and randomness. Replication factor three with
independent failures makes data loss extremely rare; correlated failures are why placement across fault
domains matters. Randomised algorithms, such as consistent hashing with virtual nodes, power-of-two-choices
load balancing, Bloom filters and HyperLogLog, trade exactness for speed with probabilities you can
compute.

Concrete system: a Bloom filter in front of a database. With m bits, k hash functions and n items, the
false positive rate is about (1 minus e^(minus kn/m))ᵏ. Choosing m and k from that formula sets how many
needless lookups reach the database.

The mistake is treating a probabilistic structure as exact. The fix is to document its error rate and
handle the false positives downstream.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

I learn this chapter by computing every example three ways: by counting, by formula, and by simulation
with a seeded generator. When the three agree, I understand it; when they disagree, I have found my
mistake.

Then I do the Bayes problems with natural frequencies out of 10,000 until the base-rate effect is
automatic, and I prove linearity, Bayes and Chebyshev from the definitions.

Done when I can compute a binomial tail, a Bayes posterior and a coupon-collector expectation from a blank
page, and explain each to someone who has not seen them.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**why does the birthday problem need so few people?**

Because it counts pairs, not people. 23 people make C(23, 2), which is 253, pairs, and each pair has a
1/365 chance of matching.

**is linearity of expectation really true without independence?**

Yes. It is just splitting a sum over outcomes. Independence is needed for products and for adding
variances, not for adding expectations.

**why do spam filters work if the naive independence assumption is false?**

They only need to rank spam above ham. The probabilities are miscalibrated, but the ordering is usually
right.

**when is Chebyshev's inequality useful if it is so loose?**

When you know only the mean and variance, or need a guarantee that holds for every distribution. With
more structure, such as independence, sharper bounds exist.

## Key takeaways

- With equally likely outcomes, probability is favourable over total; complements handle "at least one".
- A distribution weights outcomes; event probabilities are sums of weights.
- p(E | F) is p(E ∩ F)/p(F); independence means the product rule holds.
- n independent Bernoulli trials give the binomial distribution C(n, k)pᵏqⁿ⁻ᵏ.
- Collisions become likely after about the square root of the number of possible values.
- Bayes' theorem reverses conditionals; base rates dominate when the condition is rare.
- Expectation is linear, always; indicator variables turn counts into sums of probabilities.
- Variance adds for independent variables; Chebyshev bounds deviations using only the variance.

## Glossary

- **Experiment, sample space, event**: a procedure; its set of outcomes; a subset of outcomes.
- **Laplace's definition**: p(E) is |E|/|S| for equally likely outcomes.
- **Complement**: the outcomes not in E; p is 1 minus p(E).
- **Probability distribution**: non-negative weights on outcomes summing to 1.
- **Uniform distribution**: every outcome equally likely.
- **Conditional probability**: p(E | F) equal to p(E ∩ F)/p(F).
- **Independent events**: p(E ∩ F) equal to p(E)p(F).
- **Mutually independent**: the product rule holds for every subcollection.
- **Bernoulli trial**: an experiment with two outcomes.
- **Binomial distribution**: probabilities of k successes in n trials.
- **Random variable**: a function from outcomes to real numbers.
- **Birthday problem**: the chance some pair shares a value.
- **Monte Carlo algorithm**: fast, with a small chance of a wrong answer.
- **Probabilistic method**: proving existence by showing positive probability.
- **Bayes' theorem**: p(F | E) from p(E | F), p(F) and p(E).
- **Law of total probability**: p(E) as a sum over a partition.
- **Base rate fallacy**: ignoring the prior.
- **Naive Bayes filter**: multiplying word likelihoods assuming independence given the class.
- **Expected value**: the probability-weighted average.
- **Linearity of expectation**: E of a sum is the sum of Es.
- **Indicator random variable**: 1 if an event happens, 0 otherwise.
- **Geometric distribution**: trials until the first success; mean 1/p.
- **Variance, standard deviation**: expected squared deviation; its square root.
- **Bienaymé's formula**: variances add for pairwise independent variables.
- **Chebyshev's inequality**: p(|X minus E(X)| at least r) at most V(X)/r².
