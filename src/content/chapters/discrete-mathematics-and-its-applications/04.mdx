## 4.1 Divisibility and modular arithmetic

### In one sentence

a divides b when b is a multiple of a, every integer divided by a positive d leaves a unique remainder
between 0 and d minus 1, and two integers are congruent modulo m when they leave the same remainder.

### The idea

#### Divisibility

For integers a and b with a not zero, **a divides b**, written a | b, if there is an integer c with b
equal to ac. Then a is a **factor** or divisor of b, and b is a **multiple** of a. So 3 | 12, and 3 does
not divide 7. Every integer divides 0, because 0 is a times 0.

Three properties do most of the work. If a | b and a | c then a | (b plus c). If a | b then a | bc for any
c. If a | b and b | c then a | c. A useful consequence: if a | b and a | c, then a divides mb plus nc for
any integers m and n.

#### The division algorithm

For any integer a and positive integer d there are unique integers q and r with a equal to dq plus r and
r between 0 and d minus 1. q is the **quotient**, written a div d, and r is the **remainder**, written a
mod d. It is called an algorithm by tradition; it is a theorem.

101 divided by 11 gives quotient 9 and remainder 2, since 101 is 11 times 9 plus 2. For negative numbers
the remainder must still be non-negative: minus 11 divided by 3 gives quotient minus 4 and remainder 1,
since minus 11 is 3 times minus 4 plus 1.

#### Congruence modulo m

For a positive integer m, a is **congruent to b modulo m**, written a ≡ b (mod m), if m divides a minus
b. Equivalently, a mod m equals b mod m. m is the **modulus**. So 17 ≡ 5 (mod 6), because 6 divides 12,
and 24 is not congruent to 14 modulo 6, because 6 does not divide 10.

Notice the two uses of "mod". In "a mod m" it is an operation that returns a number. In "a ≡ b (mod m)"
it labels a relation between a and b. Mixing them up is the commonest early confusion.

#### Arithmetic with congruences

If a ≡ b and c ≡ d modulo m, then a plus c ≡ b plus d and ac ≡ bd modulo m. So you may reduce at any
point during a computation of sums and products and get the same remainder at the end. That is why
computers can compute huge expressions modulo m without huge numbers.

Division does *not* carry over in general. 14 ≡ 8 (mod 6), and dividing both sides by 2 gives 7 and 4,
which are not congruent mod 6. Section 4.4 explains when division is allowed.

#### Arithmetic modulo m as its own system

**ℤₘ** is the set 0, 1, ..., m minus 1 with addition and multiplication followed by reducing mod m. These
operations, written plus-sub-m and times-sub-m, keep all the familiar laws: closure, associativity,
commutativity, identities 0 and 1, additive inverses, and distributivity. What can fail is the
multiplicative inverse: in ℤ6, 2 has no inverse.

### Worked example

**Simple: div and mod in code.** JavaScript's `%` is a remainder that takes the sign of the left operand,
not the mathematical mod.

```ts
const mod = (a: number, m: number) => ((a % m) + m) % m;
console.log(Math.floor(101 / 11), 101 % 11);
console.log(Math.floor(-11 / 3), mod(-11, 3), 'js %:', -11 % 3);
console.log(mod(17 - 5, 6) === 0, mod(24 - 14, 6) === 0);
```

```text
9 2
-4 1 js %: -2
true false
```

**Realistic: what day of the week.** Today is a Friday. What day is it 1,000 days from now? Days repeat
every 7, so only 1,000 mod 7 matters, which is 6. Six days after Friday is Thursday. Every "clock" or
"wrap around" calculation in software is arithmetic mod m: ring buffers, rotating log files, round-robin
assignment.

**Realistic: reducing as you go.** To find the last digit of 7 to the 100, work mod 10. Powers of 7 end
in 7, 9, 3, 1 and repeat, because 7 to the 4 is 2,401, which is 1 mod 10. 100 is a multiple of 4, so the
last digit is 1.

Prove: if a ≡ b and c ≡ d modulo m, then ac ≡ bd modulo m. By the theorem that a ≡ b exactly when a is b
plus km for some k, write a as b plus sm and c as d plus tm. Then ac is bd plus m(bt plus ds plus stm).
That is bd plus a multiple of m, so ac ≡ bd.

### Common mistake

**Trusting `%` with negative numbers.** The wrong way: `index = (i - 1) % n` to step backwards around a
ring buffer. For i equal to 0 it gives minus 1 in JavaScript, Java and C, and the read goes out of
bounds. Why it fails: those languages truncate towards zero, so the remainder takes the sign of the
dividend.

The fix is `((i - 1) % n + n) % n`, or a helper like `mod` above. Python's `%` already follows the
mathematical definition.

**Dividing both sides of a congruence.** As 14 ≡ 8 (mod 6) shows, you can only cancel a factor that is
coprime to the modulus. Section 4.4 makes this precise.

### In real systems

Modular arithmetic is how fixed-width integers behave: a 32-bit unsigned add is addition modulo 2 to the
32, and overflow is just reduction. Hash table indexing, sharding by `id mod shards`, cyclic schedules,
time zones and calendar arithmetic all use it.

Sharding by mod has one well-known cost: changing the number of shards changes almost every key's
remainder, so almost every key moves. Consistent hashing exists to avoid that.

## 4.2 Integer representations and algorithms

### In one sentence

Every positive integer has a unique expansion in any base b greater than 1, and the grade-school
algorithms for adding and multiplying, plus repeated squaring for powers, work in any base.

### The idea

#### Base b expansions

Let b be an integer greater than 1. Every positive integer n can be written uniquely as aₖbᵏ plus
aₖ₋₁bᵏ⁻¹ plus ... plus a1b plus a0, where each digit aᵢ is between 0 and b minus 1 and aₖ is not zero.
This is the **base b expansion** of n.

Base 10 is decimal, base 2 is **binary**, base 8 is **octal** and base 16 is **hexadecimal**, which uses
A to F for 10 to 15. Computers store binary; hex is the compact way to write it, since each hex digit is
exactly four bits.

#### Converting to base b

Divide n by b; the remainder is the last digit. Replace n by the quotient and repeat until the quotient is
0. The digits come out right to left. Uniqueness of the expansion comes from uniqueness in the division
algorithm.

#### Converting between binary, octal and hex

Because 8 is 2 cubed and 16 is 2 to the 4, group binary digits in threes or fours from the right. 241 in
binary is 1111 0001, which is F1 in hex; in threes, 11 110 001 is 361 in octal.

#### Addition and multiplication in binary

Binary addition works column by column with a carry, exactly as in decimal: digit plus digit plus carry
gives a sum bit (the total mod 2) and a carry (the total div 2). Adding two n-bit numbers takes O(n) bit
operations.

Multiplication is shift and add: for each 1 bit of b, add a shifted copy of a. That takes O(n²) bit
operations for n-bit numbers. Faster methods exist, which matters for the thousands-of-bits numbers in
cryptography.

#### Fast modular exponentiation

Computing bⁿ mod m by multiplying b n times is hopeless for n with hundreds of digits. **Repeated
squaring** writes n in binary. It keeps a running power, squared each step (b, b², b⁴, b⁸, ...), and
multiplies it into the answer whenever the current bit of n is 1. Reducing mod m after every
multiplication keeps numbers small.

The number of steps is the number of bits of n, so O(log n) multiplications. This one algorithm is what
makes RSA practical.

```ts
function modPow(b: bigint, e: bigint, m: bigint) {
  let x = 1n, p = b % m;
  while (e > 0n) {
    if (e & 1n) x = (x * p) % m;
    p = (p * p) % m;
    e >>= 1n;
  }
  return x;
}
console.log(modPow(5n, 117n, 19n));
```

```text
1n
```

### Common mistake

**Off-by-one in bit widths.** The wrong way: assuming an integer fits in a type because its decimal
digits look small. 2 to the 31 does not fit in a signed 32-bit integer. Why it fails: n bits hold 2ⁿ
values, and signed types spend one on the sign.

The fix is to compute the bits needed, which is the floor of log₂ n plus 1, and to use `BigInt` in
JavaScript for anything beyond 2 to the 53.

**Computing the full power and then reducing.** `(b ** e) % m` overflows or takes forever for big e.
Reduce after every multiplication.

### In real systems

Hex dumps, colour codes, file permissions in octal (`chmod 755`), IPv4 addresses as four base-256
digits, and bit flags in a byte are all base conversions. Negative numbers are stored in **two's
complement**, which is arithmetic mod 2ⁿ: minus 5 in 8 bits is 11111011, that is 256 minus 5.

Modular exponentiation is in every TLS handshake. Constant-time versions exist because the plain version
takes slightly longer when a bit is 1, and attackers have recovered keys by timing it.

## 4.3 Primes and greatest common divisors

### In one sentence

Every integer greater than 1 is a product of primes in exactly one way, there are infinitely many primes,
and the greatest common divisor of two numbers is found fast by Euclid's algorithm and can always be
written as a combination of them.

### The idea

#### Primes and the fundamental theorem

An integer p greater than 1 is **prime** if its only positive factors are 1 and p. Otherwise, if greater
than 1, it is **composite**. The **fundamental theorem of arithmetic** says every integer greater than 1
is a prime or a product of primes, uniquely up to order. 100 is 2·2·5·5 and 7,007 is 7·7·11·13.

Existence is proved by strong induction in chapter 5. Uniqueness needs a lemma proved at the end of this
section.

#### Trial division

If n is composite, it has a prime factor at most √n. Proof: if n equals ab with both a and b greater
than √n, then ab is greater than n, a contradiction. So to test n you only try primes up to √n. 641 is
prime because no prime up to 25 divides it.

#### The sieve of Eratosthenes

To list all primes up to n, write 2 to n, then for each remaining number p up to √n, cross out its
multiples from p² on. What survives is prime. Up to 100 that leaves 25 primes. Up to a million, Node
finds 78,498.

#### Infinitely many primes

Euclid's proof: suppose p1, ..., pₙ were all the primes. Let Q be their product plus 1. Q is greater than
1, so by the fundamental theorem some prime divides Q. That prime is one of the pᵢ, and it divides the
product, so it divides Q minus the product, which is 1. No prime divides 1, a contradiction.

Note what the proof does not say: Q itself need not be prime. It only has a prime factor missing from the
list.

#### How primes are distributed

The **prime number theorem** says the number of primes up to x is approximately x / ln x, with the ratio
tending to 1. At a million, x / ln x is 72,382 against the true 78,498, within 8 percent. So a random
number near x is prime with probability about 1 / ln x, which is why random primes of hundreds of digits
are easy to find.

Mersenne primes have the form 2ᵖ minus 1. 2ᵖ minus 1 can only be prime when p is prime, but not always
then: 2 to the 11 minus 1 is 2,047, which is 23 times 89. The largest known primes are Mersenne primes,
because a special fast test exists for them.

Open problems are easy to state: Goldbach's conjecture (every even integer greater than 2 is a sum of two
primes) and the twin prime conjecture (infinitely many primes p with p plus 2 prime) remain unproved.

#### Greatest common divisor and least common multiple

The **gcd** of a and b, not both 0, is the largest integer dividing both. a and b are **relatively
prime** if their gcd is 1. The **lcm** is the smallest positive integer both divide. From prime
factorisations, gcd takes the minimum exponent of each prime and lcm the maximum, which proves ab equals
gcd times lcm.

84 is 2²·3·7 and 120 is 2³·3·5, so gcd is 2²·3, which is 12, and lcm is 2³·3·5·7, which is 840. 12 times
840 is 10,080, which is 84 times 120.

#### The Euclidean algorithm

Factoring is slow, so gcds are computed with a lemma instead: if a equals bq plus r, then gcd(a, b)
equals gcd(b, r). Repeat with the remainder until it is 0; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd.

```ts
function gcd(a: number, b: number) {
  while (b) [a, b] = [b, a % b];
  return a;
}
console.log(gcd(1071, 462), gcd(84, 120));
```

```text
21 12
```

The number of divisions is O(log b). The worst case is consecutive Fibonacci numbers: gcd(377, 233) takes
12 divisions.

#### Bézout's theorem

If a and b are positive integers, there are integers s and t with gcd(a, b) equal to sa plus tb. s and t
are **Bézout coefficients**. The extended Euclidean algorithm finds them by running Euclid's steps and
tracking each remainder as a combination of a and b.

For 240 and 46 the gcd is 2, and 2 equals minus 9 times 240 plus 47 times 46: minus 2,160 plus 2,162.

```ts
function egcd(a: number, b: number): [number, number, number] {
  if (b === 0) return [a, 1, 0];

  return [g, y, x - Math.floor(a / b) * y];
```

```ts
  return f;
}
```

Uniqueness of factorisation rests on **Euclid's lemma**: if p is prime and p | ab, then p | a or p | b.
Proof: if p does not divide a, gcd(p, a) is 1, so by Bézout 1 equals sp plus ta. Multiply by b: b equals
spb plus tab. p divides both terms, so p | b. Then, if a number had two different factorisations, a prime
from one would divide the product of the other, hence one of its primes, hence equal it; cancel and
repeat.

### Common mistake

**Thinking Euclid's Q is prime.** The wrong way: "2·3·5·7·11·13 plus 1 is prime by Euclid's argument."
It is 30,031, which is 59 times 509. Why it fails: the proof only shows Q has a prime factor not on the
list.

The fix is to read the proof's conclusion carefully: a new prime exists, not that Q is it.

**Testing primality up to n instead of √n.** Correct but needlessly slow. And for cryptographic sizes even
√n is impossible; real systems use probabilistic tests such as Miller–Rabin.

### In real systems

Every RSA key needs two large random primes, found by generating random odd numbers and testing them
probabilistically. The prime number theorem says a 1,024-bit candidate is prime with probability about 1
in 710, so a few hundred tries suffice.

gcds appear in simplifying fractions, aspect ratios, gear and schedule periods (two jobs every 84 and 120
minutes coincide every lcm, 840 minutes), and in attacks: keys generated with weak randomness have shared
a prime, and computing gcds across many public keys has broken them.

## 4.4 Solving congruences

### In one sentence

A linear congruence ax ≡ b (mod m) is solved by multiplying by an inverse of a, which exists exactly when
a and m are coprime, and the Chinese remainder theorem solves several congruences with coprime moduli at
once.

### The idea

#### Linear congruences and inverses

A **linear congruence** is ax ≡ b (mod m), with x unknown. An integer ā with āa ≡ 1 (mod m) is an
**inverse of a modulo m**. If one exists, multiplying both sides by it gives x ≡ āb.

The theorem: if a and m are relatively prime and m is greater than 1, an inverse of a modulo m exists and
is unique modulo m. Proof: Bézout gives sa plus tm equal to 1, and reducing mod m kills tm, so sa ≡ 1.
The extended Euclidean algorithm computes it.

7 and 40 are coprime. Extended Euclid gives minus 17 times 7 plus 3 times 40 equal to 1, so minus 17,
which is 23 mod 40, is an inverse of 7. To solve 7x ≡ 3 (mod 40), multiply by 23: x ≡ 69, which is 29
mod 40. Check: 7 times 29 is 203, which is 3 mod 40.

When gcd(a, m) is d greater than 1, ax ≡ b has solutions only if d | b, and then d of them mod m. 4x ≡ 2
(mod 6) has solutions 2 and 5; 4x ≡ 3 (mod 6) has none.

#### The Chinese remainder theorem

If m1, ..., mₙ are pairwise relatively prime, the system x ≡ aᵢ (mod mᵢ) has a solution, unique modulo
their product M. Construction: let Mᵢ be M divided by mᵢ, and yᵢ an inverse of Mᵢ modulo mᵢ. Then x is
the sum of aᵢMᵢyᵢ, reduced mod M.

Why it works: modulo mₖ, every term but the k-th contains mₖ as a factor in Mᵢ, so it vanishes, and the
k-th term is aₖ times Mₖyₖ, which is aₖ times 1.

#### Back substitution

The same system can be solved by hand without the formula: write x as a1 plus m1·t from the first
congruence, substitute into the second, solve for t, and continue. It is often faster for small numbers.

#### Arithmetic with large integers

CRT also lets a computer do arithmetic on huge numbers by working modulo several small coprime moduli in
parallel. Each number is represented by its tuple of remainders; addition and multiplication act
component-wise, and CRT converts back at the end.

#### Fermat's little theorem

If p is prime and p does not divide a, then aᵖ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod p). So aᵖ ≡ a (mod p) for every a. This cuts
exponents down: to compute 4 to the 100 mod 13, reduce 100 mod 12 to 4, and 4 to the 4 is 256, which is 9
mod 13.

#### Pseudoprimes and Carmichael numbers

The converse fails. A composite n with bⁿ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod n) is a **pseudoprime** to base b. 341 is 11 times
31 and still 2 to the 340 ≡ 1 (mod 341). A **Carmichael number** is a pseudoprime to every base coprime
to it; 561, which is 3·11·17, is the smallest. So a Fermat test alone cannot certify primes.

#### Primitive roots and discrete logarithms

A **primitive root** modulo a prime p is an r whose powers r, r², ..., rᵖ⁻¹ hit every non-zero residue.
2 is a primitive root mod 29. Then every non-zero a equals rᵉ for a unique e between 1 and p minus 1,
called the **discrete logarithm** of a to the base r. Computing powers is fast; computing discrete
logarithms is believed hard, which section 4.6 uses.

### Worked example

**Simple: an inverse.**

```ts
const mod = (a: number, m: number) => ((a % m) + m) % m;
const inv = (a: number, m: number) => mod(egcd(a, m)[1], m);
console.log(inv(7, 40), mod(inv(7, 40) * 3, 40));
```

```text
23 29
```

(`egcd` is the function from 4.3.)

### Common mistake

**Using the CRT formula with moduli that are not coprime.** The wrong way: x ≡ 1 (mod 4) and x ≡ 2

### In real systems

times faster, then combine. Modular inverses appear in RSA key generation and elliptic-curve arithmetic.

CRT also explains cycle alignment: tasks that repeat every 4, 5 and 7 minutes realign every 140 minutes,
and CRT tells you exactly when a given combination of phases occurs.

## 4.5 Applications of congruences

### In one sentence

Congruences assign records to hash table slots, generate sequences that look random, and build check
digits that catch the commonest typing errors.

### The idea

#### Hashing functions

A **hashing function** maps a key k, such as a customer number, to one of m memory locations. The
classic choice is h(k) equal to k mod m. It is fast, and it spreads keys evenly if m is chosen well,
usually a prime not close to a power of 2.

Because there are more possible keys than slots, two keys can map to the same slot: a **collision**. A
simple policy, **linear probing**, puts the second key in the next free slot, h(k) plus 1, plus 2, and so
on, modulo m. Other policies chain a list at each slot.

#### Pseudorandom numbers

The **linear congruential method** picks a modulus m, a multiplier a, an increment c and a seed x0, then
produces xₙ₊₁ equal to (axₙ plus c) mod m. The sequence is completely determined, so it is
**pseudorandom**: it only looks random. It must eventually repeat, with period at most m.

When c is 0 it is a **pure multiplicative generator**. A well-known choice is m equal to 2 to the 31
minus 1, a prime, and a equal to 7 to the 5, which gives period m minus 1.

#### Check digits

A **check digit** is an extra digit computed from the others by a congruence, so a reader can detect
errors. The parity bit appended to a bit string, making the number of 1s even, is the simplest: it
catches any single flipped bit, but not two.

ISBN-10 uses mod 11. With digits x1 to x10, the sum of i·xᵢ must be 0 mod 11, which works out to x10
being the sum of i·xᵢ for i from 1 to 9, reduced mod 11, written X when it is 10. Because 11 is prime and
the weights are distinct, it detects every single-digit error and every swap of two adjacent digits.

The Luhn algorithm used on payment card numbers works mod 10: double every second digit from the right,
subtract 9 from any result over 9, and require the total to be 0 mod 10. It catches all single-digit
errors and most adjacent swaps.

## 4.6 Cryptography

### In one sentence

Classical ciphers shift or scale letters modulo 26 and are easy to break, while RSA builds a public
encryption key from two secret primes so that anyone can encrypt but only the owner, who can compute an
inverse modulo (p minus 1)(q minus 1), can decrypt.

### The idea

#### Classical cryptography

A **shift cipher** replaces each letter p, numbered 0 to 25, with (p plus k) mod 26. With k equal to 3 it
is Caesar's cipher. Decryption subtracts k. An **affine cipher** uses (ap plus b) mod 26, with a coprime
to 26 so that a has an inverse and decryption works.

Both are **monoalphabetic**: each letter always maps to the same letter. They fall to **frequency
analysis**, because E, T and A are the most common letters in English text, and to brute force, since a
shift cipher has only 26 keys and an affine cipher 312.

A **block cipher** replaces blocks of letters rather than single letters, and a **transposition cipher**
permutes positions. A **cryptosystem** formally is a five-tuple of plaintexts, ciphertexts, keys,
encryption functions and decryption functions.

#### Private and public keys

In **private key** cryptography, both sides share one secret key, which they must agree on in advance.
In **public key** cryptography, everyone can know the encryption key; only the decryption key is secret.
The difficulty is building an encryption that is easy to compute and hard to reverse without the secret.

#### RSA

Choose two large primes p and q, and let n be pq. Choose e coprime to (p minus 1)(q minus 1). The public
key is (n, e). The private key is d, an inverse of e modulo (p minus 1)(q minus 1).

To encrypt a message M, a number less than n, compute C equal to Mᵉ mod n. To decrypt, compute Cᵈ mod n,
which equals M. Both steps are fast modular exponentiation from 4.2. Finding d needs (p minus 1)(q minus
1), which needs p and q, which needs factoring n.

#### Why decryption works

de is 1 plus k(p minus 1)(q minus 1) for some k. If p does not divide M, Fermat gives Mᵖ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod p), so
Cᵈ, which is Mᵈᵉ, equals M times (Mᵖ⁻¹) to a power, which is M mod p. If p divides M both sides are 0
mod p. The same holds mod q. By CRT, since p and q are coprime, Cᵈ ≡ M (mod n).

#### Why it is believed secure

Nobody knows a fast way to factor a product of two large primes, and no fast way to compute d without
factoring has been found. Nobody has proved factoring is hard; RSA's security rests on decades of failed
attempts. A large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would factor fast, which is why new
"post-quantum" schemes are being deployed.

#### Key exchange and signatures

**Diffie–Hellman key exchange** lets two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel. They
agree publicly on a prime p and a primitive root g. One picks a secret a and sends gᵃ mod p; the other
picks b and sends gᵇ mod p. Each raises what they receive to their own secret, and both get gᵃᵇ mod p. An
eavesdropper would need a discrete logarithm.

An RSA **digital signature** reverses the roles: the owner computes S equal to Mᵈ mod n with the private
key; anyone checks Sᵉ mod n equals M with the public key. Only the holder of d could have produced S.

### Common mistake

**Textbook RSA in production.** The wrong way: encrypting messages directly as Mᵉ mod n. Why it fails:
it is deterministic, so the same message always gives the same ciphertext; small messages with small e
can be recovered by taking an ordinary root; and ciphertexts can be multiplied to forge related ones.

The fix is padding schemes (OAEP for encryption, PSS for signatures), and in practice using RSA or
Diffie–Hellman only to agree on a key for a fast symmetric cipher such as AES.

**Rolling your own crypto.** Even correct maths leaks through timing, error messages and bad randomness.
Use vetted libraries.

### In real systems

A TLS handshake uses Diffie–Hellman (usually on elliptic curves) to agree a key, RSA or elliptic-curve
signatures to prove the server's identity via certificates, and a symmetric cipher for the data. SSH keys,
signed software updates, Git commit signing and JWTs signed with RS256 all use the same ideas.

Key sizes follow the best known factoring algorithms: 2,048-bit RSA is today's common minimum, and
elliptic curves give the same security with much smaller keys.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Number Theory and Cryptography">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version: mod is the remainder, and remainders have their own arithmetic where you
can reduce at any point. Clock arithmetic is the everyday picture: 10 o'clock plus 5 hours is 3 o'clock,
which is 15 mod 12.

Worked example: which of 1,000 users goes to which of 4 servers? `userId mod 4`. User 1,023 goes to
server 3, user 1,024 to server 0.

The beginner mistake is assuming the language's `%` is the mathematical mod. In JavaScript minus 1 % 4 is
minus 1, not 3. The fix is the `((a % m) + m) % m` helper, used everywhere a value can be negative.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level the tools combine: gcd to test coprimality, extended Euclid for an inverse, the inverse to
solve a congruence, and fast powers to compute anything mod m. A small library of five functions, `mod`,
`gcd`, `egcd`, `inv` and `modPow`, covers the whole chapter.

Example: a week-long schedule repeats every 7 days and a shift rota every 9. Which day does rota slot 4
next fall on a Monday (day 0)? Solve x ≡ 0 (mod 7) and x ≡ 4 (mod 9) with CRT; Node gives 49.

```ts
console.log([...Array(63).keys()].find(x => x % 7 === 0 && x % 9 === 4));
```

```text
49
```

The mistake is reaching for CRT's formula when moduli share a factor. The fix is to check gcds first.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath, the chapter rests on three proofs: the division algorithm (well-ordering, chapter 5), Bézout
(from Euclid's steps), and Euclid's lemma (from Bézout). Everything else, unique factorisation, inverses,
CRT, Fermat and RSA's correctness, is built from those.

The structure is algebraic: ℤₘ is a ring, and when m is prime it is a field, which is why every non-zero
element has an inverse modulo a prime. RSA works in the multiplicative group of units mod n, whose size
is (p minus 1)(q minus 1).

The mistake is proving RSA correct only for M coprime to n. The full proof treats p | M separately, as
4.6 does, and uses CRT to combine the two primes.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **m equal to 1.** Everything is congruent to everything; every inverse is 0 and the "ring" has one
  element. Guard against it.
- **gcd(0, 0).** Undefined. Euclid returns 0, which code must treat as a special case.
- **Inverse of 0 or of a non-coprime a.** Does not exist; `inv` must throw, not return garbage.
- **Exponent 0.** modPow returns 1 mod m, which is 0 when m is 1.
- **Check digit X.** ISBN-10's check value 10 is written X; parsers that expect digits only reject valid
  ISBNs.
- **Negative keys in hashing.** `k % m` is negative in many languages and indexes out of bounds.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **Precision.** `2 ** 60 % 7` in JavaScript numbers is wrong because 2 to the 60 is not exactly
  representable past 2 to the 53. Use `BigInt` for anything large.
- **Carmichael numbers.** A Fermat test says 561 is "probably prime". Use Miller–Rabin.
- **RSA with p equal to q.** n is p², (p minus 1)² is the wrong totient, and decryption fails; also n is
  trivially factored by a square root.
- **Small e and small M.** With e equal to 3 and M cubed less than n, C is just M cubed and an ordinary
  cube root recovers M.
- **LCG low bits.** With m a power of 2, the low bit alternates, so `rand() % 2` is a perfectly
  predictable coin.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, number theory lives inside libraries, and the job is to use them correctly. Use the
platform's `crypto` module for keys, random tokens and signatures; never `Math.random()`. Choose standard
parameters: RSA-2048 or larger with OAEP and PSS, or elliptic-curve Ed25519 and X25519.

Rotate keys, store private keys in a key management service, and plan for algorithm changes, since
post-quantum migration is under way. Hash-based sharding needs a plan for resharding: consistent
hashing or rendezvous hashing instead of plain `mod n`.

The mistake is implementing modular arithmetic yourself for security. The fix is vetted, constant-time
libraries and standard protocols such as TLS.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit is to ask "what is invariant under the remainder?" Many problems simplify when you notice only
the remainder matters: last digits, days of the week, parity, divisibility by 9 from digit sums
(987,654,321 has digit sum 45, so it is divisible by 9).

The second habit is to separate easy and hard directions. Multiplying primes, raising to powers and
checking signatures are easy; factoring, discrete logs and forging are believed hard. Security design is
choosing operations with that asymmetry.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In a larger system these ideas shape architecture. Sharding by hash mod n spreads load; consistent
hashing limits movement when n changes. Content-addressed storage (Git, IPFS) uses cryptographic hashes as
names. Idempotency keys and request IDs need secure randomness.

Concrete system: signed webhooks. The sender signs each payload with a private key (or an HMAC secret);
the receiver verifies with the public key and rejects anything older than a few minutes to stop replays.
The maths is 4.6; the design is key distribution, rotation and clock tolerance.

The mistake is designing secrecy into the algorithm rather than the key. The fix is Kerckhoffs's
principle: assume the attacker knows the system and keep only the key secret.

</Level>

## Questions people ask

**is a mod m always non-negative?**

In mathematics, yes: it is between 0 and m minus 1. In many programming languages the `%` operator can
return a negative result, so write a helper.

**why is factoring hard but multiplying easy?**

Multiplying two n-digit numbers takes about n² steps or fewer. The best known classical factoring
algorithms take time that grows faster than any polynomial in the number of digits. Nobody has proved
factoring must be hard.

**does RSA still matter if elliptic curves are preferred?**

Yes. RSA is still widely deployed in certificates and signatures, and its maths, inverses, fast powers and
CRT, is the clearest introduction to public-key ideas.

**why do hash tables like prime sizes?**

k mod a prime uses all the bits of k. k mod a power of 2 keeps only the low bits, which are often
patterned.

## Key takeaways

- a | b means b is a multiple of a; the division algorithm gives a unique quotient and non-negative
  remainder.
- Congruence mod m respects addition and multiplication, so you can reduce at every step; division needs
  an inverse.
- Every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes, and there are infinitely many primes.
- Euclid's algorithm computes gcds in O(log n) steps; extended Euclid gives Bézout coefficients and
  inverses.
- The Chinese remainder theorem solves systems with pairwise coprime moduli.
- Fermat's little theorem cuts exponents mod p minus 1; its converse fails, so primality needs better
  tests.
- Hashing, pseudorandom generators and check digits are congruences in daily use.
- RSA is fast powers mod n with an exponent whose inverse needs the factors of n.

## Glossary

- **Divides, factor, multiple**: a | b means b equals ac for an integer c.
- **Quotient, remainder (div, mod)**: q and r with a equal to dq plus r and r between 0 and d minus 1.
- **Congruent modulo m**: m divides the difference.
- **ℤₘ**: the residues 0 to m minus 1 with arithmetic mod m.
- **Base b expansion**: digits aᵢ with n the sum of aᵢbⁱ.
- **Binary, octal, hexadecimal**: bases 2, 8 and 16.
- **Two's complement**: storing negative numbers as residues mod 2ⁿ.
- **Repeated squaring**: computing powers with O(log n) multiplications.
- **Prime, composite**: only factors 1 and itself; not prime and greater than 1.
- **Fundamental theorem of arithmetic**: unique prime factorisation.
- **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: listing primes by crossing out multiples.
- **Prime number theorem**: about x / ln x primes up to x.
- **Mersenne prime**: a prime of the form 2ᵖ minus 1.
- **gcd, lcm, relatively prime**: largest common divisor; smallest common multiple; gcd 1.
- **Euclidean algorithm**: gcd by repeated remainders.
- **Bézout coefficients**: s and t with gcd equal to sa plus tb.
- **Inverse modulo m**: ā with āa ≡ 1 (mod m).
- **Linear congruence**: ax ≡ b (mod m).
- **Chinese remainder theorem**: unique solution mod the product for pairwise coprime moduli.
- **Fermat's little theorem**: aᵖ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod p) for prime p not dividing a.
- **Pseudoprime, Carmichael number**: composites that pass Fermat tests for one base or for all.
- **Primitive root, discrete logarithm**: a generator of all non-zero residues; the exponent giving a.
- **Hashing function, collision, linear probing**: key to slot; two keys in one slot; try the next slot.
- **Linear congruential generator**: xₙ₊₁ equal to (axₙ plus c) mod m.
- **Check digit, parity bit**: an extra digit or bit detecting errors.
- **Shift, affine cipher**: letters mapped by p plus k or ap plus b mod 26.
- **Private key, public key cryptography**: one shared secret; a public encryption key.
- **RSA**: public (n, e), private d inverse to e mod (p minus 1)(q minus 1).
- **Diffie–Hellman**: key agreement via gᵃᵇ mod p.
- **Digital signature**: Mᵈ mod n, verified with e.

**Self-check.**

**1. When does a have an inverse modulo m?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exactly when gcd(a, m) is 1.

</details>

**2. What does each step of Euclid's algorithm preserve?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The set of common divisors, so the gcd.

</details>

**3. How many multiplications does repeated squaring need for an exponent n?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

O(log n): one squaring per bit and at most one extra multiplication per 1 bit.

</details>

**4. Why does Euclid's proof show infinitely many primes but not that Q is prime?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It shows Q has a prime factor not on the list; Q itself may be composite.

</details>

**5. What does CRT require of the moduli?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That they are pairwise relatively prime.

</details>

**6. In RSA, which values are public?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

n and e. p, q, d and (p minus 1)(q minus 1) are secret.

</details>

**7. Which check digit scheme catches every adjacent swap: Luhn or ISBN-10?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

ISBN-10, because 11 is prime and the weights differ by 1.

</details>
