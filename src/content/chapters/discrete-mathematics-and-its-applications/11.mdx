A tree is a graph with exactly enough edges to hold together and not one more. That single property,
connected with no cycles, makes trees the most used structure in software. File systems, the document
object model, syntax trees in compilers, database indexes, decision procedures, compression codes, game
search, network broadcast and cheapest-wiring problems are all trees. This chapter starts from the
definition and the counting facts that follow from it, then turns to applications (search trees,
decision trees, prefix codes, game trees), the three ways of walking a tree and the expression notations
they produce, spanning trees with depth-first search and backtracking, and finally minimum spanning trees
with Prim's and Kruskal's algorithms. It reuses the graph vocabulary of chapter 10 throughout.

## 11.1 Introduction to trees

### In one sentence

A tree is a connected undirected graph with no simple circuits, which is equivalent to having a unique
simple path between every pair of vertices and to being connected with exactly n minus 1 edges, and
rooted and m-ary trees add a direction and a branching limit that make precise counting possible.

### The idea

#### Trees and forests

A **tree** is a connected undirected graph with no simple circuits. Because it has no circuits, it has no
loops or parallel edges, so a tree is a simple graph. A **forest** is a graph with no simple circuits,
not necessarily connected; each component of a forest is a tree.

**Theorem**: an undirected graph is a tree if and only if there is a unique simple path between any two of
its vertices.

The proof has two halves. If G is a tree, it is connected, so a simple path exists between any two
vertices. If two different simple paths joined x and y, follow them from x until they first split and
then until they first meet again; the two pieces form a simple circuit, which a tree does not have.
Conversely, if every pair has a unique simple path, G is connected, and a simple circuit would give two
different paths between any two of its vertices.

#### Rooted trees

A **rooted tree** is a tree with one vertex chosen as the **root** and every edge directed away from it.
Picking a different root gives a different rooted tree from the same underlying tree. Rooted trees are
drawn with the root at the top.

The family vocabulary is standard:

- If there is an edge from u to v, u is the **parent** of v and v is a **child** of u. The root has no
  parent; every other vertex has exactly one.
- Vertices with the same parent are **siblings**.
- The **ancestors** of v are the vertices on the path from the root to v, excluding v; its
  **descendants** are the vertices that have v as an ancestor.
- A **leaf** has no children; a vertex with children is **internal**. The root is internal unless it is
  the only vertex.
- The **subtree** rooted at v is v with all its descendants and the edges between them.

#### m-ary trees

A rooted tree is an **m-ary tree** if every internal vertex has at most m children, and a **full m-ary
tree** if every internal vertex has exactly m children. An m-ary tree with m equal to 2 is a **binary
tree**. In an **ordered rooted tree** the children of each internal vertex are ordered, drawn left to
right; in an ordered binary tree they are the **left child** and **right child**, roots of the left and
right subtrees.

#### Counting edges

**Theorem**: a tree with n vertices has n minus 1 edges.

Root the tree anywhere. Every vertex except the root has exactly one parent, joined to it by one edge, and
every edge joins a child to its parent. So the edges correspond one-to-one with the non-root vertices,
of which there are n minus 1.

The converse also holds in the useful form: a connected graph with n vertices and n minus 1 edges is a
tree, and so is an acyclic graph with n vertices and n minus 1 edges. Any two of "connected", "acyclic" and
"n minus 1 edges" imply the third.

#### Counting vertices in full m-ary trees

A full m-ary tree with i internal vertices has n equal to mi plus 1 vertices: each internal vertex has m
children, giving mi children in all, and every vertex except the root is a child. From that one equation:

| given | vertices n | internal i | leaves l |
|---|---|---|---|
| n | n | (n minus 1)/m | ((m minus 1)n plus 1)/m |
| i | mi plus 1 | i | (m minus 1)i plus 1 |
| l | (ml minus 1)/(m minus 1) | (l minus 1)/(m minus 1) | l |

A knockout tournament is a full binary tree: each match is an internal vertex and each entrant a leaf. With
l entrants there are l minus 1 matches, whatever the bracket shape.

#### Level, height and balance

The **level** of a vertex is the length of the path from the root to it; the root is at level 0. The
**height** of a rooted tree is the largest level. A rooted m-ary tree of height h is **balanced** if all
its leaves are at levels h or h minus 1.

**Theorem**: an m-ary tree of height h has at most mʰ leaves. By induction on h: a tree of height 0 has one
leaf; a tree of height h has at most m subtrees of the root, each of height at most h minus 1 and so with
at most mʰ⁻¹ leaves.

**Corollary**: an m-ary tree with l leaves has height at least ⌈log_m l⌉; if it is full and balanced, the
height is exactly that. This corollary is behind every logarithmic bound in the chapter: search trees,
decision trees and sorting.

#### How many trees are there?

**Cayley's formula** says there are nⁿ⁻² labelled trees on n vertices (trees on the vertex set 1 to n,
counting different edge sets as different). Equivalently, Kn has nⁿ⁻² spanning trees. The usual proof is a
bijection with sequences of length n minus 2 over n symbols, the Prüfer code.

### Worked example

**Simple: full m-ary counting.** A full ternary (3-ary) tree with 5 internal vertices has 3·5 plus 1, which
is 16 vertices, and (3 minus 1)·5 plus 1, which is 11 leaves.

**Simple: a phone tree.** A team uses a phone chain: the lead calls four people, each of whom calls four
more, and so on. Everyone who calls makes exactly four calls. If 85 people were called or did the calling
in total, then n is 85, m is 4, so i is (85 minus 1)/4, which is 21 people made calls and 85 minus 21, which
is 64, people only received a call. Since 4³ is 64, a balanced chain finishes in three rounds.

**Realistic: check Cayley's formula by brute force.**

```ts
function countTrees(n: number) {
  const E: [number, number][] = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) for (let j = i + 1; j < n; j++) E.push([i, j]);
  let count = 0;
  const pick = (start: number, chosen: number[]) => {
    if (chosen.length === n - 1) {
      const p = [...Array(n).keys()];
      const find = (x: number): number => (p[x] === x ? x : (p[x] = find(p[x])));
      for (const k of chosen) {
        const a = find(E[k][0]), b = find(E[k][1]);
        if (a === b) return;          // this edge closes a cycle
        p[a] = b;
      }
      count++;
      return;
    }
    for (let k = start; k < E.length; k++) { chosen.push(k); pick(k + 1, chosen); chosen.pop(); }
  };
  pick(0, []);
  return count;
}
const ns = [2, 3, 4, 5, 6];
console.log(ns.map(countTrees), ns.map(n => n ** (n - 2)));
```

```text
[ 1, 3, 16, 125, 1296 ] [ 1, 3, 16, 125, 1296 ]
```

The code uses the "any two of three" fact: among sets of n minus 1 edges, the acyclic ones are exactly the
trees. Union–find from chapter 9 detects a cycle as an edge whose endpoints are already in the same class.

### Common mistake

**Confusing height with number of vertices on the longest path.** The wrong way: saying a single-vertex
tree has height 1. Why it fails: height counts edges, and the root is at level 0. The fix: count edges from
the root; some libraries count vertices instead, so read the documentation.

**Assuming m-ary means full.** A binary tree may have internal vertices with only one child. The counting
formulas n equal to mi plus 1 apply only to full m-ary trees.

### In real systems

File systems are rooted trees (with hard links and symlinks bending the rule). The HTML document object
model is an ordered rooted tree. Package managers resolve a dependency graph into a tree per install
location. Organisation charts, category taxonomies and nested comments are rooted trees, and the n minus 1
edges fact means each is stored as one parent pointer per non-root node: the adjacency list of a tree
reduces to a parent array.

## 11.2 Applications of trees

### In one sentence

Rooted trees organise searching (binary search trees), reasoning about every possible run of an
algorithm (decision trees, which give lower bounds), compression (prefix codes built by Huffman's
algorithm) and adversarial choice (game trees evaluated by minimax).

### The idea

#### Binary search trees

A **binary search tree** stores a key at each vertex so that every key in a vertex's left subtree is
smaller and every key in its right subtree is larger. To search for a key, start at the root and go left
or right by comparison until you find it or fall off the tree. To insert, search, and attach a new leaf
where the search fell off.

The cost of a search is the number of comparisons, at most the height plus 1. A tree with n keys has
height at least ⌈log₂(n plus 1)⌉ minus 1, so a balanced tree answers a search in about log₂ n comparisons. But
the shape depends on the insertion order: inserting keys in sorted order produces a path of height n minus
1, and search degrades to a linear scan. Self-balancing trees (AVL, red–black, B-trees) restructure on
insert to keep the height logarithmic.

Inorder traversal (11.3) of a binary search tree lists its keys in sorted order, which is the quickest
way to check that a tree really is a search tree.

#### Decision trees

A **decision tree** models an algorithm that proceeds by tests: each internal vertex is a test, each edge
an outcome, and each leaf a final answer. Every run of the algorithm follows one root-to-leaf path, so the
worst-case number of tests is the height.

That gives **lower bounds**. If a problem has L possible answers and each test has m outcomes, the tree
needs at least L leaves, so its height is at least ⌈log_m L⌉. No algorithm in that model can do better.

**Sorting by comparisons.** A comparison sort of n distinct items must distinguish all n! orderings, and
each comparison has 2 outcomes. So the worst case needs at least ⌈log₂ n!⌉ comparisons. Since log₂ n! is
about n log₂ n minus 1.44n, comparison sorting needs on the order of n log n comparisons, and merge sort,
which achieves that, is asymptotically optimal. Non-comparison sorts (counting sort, radix sort) escape the
bound by using operations other than comparisons.

**Weighing coins.** With a balance scale, each weighing has 3 outcomes (left heavier, right heavier,
balance). Among 9 coins, one is lighter. There are 9 possible answers, so at least ⌈log₃ 9⌉, which is 2,
weighings are needed, and 2 suffice: weigh three against three; the lighter side, or the unweighed three if
they balance, holds the coin; then weigh one against one from that group.

#### Prefix codes

A code assigns bit strings to symbols. A **prefix code** is one in which no codeword is a prefix of
another, so a bit stream can be decoded left to right without separators. Every prefix code is a binary
tree: edges to left children are labelled 0, to right children 1, symbols sit at leaves, and a codeword is
the path from the root. Decoding walks from the root, follows bits, and emits a symbol at each leaf before
returning to the root.

Fixed-length codes are prefix codes in which every leaf is at the same level. Variable-length codes save
space by giving frequent symbols short codewords.

#### Huffman coding

**Huffman's algorithm** builds an optimal prefix code from symbol frequencies:

1. Start with one single-vertex tree per symbol, weighted by its frequency.
2. Repeatedly remove the two trees of smallest weight, and join them under a new root whose weight is
   their sum, the smaller as the left subtree.
3. When one tree remains, read off the codewords.

The result minimises the total encoded length, the sum over symbols of frequency times codeword length,
among all prefix codes. The proof is an exchange argument: in some optimal tree the two least frequent
symbols are siblings at the deepest level, so merging them first loses nothing, and induction finishes.

The average codeword length of any prefix code is at least the **entropy** of the frequencies, the sum
of minus p log₂ p, and Huffman's code is within 1 bit per symbol of it.

#### Game trees

A **game tree** models a two-player game with perfect information. Vertices are positions, edges are
moves, levels alternate between the players, and leaves are final positions labelled with their payoff to
the first player. The **minimax** value is computed bottom up: at a vertex where the first player (max) is
to move, take the largest child value; where the second (min) moves, the smallest. The root's value is the
outcome under best play by both sides.

Full game trees are enormous, so programs search to a limited depth, use an evaluation function at the
frontier, and prune. **Alpha–beta pruning** skips branches that cannot affect the result: once max knows it
can guarantee a value α, any min vertex where min can already force a value at most α can be abandoned.

### Worked example

**Realistic: a binary search tree, and how order changes its shape.**

```ts
type N = { k: number; l?: N; r?: N };
const insert = (t: N | undefined, k: number): N => {
  if (!t) return { k };
  if (k < t.k) t.l = insert(t.l, k); else t.r = insert(t.r, k);
  return t;
};
const height = (t?: N): number => (t ? 1 + Math.max(height(t.l), height(t.r)) : -1);
const inorder = (t?: N): number[] => (t ? [...inorder(t.l), t.k, ...inorder(t.r)] : []);
let comps = 0;
const find = (t: N | undefined, k: number): boolean => {
  if (!t) return false;
  comps++;
  if (k === t.k) return true;
  return find(k < t.k ? t.l : t.r, k);
};
const keys = [50, 30, 70, 20, 40, 60, 80, 35, 45, 65];
let root: N | undefined;
for (const k of keys) root = insert(root, k);
console.log('height', height(root), 'inorder', inorder(root).join(' '));
comps = 0; console.log('find 45', find(root, 45), comps);
comps = 0; console.log('find 66', find(root, 66), comps);
let path: N | undefined;
for (const k of [...keys].sort((a, b) => a - b)) path = insert(path, k);
console.log('sorted insertion height', height(path));
```

type H = { w: number; s?: string; l?: H; r?: H };
let q: H[] = Object.entries(freq).map(([s, w]) => ({ w, s }));
const merges: string[] = [];
while (q.length > 1) {
  q.sort((a, b) => a.w - b.w);
  const a = q.shift()!, b = q.shift()!;
  merges.push(a.w + '+' + b.w + '=' + (a.w + b.w));
  q.push({ w: a.w + b.w, l: a, r: b });
}
const code: Record<string, string> = {};
const walk = (t: H, p: string) => { if (t.s) { code[t.s] = p; return; } walk(t.l!, p + '0'); walk(t.r!, p + '1'); };
walk(q[0], '');

for (const s in freq) bits += freq[s] * code[s].length;
const entropy = -Object.values(freq).reduce((s, w) => s + (w / 100) * Math.log2(w / 100), 0);

const leaves = [[6, 9, 7], [4, 8, 10], [12, 5, 11]];

**Realistic: deleting from a binary search tree.** Deleting a leaf just removes it, and deleting a vertex
with one child splices the child into its place. A vertex with two children is the interesting case:
replace its key with its **inorder successor**, the smallest key in its right subtree (which has no left
child), then delete that successor from the right subtree, which is one of the easy cases.

```ts
type B = { k: number; l?: B; r?: B };
const add = (t: B | undefined, k: number): B => {
  if (!t) return { k };
  if (k < t.k) t.l = add(t.l, k); else t.r = add(t.r, k);
  return t;
};
const remove = (t: B | undefined, k: number): B | undefined => {
  if (!t) return t;
  if (k < t.k) t.l = remove(t.l, k);
  else if (k > t.k) t.r = remove(t.r, k);
  else {
    if (!t.l) return t.r;
    if (!t.r) return t.l;
    let s = t.r;
    while (s.l) s = s.l;
    t.k = s.k;
    t.r = remove(t.r, s.k);
  }
  return t;
};
let bst: B | undefined;
for (const k of [50, 30, 70, 20, 40, 60, 80, 35, 45, 65]) bst = add(bst, k);
bst = remove(bst, 30);
const preorder = (t?: B): number[] => (t ? [t.k, ...preorder(t.l), ...preorder(t.r)] : []);
console.log(preorder(bst).join(' '));
```

Deleting 30, which has children 20 and 40, copies its successor 35 into its place and removes the old leaf
35. The left subtree of 50 becomes 35 with children 20 and 40, and 40 keeps only its right child 45. Every
key left of 35 is still smaller and every key right of it larger, so the search property survives.

intuition, such as e 0, t 1, a 01. Why it fails: 0 is a prefix of 01, so "01" could be "et" or "a". The
fix: put symbols only at leaves of a binary tree; Huffman's algorithm does this automatically.

**Reading a decision-tree bound as an algorithm.** The bound ⌈log₂ n!⌉ says no comparison sort can do
better; it does not say a given sort achieves it. The fix: pair a lower bound with an algorithm that meets
it before claiming optimality.

model, where each test is a feature threshold. Game engines and puzzle solvers use minimax with

## 11.3 Tree traversal

### In one sentence

A traversal visits every vertex of an ordered rooted tree once in a systematic order, preorder (root
first), inorder (root between subtrees) and postorder (root last), and applied to expression trees these
orders produce prefix, infix and postfix notation, the last two of which drive how calculators and
compilers evaluate expressions.

### The idea

#### Universal address systems

Label the vertices of an ordered rooted tree as follows: the root gets 0, its children get 1, 2, 3 and so
on from left to right, and if a vertex has address A, its children get A.1, A.2, and so on. Every vertex
gets a unique address that spells out the path from the root. Ordering the addresses **lexicographically**
(compare component by component as integers, with a prefix coming first) lists the vertices in a fixed
order, in fact preorder.

For a tree whose root has children 1, 2 and 3, where 1 has children 1.1 and 1.2, and 3 has children 3.1
and 3.2, and 3.1 has children 3.1.1 and 3.1.2, the lexicographic order is 0, 1, 1.1, 1.2, 2, 3, 3.1, 3.1.1,
3.1.2, 3.2. This is how outline numbering in documents works, and how the section numbers of this book
work.

#### Preorder, inorder and postorder

Each traversal is defined recursively, by the recursive definition of an ordered rooted tree from
chapter 5. Let T have root r and subtrees T1, ..., Tₙ from left to right.

- **Preorder**: visit r, then traverse T1 in preorder, then T2 in preorder, and so on.
- **Inorder**: traverse T1 in inorder, visit r, then traverse T2, ..., Tₙ in inorder. For binary trees,
  this is left subtree, root, right subtree.
- **Postorder**: traverse T1, ..., Tₙ in postorder, then visit r.

A quick way to read them off a drawing: trace a path around the tree, counterclockwise, starting at the
root and hugging the edges. Preorder lists each vertex the first time you pass it; postorder lists it the
last time; inorder (for binary trees) lists a vertex when you pass underneath it.

A fourth order, **level order**, visits the root, then all vertices at level 1, then level 2, and so on:
breadth-first search on the tree.

#### Expression trees

An arithmetic expression is an ordered rooted tree: internal vertices are operators, leaves are operands,
and the subtrees of an operator are its operands in order. The expression ((7 minus 3) times (2 plus 4))
divided by 8 has division at the root, with the times subtree on the left and 8 on the right.

- **Inorder** gives **infix** notation, which is ambiguous without parentheses or precedence rules: 7
  minus 3 times 2 plus 4 divided by 8 could mean many things.
- **Preorder** gives **prefix** notation, also called Polish notation, in which each operator comes before
  its operands. It needs no parentheses.
- **Postorder** gives **postfix** notation, reverse Polish notation, in which each operator follows its
  operands. It also needs no parentheses.

#### Evaluating postfix and prefix

Evaluate postfix left to right with a stack: push each operand; on an operator, pop the top two values
(the second popped is the left operand), apply the operator, and push the result. At the end the stack
holds one value.

Evaluate prefix the same way but right to left, and the first popped value is the left operand.

These are why reverse Polish calculators need no parentheses key, and why stack-based virtual machines
(the JVM, WebAssembly, many interpreters) execute code that is essentially postfix.

#### Implementing traversals

The recursive definitions translate directly into recursive functions. For very deep trees an explicit
stack avoids recursion limits: push the root; repeatedly pop a vertex, visit it, and push its children in
reverse order for preorder. Postorder with a stack can be done by producing "root, right, left" and
reversing the output.

## 11.4 Spanning trees

### In one sentence

A spanning tree of a connected graph is a subgraph that is a tree and contains every vertex, every
connected simple graph has one, depth-first and breadth-first search build them in linear time, and the
depth-first idea, extended with undoing, becomes backtracking, the general method for searching huge
spaces of choices.

### The idea

#### Spanning trees

A **spanning tree** of a simple graph G is a subgraph of G that is a tree and contains every vertex of G.
It keeps the graph connected with the fewest possible edges, n minus 1 of them.

**Theorem**: a simple graph is connected if and only if it has a spanning tree.

If G has a spanning tree, any two vertices are joined by a path in the tree, which is a path in G. For the
converse, start with G. While there is a simple circuit, remove one of its edges; the graph stays
connected, because the rest of the circuit still joins the endpoints of the removed edge. Each removal
reduces the edges, so the process stops, at a connected graph with no circuits: a spanning tree.

That proof is not an efficient algorithm (finding circuits is extra work), but it shows that spanning trees
exist. The efficient way is to grow one.

#### Depth-first search

**Depth-first search** grows a spanning tree by going as deep as possible before backing up. Start at any
vertex. From the current vertex, move along an edge to an unvisited vertex, adding the edge to the tree,
and repeat from there. When the current vertex has no unvisited neighbours, back up to its parent and
continue. When you back up to the start with nothing left, the tree spans the component.

The edges of G not in the tree are **back edges**: in an undirected graph, each joins a vertex to one of
its ancestors in the depth-first tree, never across to another branch. That property is what makes the
cut-vertex algorithm of 10.4 work.

#### Breadth-first search

**Breadth-first search** grows the tree level by level. Start at a root, add all its neighbours as
children, then all unvisited neighbours of those, and so on, using a queue. The level of each vertex in a
breadth-first tree is its distance from the root, so the tree is a **shortest-path tree** for unweighted
graphs. Non-tree edges join vertices at the same level or adjacent levels.

Both searches take time proportional to the number of vertices plus edges.

#### Backtracking

Many problems ask for an arrangement satisfying constraints: place queens, colour a graph, fill a Sudoku,
pick a subset with a given sum. Their possible partial solutions form a rooted tree: the root is the empty
choice, and each child extends its parent's partial solution by one more decision. **Backtracking** is
depth-first search of this implicit tree that abandons a branch as soon as the partial solution breaks a
constraint, then backs up and tries the next choice. Pruning early is what makes it practical: most of the
tree is never generated.

The classic example is placing n queens on an n-by-n board with no two attacking each other (no two in the
same row, column or diagonal). Place one queen per row; at row r, try each column that is not attacked
by the queens in rows above; if none works, back up.

#### Spanning forests and counting spanning trees

A **spanning forest** of a graph is a forest containing every vertex, one tree per component. Its size
reveals the number of components: a spanning forest of a graph with n vertices and c components has n
minus c edges.

The number of spanning trees of a graph is given by **Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem**: build the
Laplacian matrix (degrees on the diagonal, minus 1 for each edge off it), delete any one row and the same
column, and take the determinant. For Kn this gives Cayley's nⁿ⁻²; for the cycle Cn it gives n (remove any
one of its n edges).

## 11.5 Minimum spanning trees

### In one sentence

A minimum spanning tree of a connected weighted graph is a spanning tree of least total weight, and two
greedy algorithms find one: Prim's grows a single tree by always adding the cheapest edge leaving it, and
Kruskal's adds the cheapest remaining edge anywhere that does not form a circuit, both justified by the cut
property.

### The idea

#### The problem

Given a connected weighted graph, a **minimum spanning tree** is a spanning tree whose total edge weight
is as small as possible. The motivating problem is connecting sites (offices, towns, circuit pins) with the
cheapest network that reaches them all. There may be many spanning trees: the office network above has
30, and K20 has 20¹⁸. Trying them all is out of the question.

#### Prim's algorithm

1. Start with a tree containing one vertex.
2. Repeatedly add the edge of least weight that joins a vertex in the tree to a vertex not in it.
3. Stop after n minus 1 edges.

With a heap of candidate edges, Prim's algorithm runs in time proportional to edges times log vertices.
It looks like Dijkstra's algorithm, but the key it minimises is the weight of a single edge, not a
distance from the source.

#### Kruskal's algorithm

1. Sort the edges by weight.
2. Go through them in order, adding each edge that does not form a simple circuit with the edges already
   chosen.
3. Stop after n minus 1 edges.

Kruskal's algorithm maintains a forest, which merges into one tree. Checking "does this edge form a
circuit" is checking whether its endpoints are already in the same tree: union–find, from chapter 9,
answers it in nearly constant time. Sorting dominates, so the time is proportional to edges times log
edges.

#### Why greedy works: the cut property

A **cut** splits the vertices into two non-empty sets S and V minus S; an edge **crosses** the cut if it
has one endpoint on each side.

**Cut property**: for any cut, a crossing edge of least weight belongs to some minimum spanning tree (to
every one, if that weight is unique).

Both algorithms only ever add such edges. In Prim's, the cut is "tree versus the rest". In Kruskal's, when
edge u–v is added, take S to be the current tree containing u; no cheaper edge crosses that cut, since a
cheaper one would have been considered first and would have joined u's tree to something.

A matching **cycle property** says the heaviest edge on any circuit (if uniquely heaviest) is in no
minimum spanning tree, which is why Kruskal's skips edges that close a circuit.

If all edge weights are distinct, the minimum spanning tree is unique.

  let total = 0;
  while (inTree.size < Object.keys(adj).length) {
    let best: [string, string, number] | null = null;
    for (const u of inTree) for (const [v, w] of adj[u]) if (!inTree.has(v) && (!best || w < best[2])) best = [u, v, w];
    inTree.add(best![1]); tree.push(`${best![0]}-${best![1]} ${best![2]}`); total += best![2];
  }
  return { tree, total };
}

### Common mistake

**Using Prim's or Kruskal's for shortest paths.** The wrong way: assuming the path between two vertices in
the minimum spanning tree is a shortest path. Why it fails: in the office example the tree path from Pune
to Surat goes via Mumbai and Nashik and costs 570, but Pune–Nashik–Surat costs 460. The tree minimises
total wiring, not any one route. The fix: Dijkstra for routes, minimum spanning trees
for networks.

**Greedily adding the cheapest edge at a vertex.** Picking, for each vertex, its cheapest edge, and
stopping there, can leave the graph disconnected. In the office example it happens to give the whole
tree, but on edges a–b 1, c–d 1 and b–c 5 it gives only a–b and c–d, a forest of two trees. The
fix: that step is the first round of Borůvka's algorithm, which then merges components and repeats.

### In real systems

Network designers use minimum spanning trees as the starting point for cabling, pipelines and power
grids, then add redundancy. Single-linkage clustering is Kruskal's algorithm stopped early: removing the k
minus 1 heaviest edges of a minimum spanning tree leaves k clusters (removing the 560 link above separates
Hyderabad from the rest). For the travelling salesperson problem with distances satisfying the triangle
inequality, walking around a minimum spanning tree and skipping repeats gives a tour at most twice optimal.
Image segmentation and approximate Steiner trees build on minimum spanning trees too.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 11, Trees">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version of this chapter: a tree is connected with no cycles, has n minus 1 edges, and
has exactly one path between any two vertices. Rooting it gives parents, children and leaves, and three
recursive traversals visit every vertex.

Example: a folder structure with 120 folders and files (counting the top folder) has exactly 119
parent–child links, whatever its shape. If a sync tool reports 125 links, something is a shortcut or a
link, not a plain tree edge.

The beginner mistake is to treat any hierarchy as a tree without checking for shared children. A
spreadsheet of "reports to" where one person has two managers is not a tree, and recursive code that
assumes it is will visit that person twice. Fix it by checking that every non-root has exactly one parent.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level trees are data structures and the questions are about cost. Searching a binary search tree
costs its height, building a Huffman code costs a heap of merges, and search-built spanning trees cost
linear time. You choose recursion or an explicit stack, and the traversal order that fits the job.

Example: storing 1,000,000 keys in a balanced binary search tree gives height about 19, so a lookup takes
about 20 comparisons; inserting the same keys in sorted order into a plain tree gives height 999,999 and a
recursion that crashes.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

The advanced view uses trees to prove things: lower bounds by decision trees, optimality by exchange
arguments (Huffman, the cut property), and counts by bijections (Cayley's formula via Prüfer codes) or
determinants (the matrix-tree theorem).

Example: finding the largest of n numbers needs n minus 1 comparisons, and a decision tree alone gives only
⌈log₂ n⌉. The stronger bound comes from an adversary argument: every number except the maximum must lose a
comparison. Decision trees are one tool among several for lower bounds.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases are the smallest trees. A single vertex is a tree with 0 edges, height 0, and one leaf that is
also the root. The empty graph is usually not called a tree. A tree with two vertices has two leaves. A full
binary tree cannot have exactly 2 vertices or any even number of them, since n equals 2i plus 1.

Example: a Huffman code for a single symbol needs a codeword, but the tree is just a root with an empty
path. Real encoders special-case it with a 1-bit code, which is why my code in 11.2 would need a tweak for
one-symbol input.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases come from ties and degeneracy. With equal edge weights there can be many minimum spanning
trees, and Prim's and Kruskal's may return different ones. Huffman's algorithm with ties can give different
codeword lengths with the same total. Duplicate keys in a binary search tree need a policy: left, right, or
a count at the vertex.

Example: with weights a–b 1, b–c 1 and a–c 1, all three spanning trees cost 2. A test asserting a particular
tree is brittle; assert the total weight and that the result is a spanning tree instead.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, trees live on disks and caches. B-trees choose a high branching factor so each vertex fills a
page. Recursion is replaced by iteration for deep inputs. Huffman tables are canonicalised so only code
lengths need to be transmitted. Minimum spanning trees on millions of edges use Kruskal's with a parallel
sort or Borůvka's in distributed settings.

Example: a B-tree with 200 keys per page holds 200⁴, which is 1.6 billion keys, at height 3, with four page
reads per lookup and the top two levels usually in memory.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is to look for the tree inside the problem. A recursive function's calls form a tree; a search
space is a tree of choices; a hierarchy is a tree if parents are unique; a network has spanning trees. Once
you see the tree, its height is usually the cost.

Example: memoising a recursive function turns its call tree into a DAG of distinct subproblems. Seeing the
tree first makes the waste visible: naive recursive Fibonacci's call tree has about φⁿ leaves.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design, trees give hierarchy, fan-out and single paths. They make routing and aggregation simple
(sum up a tree, broadcast down it) but have no redundancy: every edge of a tree is a bridge. Designs start
from a tree and add edges for resilience, or keep a spanning tree as the active topology and hold spare
links in reserve.

Example: a monitoring system aggregates metrics up a tree of collectors, 50 hosts per collector and 50
collectors per regional node, so a region of 2,500 hosts needs one hop to a collector and one more to the
regional node. Any collector failure blinds 50 hosts, so collectors are paired.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategic view is to know when greedy works. Prim's, Kruskal's and Huffman's are greedy algorithms
backed by exchange arguments; many tree problems that look similar (the Steiner tree, the minimum-degree
spanning tree) have no such argument and are NP-hard. Before trusting a greedy algorithm, ask for its
exchange argument.

Example: connecting 10 offices using optional relay sites, which may be used or not, is the Steiner tree
problem. A minimum spanning tree over the offices alone is a 2-approximation, a good default while an exact
solver runs.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**is every tree a bipartite graph?**

Yes. Colour vertices by the parity of their level from any root; every edge joins consecutive levels.

**what is the difference between a tree and a rooted tree?**

A tree is undirected and has no special vertex. A rooted tree picks a root and directs every edge away
from it, which gives parents and children.

**why are B-trees used instead of binary search trees in databases?**

Because disk and memory pages favour few, large reads. A B-tree with hundreds of keys per vertex has a much
smaller height, so a lookup touches a handful of pages.

**is Huffman coding still used?**

Yes, as the entropy-coding stage of DEFLATE and JPEG. Newer formats often use arithmetic coding or
asymmetric numeral systems, which approach the entropy more closely.

**Prim or Kruskal?**

Kruskal's is simplest when edges arrive as a list and the graph is sparse. Prim's with a heap suits dense
graphs or adjacency-list representations. Both give a minimum spanning tree.

## Key takeaways

- A tree is connected and acyclic; equivalently, unique simple paths; equivalently, connected with n
  minus 1 edges.
- Rooted trees give parent, child, ancestor, leaf, level and height; a full m-ary tree with i internal
  vertices has mi plus 1 vertices.
- An m-ary tree of height h has at most mʰ leaves, so heights are at least logarithmic in the leaves.
- Binary search trees search in height plus 1 comparisons; balance keeps that logarithmic.
- Decision trees give lower bounds, including about n log n comparisons for sorting.
- Huffman's greedy merges give optimal prefix codes; minimax evaluates game trees; alpha–beta prunes them.
- Preorder, inorder and postorder give prefix, infix and postfix notation; postfix evaluates with a stack.
- Every connected graph has a spanning tree; DFS and BFS find one; BFS trees give unweighted distances.
- Backtracking is depth-first search of a tree of partial solutions with early pruning.
- Prim's and Kruskal's find minimum spanning trees, justified by the cut property.

## Glossary

- **Tree, forest**: a connected graph with no simple circuits; a graph whose components are trees.
- **Rooted tree, root**: a tree with one vertex chosen and edges directed away from it.
- **Parent, child, sibling**: the vertex above; a vertex below; vertices with the same parent.
- **Ancestor, descendant**: vertices on the path to the root; vertices below.
- **Leaf, internal vertex**: no children; at least one child.
- **Subtree**: a vertex with all its descendants.
- **m-ary tree, full m-ary tree, binary tree**: at most m children; exactly m; m equal to 2.
- **Ordered rooted tree**: children are ordered left to right.
- **Level, height, balanced**: distance from the root; largest level; all leaves at levels h or h minus 1.
- **Cayley's formula**: nⁿ⁻² labelled trees on n vertices.
- **Binary search tree**: smaller keys left, larger right, at every vertex.
- **Decision tree**: tests at internal vertices, outcomes on edges, answers at leaves.
- **Prefix code**: no codeword is a prefix of another.
- **Huffman coding**: optimal prefix codes by merging the two lightest trees.
- **Entropy**: the lower limit on average bits per symbol, the sum of minus p log₂ p.
- **Game tree, minimax**: positions and moves; values by alternating max and min.
- **Alpha–beta pruning**: skipping branches that cannot change the minimax value.
- **Universal address system**: labels 0, 1, 1.1, ... giving each vertex's path.
- **Preorder, inorder, postorder, level order**: root first; root between subtrees; root last; by levels.
- **Prefix, infix, postfix notation**: operator before, between, after operands; Polish and reverse Polish.
- **Spanning tree, spanning forest**: a tree containing every vertex; one tree per component.
- **Depth-first search, back edge**: deep-first growth; a non-tree edge to an ancestor.
- **Breadth-first search**: level-by-level growth with a queue.
- **Backtracking**: depth-first search over partial solutions, undoing failed choices.
- **Matrix-tree theorem**: spanning trees counted by a determinant of the Laplacian.
- **Minimum spanning tree**: a spanning tree of least total weight.
- **Prim's, Kruskal's algorithm**: grow one tree by cheapest leaving edge; add cheapest non-circuit edge.
- **Cut, cut property**: a split of the vertices; a lightest crossing edge is in some minimum spanning tree.
