Induction is how you prove a statement for infinitely many cases with a finite argument, and recursion is
how you define and compute things in terms of smaller versions of themselves. They are the same idea seen
from two sides, and this chapter uses both to reason about the programs we write, ending with proofs that
a loop does what it claims.

## 5.1 Mathematical induction

### In one sentence

To prove P(n) for every positive integer n, prove P(1), then prove that P(k) implies P(k plus 1) for an
arbitrary k; together they reach every n.

### The idea

#### The principle

**Mathematical induction** proves "for all positive integers n, P(n)" in two steps. The **basis step**
shows P(1) is true. The **inductive step** shows that for every positive integer k, if P(k) is true then
P(k plus 1) is true. The assumption that P(k) holds is the **inductive hypothesis**.

As a rule of inference: from P(1) and "for all k, P(k) implies P(k plus 1)", conclude "for all n, P(n)".
It is valid because the positive integers are well ordered, as 5.2 shows.

#### The dominoes picture

#### Where it starts

The basis need not be 1. To prove P(n) for every n at least b, prove P(b) and that P(k) implies P(k plus
1) for every k at least b. Many inequalities start later: 2ⁿ is less than n! only from n equal to 4.

#### The shape of a written proof

Every induction proof should say, in this order: what P(n) is; the basis step with the arithmetic shown;
"assume P(k) for an arbitrary k at least b"; the inductive step, which must *use* the hypothesis; and the
conclusion. Graders look for the moment the hypothesis is used. If you never use it, it was not
induction.

#### What it can prove

Summation formulas, inequalities, divisibility facts, facts about sets (a set with n elements has 2ⁿ
subsets), and facts about algorithms (a loop does the right thing after every iteration). It cannot
*find* a formula; you guess the formula first, often from small cases, and induction confirms it.

#### Induction on sets: counting subsets

A set with n elements has 2ⁿ subsets. Basis: the empty set has one subset, itself, and 2⁰ is 1. Step:
take a set S with k plus 1 elements and pick one element a. Every subset of S either leaves a out, and is
a subset of S minus a, or contains a, and is a subset of S minus a with a added. By the hypothesis there
are 2ᵏ of each kind, so 2ᵏ⁺¹ in total.

The proof is also an algorithm. The recursive function below builds the subsets exactly the way the
inductive step counts them: the subsets without the first element, and the same subsets with it.

```ts
const subsets = (xs: string[]): string[][] =>
  xs.length === 0 ? [[]] : subsets(xs.slice(1)).flatMap(s => [s, [xs[0], ...s]]);
console.log(subsets(['a', 'b', 'c']).map(s => s.join('') || '∅').join(' '));
console.log(subsets(['a', 'b', 'c', 'd']).length);
```

```text
∅ a b ab c ac bc abc
16
```

#### Induction for inequalities and for algorithms

Inequality proofs usually need one extra fact in the step, such as "k plus 1 is greater than 2" in the
proof of 2ⁿ less than n!. Finding that fact is the hard part; write the target inequality for k plus 1,
substitute the hypothesis, and see what is left to show.

Induction also proves facts about algorithms: "after k iterations the first k elements are sorted" is
the inductive statement behind insertion sort. That idea becomes loop invariants in 5.5.

### Worked example

**Simple: the sum of the first n odd numbers.** Small cases: 1, 1 plus 3 is 4, plus 5 is 9, plus 7 is 16.
Guess: n². Node checks it up to 1,000, but only induction proves it for every n.

```ts
let ok = true;
for (let n = 1; n <= 1000; n++) {
  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 1; i <= n; i++) s += 2 * i - 1;
  if (s !== n * n) ok = false;
}
console.log('sum of first n odds is n² up to 1000:', ok);
```

```text
sum of first n odds is n² up to 1000: true
```

**Realistic: a geometric sum.** 1 plus 2 plus 4 plus ... plus 2ⁿ equals 2ⁿ⁺¹ minus 1. This is why a
binary counter with n plus 1 bits can hold values up to 2ⁿ⁺¹ minus 1, and why a full binary tree of height
n has 2ⁿ⁺¹ minus 1 nodes.

Basis, n equal to 0: 1 equals 2 minus 1. Step: assume the sum up to 2ᵏ is 2ᵏ⁺¹ minus 1. Add 2ᵏ⁺¹: the
total is 2·2ᵏ⁺¹ minus 1, which is 2ᵏ⁺² minus 1.

**Realistic: divisibility.** n³ minus n is divisible by 3 for every non-negative n. Basis: 0. Step: (k
plus 1)³ minus (k plus 1) expands to (k³ minus k) plus 3(k² plus k). The first part is divisible by 3 by
hypothesis, and the second plainly is.

Prove 2ⁿ is less than n! for every n at least 4. Basis: 16 is less than 24. Step: assume 2ᵏ is less than
k! with k at least 4. Then 2ᵏ⁺¹ is 2·2ᵏ, less than 2·k!, which is less than (k plus 1)·k! because k plus
1 is greater than 2. That is (k plus 1)!.

### Common mistake

**Assuming what you want to prove.** The wrong way: in the inductive step, start from P(k plus 1) and
manipulate it until it becomes something true. Why it fails: deriving a truth from P(k plus 1) does not
show P(k plus 1) is true; false statements imply true ones too.

The fix is to start from the left side of P(k plus 1), apply the hypothesis to a piece of it, and reach
the right side.

**The "all horses are the same colour" trap.** A famous bogus proof shows any n horses share a colour by
removing one horse, then another, and overlapping the two groups. The step fails from n equal to 1 to 2,
where the two groups do not overlap. The fix is to check that the inductive step works for the smallest
k, not just "typical" k.

### In real systems

Induction is how you trust anything that repeats: every iteration of a loop, every level of a recursion,
every block appended to a log or ledger. "If the state is valid before this step, it is valid after" plus
"the initial state is valid" is an induction proof, whether or not anyone writes it down.

Property-based tests check many cases but, like the Node loop above, never all. Induction is what turns
"it worked for the first thousand" into "it works".

## 5.2 Strong induction and well-ordering

### In one sentence

Strong induction lets the inductive step assume P(j) for every j up to k, not just P(k), and the
well-ordering principle, every non-empty set of positive integers has a least element, is the axiom that
makes all forms of induction valid.

### The idea

#### Strong induction

**Strong induction**, also called the second principle of induction, proves P(n) for all positive n by
showing P(1), and that if P(1), P(2), ..., P(k) are all true then P(k plus 1) is true. It is no stronger
in what it can prove; it is more convenient when P(k plus 1) depends on a case further back than k.

The classic example: every integer greater than 1 is a product of primes. If k plus 1 is prime, done. If
not, it is ab with a and b between 2 and k, and both factor into primes by the strong hypothesis. Knowing
only P(k) would not help, because a and b are not k.

#### Multiple basis cases

When the step reaches back a fixed distance, it needs that many basis cases. To show every amount of
postage of 8 or more can be made with 3 and 5 stamps, the step uses P(k minus 2) (add a 3), so the basis
must cover 8, 9 and 10.

#### The well-ordering principle

Every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a least element. It sounds obvious and is an axiom of
the integers. It is equivalent to induction: each can be proved from the other.

Proofs by well-ordering usually go: suppose the claim fails; then the set of counterexamples is non-empty
and has a least element; use that least element to construct a smaller counterexample, a contradiction.
It is induction run backwards, sometimes called a **minimal counterexample** argument.

#### The division algorithm, proved

Chapter 4 used the division algorithm without proof. Well-ordering gives it: for a and positive d,
consider the set of non-negative numbers of the form a minus dq. It is non-empty, so it has a least
element r equal to a minus dq. If r were at least d, then r minus d would be a smaller non-negative
element, so r is between 0 and d minus 1.

### Worked example

**Simple: postage with 3 and 5.**

```ts
function stamps(n: number): [number, number] | null {
  for (let f = 0; 5 * f <= n; f++) if ((n - 5 * f) % 3 === 0) return [(n - 5 * f) / 3, f];
  return null;
}
console.log([4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].map(n => `${n}: ${JSON.stringify(stamps(n))}`).join('  '));
```

```text
4: null  7: null  8: [1,1]  9: [3,0]  10: [0,2]  11: [2,1]
```

7 cannot be made, 8 is 3 plus 5, 9 is three 3s, 10 is two 5s, and from then on each amount is a 3 added to
the amount three below.

**Realistic: a game.** Two players take turns removing 1 or 2 matches from a pile; whoever takes the last
match wins. Claim: the first player wins exactly when n is not a multiple of 3. Strong induction on n: from
a non-multiple of 3, remove enough to leave a multiple of 3; from a multiple of 3, every move leaves a
non-multiple, which by hypothesis is a win for the opponent.

**Realistic: well-ordering for a loop.** Every loop that decreases a non-negative integer each time must
stop, because an infinite run would give a set of values with no least element. This is how termination
proofs work.

### Common mistake

**Too few basis cases.** The wrong way: proving P(8) and then stepping from k minus 2. Why it fails: the
step for 9 needs P(6) and for 10 needs P(7), which are not covered and are not even true for 7.

The fix is to count how far back the step reaches and supply that many consecutive basis cases.

**Applying well-ordering to a set that can be empty or unbounded below.** The integers have no least
element; well-ordering applies to non-negative integers or any set bounded below.

### In real systems

Strong induction is the proof pattern behind dynamic programming: the answer for size n is built from
answers for smaller sizes, not just n minus 1. Termination arguments, "this value decreases each iteration
and cannot go below 0", are well-ordering; tools such as Dafny and Lean ask for exactly that measure.

## 5.3 Recursive definitions and structural induction

### In one sentence

A recursive definition gives a few starting values or elements and rules that build new ones from old,
and structural induction proves a property of everything so built by checking the start and each rule.

### The idea

#### Recursively defined functions

A function on the non-negative integers is defined **recursively** by a **basis step**, its value at 0,
and a **recursive step**, a rule for its value at n plus 1 in terms of smaller values. Factorial is 0!
equal to 1 and (n plus 1)! equal to (n plus 1)·n!. Induction proves such a definition gives exactly one
value for every n.

The **Fibonacci numbers** are f0 equal to 0, f1 equal to 1, and fₙ equal to fₙ₋₁ plus fₙ₋₂. They grow
exponentially: fₙ is greater than αⁿ⁻² for n at least 3, where α is the golden ratio, (1 plus √5)/2, about
1.618. Node shows f20 is 6,765 against α to the 18 of about 5,778.

#### Lamé's theorem

Fibonacci numbers are the worst case for Euclid's algorithm. **Lamé's theorem** says the number of
divisions Euclid's algorithm uses on a and b, with a at least b, is at most five times the number of
decimal digits of b. On 10,946 and 6,765, consecutive Fibonacci numbers, it takes 19 divisions, against
the bound 5 times 4, which is 20. So Euclid is O(log b).

#### Recursively defined sets

A set is defined recursively by a basis (some elements are in it) and a recursive step (rules that make
new elements from existing ones). An **exclusion rule** is implicit: nothing else is in the set. The set
of positive multiples of 3: 3 is in S, and if x and y are in S then x plus y is.

#### Strings

The set Σ* of **strings** over an alphabet Σ: the **empty string** λ is in Σ*, and if w is in Σ* and x is
in Σ then wx is in Σ*. **Concatenation** and **length** are defined on the same pattern: the length of λ
is 0, and the length of wx is the length of w plus 1.

Well-formed formulas, arithmetic expressions and balanced parentheses are all defined this way. Balanced
strings of n pairs number 1, 2, 5, 14, 42 for n from 1 to 5, the Catalan numbers.

#### Rooted trees and full binary trees

**Rooted trees**: a single vertex is a rooted tree; and given disjoint rooted trees T1 to Tₙ, a new root
joined to each of their roots is a rooted tree. **Extended binary trees** allow an empty tree as basis.
**Full binary trees**: a single vertex is one; and a new root with two full binary trees as left and right
subtrees is one.

Height and node count are defined recursively on the same structure: a single vertex has height 0 and
one node; a root over T1 and T2 has height 1 plus the larger height, and one node plus the two counts.

#### Structural induction

To prove a property for every element of a recursively defined set, prove it for the basis elements, and
prove that if it holds for the elements a rule uses, it holds for the element the rule builds. This is
**structural induction**. It is valid because every element is reached by finitely many rule applications.

#### Generalised induction

Induction also works on any **well-ordered** set, such as pairs of non-negative integers ordered
lexicographically: (a, b) comes before (c, d) if a is less than c, or a equals c and b is less than d.
That covers functions defined on two arguments.

### Worked example

**Simple: Fibonacci three ways.**

```ts
let calls = 0;
const fib = (n: number): number => { calls++; return n < 2 ? n : fib(n - 1) + fib(n - 2); };
fib(20); console.log('fib(20) calls:', calls);
const fibIter = (n: number) => { let a = 0, b = 1; for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) [a, b] = [b, a + b]; return a; };
console.log(fibIter(20), fibIter(50));
```

```text
fib(20) calls: 21891
6765 12586269025
```

The recursive version makes 21,891 calls for f20 (and over 2.6 million for f30), because it recomputes
the same values. The loop makes 20 steps.

**Realistic: string reversal and a structural proof.** Define reverse(λ) equal to λ and reverse(xw) equal
to reverse(w)x. Claim: reverse(uv) equals reverse(v)reverse(u). Structural induction on u: for u equal to
λ both sides are reverse(v). For u equal to xw, reverse(xwv) is reverse(wv)x, which by hypothesis is
reverse(v)reverse(w)x, which is reverse(v)reverse(xw).

```ts
const rev = (s: string): string => (s === '' ? '' : rev(s.slice(1)) + s[0]);
console.log(rev('stack'), rev('ab' + 'cd') === rev('cd') + rev('ab'));
```

```text
kcats true
```

**Realistic: balanced parentheses.** Basis: the empty string is balanced. Rules: if w is balanced so is
(w), and if u and v are balanced so is uv. Structural induction shows every balanced string has equal
numbers of ( and ), and every prefix has at least as many ( as ).

### Common mistake

**Missing a rule in the structural step.** The wrong way: proving a property of arithmetic expressions
for numbers, sums and products, but forgetting the parenthesised-expression rule. Why it fails: the proof
covers only the elements built without that rule.

The fix is to list every basis element and every rule from the definition, and handle each one.

**Recursive definitions without a basis**, or with a step that does not make progress, define nothing:
f(n) equal to f(n) plus 0 is satisfied by every function.

### In real systems

Every tree-shaped data structure is recursively defined: JSON values, ASTs, file systems, DOM trees,
React component trees. Functions over them, such as serialising, pretty-printing or type checking, follow
the definition one case per rule, and TypeScript's discriminated unions with exhaustive `switch` enforce
"handle every rule".

Grammars for programming languages are recursive set definitions, and a parser is structural recursion
over the input.

## 5.4 Recursive algorithms

### In one sentence

A recursive algorithm solves a problem by reducing it to the same problem on smaller inputs, and is
proved correct by induction on the input size.

### The idea

#### What a recursive algorithm is

An algorithm is **recursive** if it solves a problem by solving smaller instances of the same problem. It
needs base cases that it solves directly, and every recursive call must move towards one. Factorial,
powers, gcd, binary search and linear search all have natural recursive forms.

#### Proving them correct

Induction on input size proves a recursive algorithm correct. The basis case checks the base cases. The
step assumes the algorithm is correct on all smaller inputs (strong induction) and shows the recursive
step combines those correct results into a correct answer.

#### Recursive modular exponentiation

bⁿ mod m: if n is 0, return 1. Otherwise compute h equal to b^(n div 2) mod m recursively. If n is even
return h² mod m, else h²·b mod m. That makes about log₂ n calls; for 3 to the 100 mod 7 it takes 8 calls
and returns 4.

#### Recursion versus iteration

Every recursive algorithm can be written iteratively and vice versa. Recursion is often clearer and
mirrors the definition. Iteration avoids call overhead and stack growth, and avoids the repeated work that
naive Fibonacci shows. Memoising, storing results already computed, gives recursion the same speed.

#### Merge sort

**Merge sort** splits a list in half, sorts each half recursively, and merges the two sorted halves. A
list of length 0 or 1 is already sorted. **Merging** two sorted lists repeatedly takes the smaller of the
two front elements; merging lists of lengths m and n takes at most m plus n minus 1 comparisons.

The total for n equal to 2ᵐ is at most n log₂ n comparisons: log₂ n levels of splitting, and at most n
comparisons per level. Chapter 8 proves it with a recurrence, and also that no comparison sort beats n log
n in the worst case.

### Worked example

**Simple: recursive binary search.**

```ts
const bs = (xs: number[], x: number, i: number, j: number): number => {
  if (i > j) return -1;
  const m = (i + j) >> 1;
  if (xs[m] === x) return m;
  return x < xs[m] ? bs(xs, x, i, m - 1) : bs(xs, x, m + 1, j);
};
console.log(bs([1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11], 9, 0, 5));
```

```text
4
```

**Realistic: merge sort, counted.**

```ts
let cmp = 0;
function mergeSort(xs: number[]): number[] {
  if (xs.length <= 1) return xs;
  const m = Math.floor(xs.length / 2);
  const L = mergeSort(xs.slice(0, m)), R = mergeSort(xs.slice(m));
  const out: number[] = []; let i = 0, j = 0;
  while (i < L.length && j < R.length) { cmp++; out.push(L[i] <= R[j] ? L[i++] : R[j++]); }
  return out.concat(L.slice(i), R.slice(j));
}
console.log(mergeSort([5, 1, 8, 3, 7, 2, 6, 4]), cmp);
```

```text
[ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 ] 17
```

### Common mistake

**A recursive call that does not shrink.** The wrong way: `bs(xs, x, i, m)` instead of `m - 1`. Why it
fails: when i equals j, m equals i and the call repeats forever, or overflows the stack.

The fix is a **measure**: an integer that each call strictly decreases and that cannot go below 0, here j
minus i plus 1. If you cannot name it, the recursion may not terminate.

**Deep recursion in JavaScript.** Node's default stack holds around ten thousand frames; recursing over a
linked list of a million nodes crashes. Convert to a loop or an explicit stack.

### In real systems

merge sort, quicksort, FFT and many parallel algorithms, recurse. Merge sort's idea runs external sorting
in databases and is the basis of Timsort, the standard sort in Python, in Java for objects, and in
V8.

## 5.5 Program correctness

### In one sentence

A program is correct with respect to a precondition and a postcondition if, whenever it starts with the
precondition true and stops, the postcondition is true, and loops are handled by finding an invariant
that every iteration preserves.

### The idea

#### Partial and total correctness

A program is **correct** if it produces the right output for every input. Testing checks some inputs; a
proof covers all. Proving correctness has two parts: **partial correctness**, that if the program stops,
the answer is right, and **termination**, that it does stop. Both together give **total correctness**.

#### Preconditions, postconditions and Hoare triples

An **initial assertion** or **precondition** p states what holds of the input. A **final assertion** or
**postcondition** q states what the output must satisfy. The **Hoare triple** `p {S} q` says: if p is true
before program segment S runs and S terminates, then q is true afterwards. That is exactly partial
correctness.

For a program that swaps x and y, the precondition might be "x equals a and y equals b" and the
postcondition "x equals b and y equals a".

#### Rules of inference for programs

Programs are built from a few constructs, and each has a rule:

- **Composition.** If `p {S1} q` and `q {S2} r`, then `p {S1; S2} r`. The middle assertion links the two parts.
- **Conditional.** For `if c then S`: if `(p and c) {S} q` and (p and not c) implies q, then `p {if c then S} q`.
- **If-then-else.** If `(p and c) {S1} q` and `(p and not c) {S2} q`, then `p {if c then S1 else S2} q`.
- **Loop.** If `(p and c) {S} p`, then `p {while c do S} (p and not c)`. Here p is a **loop invariant**.

#### Loop invariants

A **loop invariant** is an assertion that is true before the loop starts and stays true after every
iteration. When the loop ends, the invariant is still true and the loop condition is false. Choosing an
invariant that, together with "condition false", implies the postcondition is the creative part of the
proof.

#### A worked invariant: binary search, carefully

Binary search is the classic loop that people get wrong. Writing the invariant first makes it easy. Use a
half-open range lo to hi, and keep this invariant: every element before lo is less than x, and every
element from hi on is at least x. Then, when lo equals hi, lo is the first position where x could be.

```ts
function bsearch(xs: number[], x: number) {
  let lo = 0, hi = xs.length;
  while (lo < hi) {
    const mid = lo + ((hi - lo) >> 1);
    if (xs[mid] < x) lo = mid + 1; else hi = mid;
  }
  return lo < xs.length && xs[lo] === x ? lo : -1;
}
console.log(bsearch([2, 4, 4, 4, 7, 9, 12], 4));
```

```text
1
```

Maintenance: if xs[mid] is less than x, every element up to mid is less than x because the array is
sorted, so lo can move to mid plus 1. Otherwise xs[mid] is at least x, so hi can move to mid. Termination:
mid is between lo and hi minus 1, so either change shrinks hi minus lo. This version also finds the first
copy of x, which the version in chapter 3 needed care to do.

#### Termination

Partial correctness says nothing if the loop runs forever. Termination is shown with a **variant** or
measure: a non-negative integer expression that strictly decreases each iteration. By well-ordering it
cannot decrease forever. For a loop `while k is less than a`, the measure a minus k works.

### Worked example

**Simple: multiplication by repeated addition.** Precondition: m and n are integers. Postcondition: x
equals mn.

```ts
function mult(m: number, n: number) {
  const a = Math.abs(n); let k = 0, x = 0;
  while (k < a) { x += m; k++; }
  return n < 0 ? -x : x;
}
console.log(mult(6, 4), mult(6, -3));
```

```text
24 -18
```

The invariant is "x equals m·k and k is at most a". Logging inside the loop for mult(6, 4) printed k equal
to 1, 2, 3, 4 with x equal to 6, 12, 18, 24, and the invariant true each time.

**Realistic: integer square root.** Precondition: n is a non-negative integer. Postcondition: r² is at
most n and (r plus 1)² is greater than n.

```ts
function isqrt(n: number) {
  let r = 0;
  while ((r + 1) * (r + 1) <= n) r++;
  return r;
}
console.log(isqrt(50), isqrt(49), isqrt(0));
```

```text
7 7 0
```

Invariant: r² is at most n. True at the start, since 0 is at most n. Kept, because the body runs only
when (r plus 1)² is at most n, and then r becomes r plus 1. At exit, the condition is false, so (r plus
1)² is greater than n. Together, the postcondition. Termination: n minus r² decreases and stays
non-negative.

**Realistic: finding the maximum.** For `best = xs[0]; for i from 1: if xs[i] is greater than best, best
= xs[i]`, the invariant is "best is the maximum of xs[0..i − 1]". At exit, i is the length, so best is the
maximum of the whole array. Node returns 9 for 4 9 2 9 1.

### Common mistake

**An invariant too weak to give the postcondition.** The wrong way: for isqrt, choosing "r is
non-negative" as the invariant. It is true and preserved, but at exit it says nothing about r being the
square root. Why it fails: the invariant must carry the meaning of the computation so far.

The fix is to ask "what has the loop accomplished after k iterations?" and write that down; the
postcondition is usually the invariant with the loop variable at its final value.

**Forgetting termination.** A loop with a correct invariant that never ends is partially correct and
useless. Always name the measure.

### In real systems

Loop invariants are how experienced engineers write tricky loops correctly the first time: binary search
boundaries, partition in quicksort, two-pointer algorithms. Writing the invariant as a comment, or as an
`assert` in debug builds, turns an off-by-one hunt into a check.

Formal tools put Hoare logic to work at scale: Dafny and Why3 verify invariants automatically, TLA+
checks invariants of distributed protocols, and the seL4 kernel and AWS's s2n TLS library have
machine-checked correctness proofs. Type systems are a lightweight version: a type is a precondition the
compiler checks.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Induction and Recursion">
<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version: to prove something for every n, prove it for the first n, and prove that
whenever it holds for one n it holds for the next. To compute something recursively, solve the smallest
case directly and build bigger cases from smaller ones.

Worked example: a staircase. If you can reach the first step, and from any step you can reach the next,
you can reach every step. The sum 1 plus 2 plus ... plus n equals n(n plus 1)/2 is proved exactly that
way.

The beginner mistake is checking a few cases and calling it proved. The fix is to write the basis and
the inductive step separately, and point at where the hypothesis is used.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At this level you choose the right form. Ordinary induction when P(k plus 1) follows from P(k); strong
induction when it needs earlier cases; structural induction for trees, strings and expressions; a loop
invariant for a loop.

Example: a function that counts the leaves of a JSON-like tree. Its correctness proof is structural: a
leaf counts 1, and an object or array counts the sum of its children, each correct by hypothesis.

```ts
type J = number | string | J[] | { [k: string]: J };
const leaves = (j: J): number =>
  typeof j !== 'object' ? 1 : Object.values(j).reduce((a: number, c) => a + leaves(c), 0);
console.log(leaves({ a: 1, b: [2, 3, { c: 'x' }], d: [] }));
```

```text
4
```

The mistake is forgetting a case: here the empty array, which correctly contributes 0. The fix is to
list every constructor of the type.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath, induction, strong induction and well-ordering are equivalent axioms about the integers, and
structural induction is induction on the number of rule applications. Generalised induction works on any
well-founded order: one with no infinite descending chain.

That is what termination proofs use. Lexicographic order on pairs proves functions like Euclid's
algorithm or Ackermann-style recursions terminate even when one argument grows. Proof assistants such as
Lean and Coq demand a well-founded measure for every recursive function.

The mistake is believing a strengthened statement is harder to prove. Often it is easier, because the
stronger hypothesis gives more to work with: proving "the sum is n² and it is odd when n is odd" can be
simpler than either alone.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

- **n equal to 0.** Many formulas hold at 0 too (the empty sum is 0); state the range explicitly.
- **Empty structures.** The empty string, empty list and empty tree are basis cases; code must handle
  them.
- **One-element inputs.** Merge sort returns them unchanged; binary search does one comparison.
- **Loops that never run.** The invariant must imply the postcondition directly when the condition is
  false at the start, as isqrt(0) shows.
- **Negative inputs.** mult handles them by working with |n| and fixing the sign at the end; a proof
  that assumes n is non-negative misses half the inputs.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

- **The horses paradox.** An inductive step that works for large k but not the smallest k proves nothing.
  Check the step at the boundary.
- **Stack depth.** A correct recursive algorithm can still crash on deep input. Correctness proofs assume
  unbounded memory.
- **Floating point.** An invariant like "s equals the sum so far" holds in exact arithmetic but not in
  floating point; the proof needs an error term.
- **Mutation during recursion.** Proofs assume the input does not change; code that mutates a shared
  array while recursing breaks the hypothesis.
- **Non-strictly decreasing measures.** A measure that stays the same on some iterations does not prove
  termination.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, the ideas show up as asserted invariants, exhaustive pattern matching, and property-based
tests. Write the invariant as an `assert` in debug builds. Use TypeScript's `never` check in `switch`
statements so adding a new variant forces every function to handle it, which is structural induction
enforced by the compiler.

Recursion over user-controlled data needs depth limits: JSON parsers, template engines and regular
expression engines have all had stack-overflow bugs from deeply nested input.

The mistake is relying on tests for a loop with tricky boundaries. The fix is the invariant first, then
the code, then the tests.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit is to trust the recursive call. When writing a recursive function, assume it works on smaller
inputs, exactly as the inductive hypothesis does, and only think about combining results. Tracing every
call by hand is the wrong mental model for anything beyond a few levels.

The second habit is to ask "what stays true?" for every loop and "what gets smaller?" for every recursion.
Those two questions catch most infinite loops and off-by-one errors before the code runs.

</Level>
<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In a larger system, invariants are the contract between components. A replicated database promises an
invariant such as "every committed write is on a majority of replicas", and every protocol step is
designed to preserve it; that is a loop invariant over the system's lifetime.

Concrete system: an append-only event log with checksums. Invariant: every entry's hash covers the
previous hash. Basis: the first entry; step: appending computes the new hash from the old. By induction,
tampering with any entry breaks every later hash, which is how Git and certificate transparency logs work.

The mistake is stating invariants only in people's heads. The fix is writing them down and checking them
in code, in monitoring, or in TLA+.

</Level>

</Level>

## Questions people ask

**why is induction valid?**

Because of well-ordering. If P failed somewhere, there would be a least n where it fails; n is not the
basis, so P(n minus 1) is true, and the inductive step then gives P(n), a contradiction.

**is strong induction more powerful than ordinary induction?**

No. Anything provable by one is provable by the other. Strong induction is just more convenient when the
step needs earlier cases.

**do I need to prove my programs correct formally?**

Rarely in full. But knowing the invariant of every non-trivial loop is cheap and prevents most loop bugs,
and formal methods are used where failures are expensive: kernels, cryptography, distributed protocols.

**when should I prefer recursion to iteration?**

When the data or the problem is recursive, such as trees, grammars or divide and conquer, and depth is
bounded. Prefer iteration when depth can be large or the recursion repeats work.

## Key takeaways

- Induction proves P(n) for all n from a basis step and an inductive step that uses P(k) to get P(k plus
  1).
- Strong induction assumes all earlier cases; it needs as many basis cases as the step reaches back.
- Well-ordering, every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a least element, underlies every form.
- Recursive definitions build functions, sets, strings and trees from a basis and rules; structural
  induction proves properties of them, one case per rule.
- Recursive algorithms are proved correct by induction on input size; they need a measure that shrinks.
- Merge sort sorts in O(n log n) comparisons by splitting, recursing and merging.
- Program correctness is a precondition, a postcondition, a loop invariant and a termination measure.

## Glossary

- **Mathematical induction**: proof from a basis step and an inductive step.
- **Basis step, inductive step, inductive hypothesis**: P(b); P(k) implies P(k plus 1); the assumption P(k).
- **Strong induction**: the step assumes P(j) for every j up to k.
- **Well-ordering principle**: every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a least element.
- **Recursive definition**: a basis plus rules building new values or elements from old ones.
- **Fibonacci numbers**: f0 equal to 0, f1 equal to 1, fₙ equal to fₙ₋₁ plus fₙ₋₂.
- **Lamé's theorem**: Euclid uses at most five times the number of digits of the smaller number.
- **Σ*, empty string λ**: all strings over Σ; the string of length 0.
- **Full binary tree**: a single vertex, or a root with two full binary subtrees.
- **Structural induction**: proving a property for basis elements and preserved by each rule.
- **Recursive algorithm**: one that solves smaller instances of the same problem.
- **Memoisation**: storing results of calls to avoid recomputing them.
- **Merge sort, merging**: sort by halving, recursing and merging two sorted lists.
- **Partial, total correctness**: right if it stops; right and it stops.
- **Precondition, postcondition**: assertions before and after a program segment.
- **Hoare triple**: `p {S} q`, if p holds and S terminates, q holds.
- **Loop invariant**: an assertion true before and after every iteration.
- **Variant, measure**: a non-negative quantity that decreases, proving termination.
