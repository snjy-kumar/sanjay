The First Way is about making work move quickly and smoothly from
development, through operations, to the customer. This chapter gives the
six principles behind that: make the work visible, limit how much is in
progress, keep batches small, cut handoffs, find and lift the constraint,
and remove waste. Each one is simple to say and surprisingly hard to do,
and every technical practice in Part III is one of them made concrete.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- make all the work in a team, or in my own week, visible on one board, including unplanned work;
- set and tune a limit on work in progress, and explain with Little's law why it shortens lead time;
- show with a worked example why small batches finish sooner and fail smaller;
- count the handoffs in a value stream and remove or automate the costly ones;
- find the constraint in a system and apply the five focusing steps to it;
- name the kinds of waste in software delivery and spot each in a real team.

<a href={withBase('/books/the-devops-handbook/01-agile-continuous-delivery-and-the-three-ways/')}>chapter 1</a>,

## Make our work visible

### In one sentence

Knowledge work is invisible by default, so the first step to flow is to put
every piece of work, and where it is, somewhere everyone can see.

### The idea

In a factory you can see the work. A pile of half-finished parts in front
of a machine tells everyone there is a problem there. In software, work is
a branch, a ticket, a message, a half-written document. None of it piles up
physically, so a queue of thirty items waiting for review looks exactly like
an empty one unless someone counts.

That invisibility is dangerous in three ways. Nobody can see where work is
stuck, so nobody fixes the queue. People keep starting new work because they
cannot see how much is already in flight. And unplanned work, such as
outages, urgent requests and favours, quietly eats capacity without ever
appearing in the plan.

The fix the book recommends is a visual work board, often a kanban board:
columns for each step of the value stream, and a card for each item, moved
left to right as it progresses. The board is not decoration. It is how a
team sees queues, sees blocked items and sees how much is in progress at
once. When work is visible, waits become obvious, and lead time can be
measured from when a card enters the board to when it leaves.

The important rule is that **all** work goes on the board: features, bug
fixes, operational tasks, unplanned incidents and requests from other
teams. A board that shows only planned features makes the team look
underused while people are actually firefighting.

<Figure caption="A simple work board. Read it left to right; the number of cards in each column is the queue at that step. The Review column is visibly the pile." art={`
 +----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+
 | Ready    | Doing    | Review   | Testing  | Done     |
 +----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+
 | [A]      | [D]      | [F] [G]  | [K]      | [M] [N]  |
 | [B]      | [E]      | [H] [I]  |          |          |
 | [C]      |          | [J]      |          |          |
 +----------+----------+----------+----------+----------+
   3 waiting  2 active   5 waiting  1 active   shipped
`} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** For a week I write every task I touch on a sticky note on my
wall, including the ones I did not plan: "fix the broken link someone
reported", "help a friend with a deploy". At the end of the week a third of
the notes were unplanned. That explains why my planned exercises slipped,
and it is information I never had before.

**Realistic.** A team of six has a board with only planned features on it.
It looks like they finish two features a week, and stakeholders ask why so
few. The team adds a swimlane for unplanned work and puts every incident,
support request and urgent fix on it as a card. Within two weeks the board
shows that about half of the team's time goes to unplanned work. The
conversation changes from "why are you slow?" to "how do we reduce the
unplanned work?", which is the right question.

### Step by step

Setting up a board that actually makes work visible:

1. **Name the steps** of the value stream from chapter 1 as columns, from
   "ready" to "running in production". Not "done in dev".
2. **Put every current item on a card**, including operational and
   unplanned work. Ask each person what they are working on and write it
   down.
3. **Mark blocked items** with a clear sign and the reason they are blocked.
4. **Record the date** each card enters the board and each time it moves.
5. **Review the board daily**, walking it from right to left, so the
   conversation starts with work closest to the customer.
6. **After two weeks, compute lead time** for finished cards from their
   dates, and count how many cards sat in each column.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is a board that is maintained for managers, not used by
the team: updated once a week, missing the work that was not planned, and
showing "done" when the code is merged but not deployed. That board hides
exactly what it is meant to show. The fix is to make the board the place
where work is picked up and moved, so it cannot drift from reality, and to
end the columns at production.

### In real systems

Most teams use a tool such as an issue tracker with a board view rather
than a wall. The tool matters less than two habits: unplanned work gets a
card immediately, even if it takes ten minutes, and "done" means running in
production. Many teams also automate card movement from pull request and
deploy events, so the board stays honest without anyone remembering to
drag a card.

## Limit work in progress

### In one sentence

Limiting work in progress, often written WIP, means capping how many items
can be in each step at once, so people finish work before starting new
work and lead times shrink.

### The idea

In a factory, work in progress is limited by physical space. In software it
is limited by nothing at all. Anyone can start another ticket, open another
branch or accept another request, and the cost is invisible until lead
times stretch and everything is "nearly done".

There are two costs. The first is queueing. By Little's law, from chapter
1, average lead time equals work in progress divided by throughput. If a
team finishes five items a week and has twenty in flight, each takes about
four weeks. If it has five in flight, about one week. Same team, same
speed, a quarter of the lead time.

The second cost is task switching. Every time a person switches between
items, they lose time reloading the context: what was I doing, what was
the state, which test was failing. Several items in progress at once means
constant switching, which lowers throughput too. So high work in progress
makes each item slower in two ways at once.

A work-in-progress limit is a rule on the board: no more than N cards in
this column. When a column is full, nobody can pull a new card into it.
Instead, they help finish something downstream. That is the change in
behaviour the limit is for: **stop starting, start finishing**.

<Flow caption="What a WIP limit changes when a column is full. Follow the path: instead of starting new work, the person moves right and helps finish existing work." steps={[
  "I finish my current item",
  "Is the next column full?",
  "Yes: help review or test what is there",
  "That column drains",
  "Now pull the next item",
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I have five half-finished exercises in my study repository. I
set a limit of one exercise and one note in progress. The first week is
uncomfortable, because I keep wanting to start something new when I get
stuck. By the end of it I have finished three of the five, instead of
nudging all five forward a little.

**Realistic.** Using Little's law, a team that finishes five items a week
can see what different limits would do to its average lead time:

```js
// Little's law: average time in system = WIP / throughput.
const throughputPerWeek = 5;
for (const wip of [20, 10, 5, 3]) {
  console.log(`WIP ${String(wip).padStart(2)} -> about ${(wip / throughputPerWeek).toFixed(1)} weeks per item`);
}
```

Output:

```text
WIP 20 -> about 4.0 weeks per item
WIP 10 -> about 2.0 weeks per item
WIP  5 -> about 1.0 weeks per item
WIP  3 -> about 0.6 weeks per item
```

The team does not get faster; items simply stop queueing. In practice,
throughput often rises too, because people switch less.

### Step by step

<Trace columns={["Week", "WIP limit", "Items in progress", "Finished this week", "Typical lead time"]} rows={[
  ["1", "none", "20", "5", "about 4 weeks"],
  ["2", "10 per team", "14", "6", "falling"],
  ["3", "10 per team", "10", "6", "about 2 weeks"],
  ["4", "2 per person in Doing, 3 in Review", "8", "7", "under 2 weeks"],
]} caption="Introducing a limit over four weeks. Watch the third column fall while the fourth holds steady or rises: that is lead time shrinking without anyone working harder." />

1. **Count the current work in progress** in each column, honestly.
2. **Set a limit slightly below the current level** in the column with the
   biggest pile. Do not halve it overnight.
3. **When the column is full, swarm**: the person who would have started
   something new helps finish what is in the column.
4. **Watch for blocked items.** A limit exposes them, because they now
   stop new work from entering.
5. **Lower the limit gradually** while lead time keeps falling and people
   are not idle for long stretches.
6. **Stop lowering it** when people are regularly blocked with nothing they
   can help with. That is the right level for now.

### Common mistake

The first common mistake is to set the limit and then ignore it whenever a
"priority" item arrives. Every new item then pushes the column over, and the
limit becomes decoration. The fix is to treat an urgent item as replacing
another, not adding to them: if it comes in, something goes back to the
queue.

The second mistake is to set limits so low that people sit idle. A WIP
limit is tuned, not absolute. If a person cannot help anywhere because the
work needs a skill they do not have, the right answer is cross-training,
not a looser limit forever.

### In real systems

Teams often limit pull requests open per person, or set a team-wide cap on
work in a "review" column, because review queues are a common pile. Some
also cap the number of in-flight projects at the portfolio level, which is
where the largest queues usually hide: twenty projects started, three
finished.

24 / 4, about six weeks. At 8 in progress, 8 / 4, about two weeks, with no

</details>

## Reduce batch sizes

### In one sentence

Moving work through the stream in small batches, ideally one item at a
time, makes the first results arrive much sooner, makes problems show up
earlier, and makes each failure smaller.

### The idea

A batch is the amount of work that moves from one step to the next
together. In a factory, a big batch means making a thousand parts at step
one before any of them move to step two. In software, a big batch is a
release with hundreds of changes, a branch with weeks of work, or a pull
request with two thousand lines.

Big batches feel efficient, because setting up each step is done once. But
they have three costs. First, the first finished item arrives late, because
everything waits for the whole batch. Second, defects are discovered late:
if step four finds that step one did something wrong, every item in the
batch is already wrong. Third, variation goes up: big batches arrive in
lumps, which creates queues downstream.

The ideal Lean aims for is **single-piece flow**: each item moves to the
next step as soon as it is done. In software, the equivalents are small
commits, short-lived branches, small pull requests and frequent deploys. The
cost that makes big batches attractive, the setup cost of each step, is
attacked directly, usually by automation: if a deploy takes one command,
there is no reason to save deploys up.

<Compare left="Large batch" right="Single-piece flow" caption="Ten envelopes, four steps. Both finish at the same time, but compare when the first result arrives and when a mistake would be noticed." rows={[
  ["Fold all 10, then stuff all 10, then seal all 10, then stamp all 10", "Fold, stuff, seal and stamp one envelope, then the next"],
  ["First finished envelope at minute 31", "First finished envelope at minute 4"],
  ["A misfold shows up only at sealing, after 10 were folded", "A misfold shows up at minute 3, on one envelope"],
  ["All 10 at minute 40", "All 10 at minute 40"],
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** A classic Lean exercise is mailing envelopes: fold a letter,
put it in, seal it, stamp it. With ten envelopes and one minute per step,
here is what each approach produces:

```js
// 10 envelopes, 4 steps of 1 minute each: fold, stuff, seal, stamp.
const items = 10, steps = 4, perStep = 1;
const batchFirstDone = (steps - 1) * items * perStep + perStep;
const batchAllDone = steps * items * perStep;
const flowFirstDone = steps * perStep;
const flowAllDone = steps * items * perStep;
console.log(`batch: first envelope done at ${batchFirstDone} min, all at ${batchAllDone} min`);
console.log(`flow:  first envelope done at ${flowFirstDone} min, all at ${flowAllDone} min`);
```

Output:

```text
batch: first envelope done at 31 min, all at 40 min
flow:  first envelope done at 4 min, all at 40 min
```

Total time is the same, but one-piece flow delivers the first result
almost eight times sooner, and a mistake at any step shows up on the first
envelope instead of after ten have been made wrong. In practice, one-piece
flow usually finishes sooner overall too, because people skip the handling
of stacks.

**Realistic.** A developer builds a feature on a branch for three weeks:
forty commits, two thousand lines. The review takes two days because
nobody can hold it in their head, finds a design problem that touches half
the code, and the merge conflicts with two other branches. Split into ten
small pull requests merged over the same three weeks, each review takes
an hour, the design problem surfaces in the second one when it is cheap to
fix, and conflicts are a few lines each.

### Step by step

Why a small batch finds a defect sooner, for a change with a bug in its
first part:

1. **Big batch.** The developer writes all ten parts, then the batch goes to
   review and test. The bug in part one is found after all ten parts are
   written, and parts two to ten may depend on it.
2. **Small batch.** Part one goes to review and test alone. The bug is found
   right away, before parts two to ten build on it.
3. **Cost to fix in the big batch.** Fix part one and check whether parts
   two to ten need changes. Work has been built on a wrong foundation.
4. **Cost to fix in the small batch.** Fix part one. Nothing else exists
   yet.
5. **Feedback time.** In the big batch the developer hears about the bug
   weeks after writing it, when the context is gone. In the small batch,
   hours later, while it is fresh.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to keep batch sizes big because each step is
expensive to run: "we only deploy monthly because a deploy takes a day".
That treats the setup cost as fixed. The fix is to attack the setup cost:
automate the deploy, the test run and the environment, until small batches
are cheap. Most of Part III is about exactly this.

Another mistake is to make batches small on paper but not in practice, for
example small commits that sit on a branch for a month. A batch is only as
small as the unit that actually moves to the next step and on to
production.

### In real systems

Small batches show up as trunk-based development (chapter 11), small pull
requests, feature flags that let incomplete work merge safely, and deploys
of one change at a time. Teams that work this way find it easier to answer
"what changed?" when something breaks, because the answer is one small
diff.

## Reduce the number of handoffs

### In one sentence

Every time work passes from one person or team to another it waits in a
queue and loses context, so fewer handoffs means faster and more accurate
flow.

### The idea

To get code from a commit to production, many organisations need several
teams: someone to provision a server, someone to change the network,
someone to configure the load balancer, someone to approve the change,
someone to deploy it. Each one is a **handoff**: the work leaves one queue
and enters another.

Handoffs cost in two ways. First, each one adds a wait. The receiving team
has its own queue and its own priorities, so a request might sit for days
even if the actual task takes ten minutes. Five handoffs of two days each is
two weeks of lead time for an hour of work.

Second, each handoff loses information. The person receiving the request
knows less about why it is needed than the person sending it. They ask
questions, which goes back through the queue. They make assumptions, which
are sometimes wrong. That shows up as low percent complete and accurate at
each handoff.

The book's fixes are to reduce handoffs, by giving teams the ability to do
more of the work themselves, and to automate the handoffs that remain, so
that a request becomes a self-service action rather than a ticket in
someone's queue. That is why later chapters push so hard on self-service
environments and deploys.

<Figure caption="Five handoffs to get one change into production. Each arrow is a queue; count the arrows, not the boxes, to estimate the lead time." art={`
 Dev --ticket--> Infra --ticket--> Network --ticket--> Security
                                                          |
 Customer <--deploy-- Ops <--approval-- Change board <--ticket
`} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I want a new domain for a side project. I have to ask a friend
who owns the DNS account to add a record, then wait for them to have time.
It takes four days for a two-minute change. When I move the domain to an
account I control, the next change takes two minutes. One handoff removed.

**Realistic.** A team needs a database for a new service. Today they file a
ticket with the database team, who file one with infrastructure for a
server, who file one with network for firewall rules. The whole chain takes
about three weeks. The organisation introduces a self-service catalogue
where a team can request a standard database with one form; the platform
creates it automatically with the standard firewall rules. The handoffs
are still there in principle, but they are encoded in automation rather
than tickets, and the database is ready in under an hour.

### Step by step

Counting and removing handoffs on a value stream map:

1. **Mark every point** where work changes hands between people or teams.
2. **For each handoff, record the typical wait** and how often the receiver
   has to ask a question back.
3. **Rank them** by total delay: wait times multiplied by how often the
   step is needed.
4. **For the worst one, ask**: could the sending team do this itself with
   the right access and tools? If yes, give them that.
5. **If not, ask**: could the receiving team turn the task into a
   self-service, automated action? If yes, build that.
6. **If neither**, at least make the request complete: a template that
   captures everything the receiver needs, raising the handoff's %C/A.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to "solve" handoffs by putting everyone in one
meeting, or by assigning a coordinator. That adds a person to the chain
rather than removing a link. The fix is to remove the need for the handoff
through access, tooling or automation.

Another mistake is to give teams access without the safeguards the
specialist team provided. Self-service only works when the automation
encodes the specialist's knowledge: standard firewall rules, standard
backups, standard security settings.

### In real systems

This is the reason internal developer platforms exist: they turn common
requests, such as a new environment, a database, a DNS record or a
deployment, into self-service actions that encode the platform team's
standards. The handoff becomes a pull request against configuration, or a
button, instead of a ticket.

</details>

## Identify and elevate the constraint

### In one sentence

Every system has one step that limits its throughput, the constraint, and
any improvement that is not made at the constraint does not make the
system faster.

### The idea

This principle comes from Eliyahu Goldratt's theory of constraints. Think
of a pipe made of segments of different widths. However wide the other
segments are, the water flows only as fast as the narrowest one allows.
Widening any other segment changes nothing. Widening the narrow one
increases the flow, until some other segment becomes the narrowest.

In a value stream the narrow segment is the **constraint**, or bottleneck:
the step with the lowest capacity relative to the demand on it. Work piles
up in front of it, and everything after it waits for it. The consequence is
blunt. An hour saved at the constraint is an hour gained by the whole
system. An hour saved anywhere else is, in Goldratt's phrase, a mirage: it
just makes the pile in front of the constraint bigger.

Goldratt gave a method for working with constraints, usually called the
five focusing steps:

1. **Identify** the constraint.
2. **Exploit** it: make sure it never wastes time, by never letting it sit
   idle or work on the wrong things.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to it: other steps work at the pace the
   constraint can absorb, not faster.
4. **Elevate** it: add capacity, automate it or redesign it.
5. **Repeat**: once it is no longer the constraint, find the new one, and
   do not let inertia keep the old rules in place.

The handbook adds an observation from DevOps transformations: the
constraint tends to move in a typical order. First it is creating
environments, because getting a test or production environment takes
weeks. Once environments are on demand, it is deployment, because releases
are manual. Once deployment is automated, it is test setup and running,
because tests are slow or manual. Once tests are fast, it is the
architecture itself, because tightly coupled systems force changes to wait
for each other. After that, the constraint is often in product: deciding
what to build and learning from what was built.

  "Environment creation (chapter 9)",

  "Test setup and run (chapter 10)",
  "Overly tight architecture (chapter 13)",
  "Product: what to build next (chapter 17)",

### Worked example

**Simple.** My study loop has four steps: read, write code, write a note,
publish. I can read and code fast, but each note takes me a long evening,
so drafts pile up. The note step is the constraint. Reading faster only adds
to the pile. Exploiting it means writing notes at my best hour, not at the
end of the day. Elevating it means a template that removes the formatting
work.

**Realistic.** A team measures each step's capacity in changes per day and
finds where throughput is set:

```js
const capacityPerDay = { code: 12, review: 8, test: 3, deploy: 10 };
const [constraint, rate] = Object.entries(capacityPerDay)
  .reduce((a, b) => (b[1] < a[1] ? b : a));
console.log(`constraint: ${constraint}, system throughput: ${rate} changes/day`);
capacityPerDay.code = 24; // double the speed of coding
const after = Math.min(...Object.values(capacityPerDay));
console.log(`after doubling code: ${after} changes/day`);
capacityPerDay.test = 9; // elevate the constraint
const [c2, r2] = Object.entries(capacityPerDay).reduce((a, b) => (b[1] < a[1] ? b : a));
console.log(`after fixing test: ${r2} changes/day, new constraint: ${c2}`);
```

Output:

```text
constraint: test, system throughput: 3 changes/day
after doubling code: 3 changes/day
after fixing test: 8 changes/day, new constraint: review
```

Doubling coding speed achieved nothing. Raising test capacity tripled
throughput, and moved the constraint to review, which is now the next thing
to look at.

### Step by step

<Trace columns={["Focusing step", "What the team does", "Result"]} rows={[
  ["1. Identify", "Count cards waiting per column for two weeks; Testing always has the biggest pile", "Test is the constraint"],
  ["2. Exploit", "Testers stop attending status meetings; builds arrive with data and config ready", "Test capacity rises a little with no new spend"],
  ["3. Subordinate", "Developers stop starting new features when Testing is full and help write automated tests", "Pile in front of Testing stops growing"],
  ["4. Elevate", "Automate the regression suite and run it on every build", "Test capacity rises sharply"],
  ["5. Repeat", "Count piles again; Review now has the biggest one", "Start again at step 1 with Review"],
]} caption="The five focusing steps applied to a slow test stage. Read down the middle column: each step costs more than the one before, which is why exploit and subordinate come first." />

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to improve whatever is easiest or most interesting
rather than the constraint. Teams buy faster build servers when the real
wait is a manual approval, or rewrite the deploy tool when environments
take a month. The fix is to identify the constraint with data, usually by
looking at where work piles up, before spending effort anywhere.

The second mistake is to skip "exploit" and "subordinate" and jump to
"elevate", usually by hiring or buying. Much of a constraint's lost
capacity is often waste: it waits for missing inputs or does work that is
not needed. Recovering that is cheaper and faster.

### In real systems

A practical way to find the constraint is to look at queue lengths and
wait times on the board or in the pipeline: the stage with the longest
queue and the longest waits is usually it. In CI pipelines the constraint
is often a long test stage or a shared, limited test environment. In
organisations it is often a specialist team that every change needs, such
as security review or database changes.

<details>
<summary>3. Why does the book say constraints move in a typical order?</summary>

Lifting one reveals the next. Once environments are on demand, the next
slowest thing is usually deployment, then testing, then architecture.

</details>

## Eliminate hardship and waste

### In one sentence

Waste is anything in the value stream that takes time or effort without
adding value for the customer, and hardship is the stressful, heroic work
that comes from a poorly designed stream; both should be found and removed.

### The idea

Lean manufacturing names waste as anything the customer would not pay for.
The handbook uses a version adapted to software by Mary and Tom
Poppendieck, and adds hardship: work that is stressful, dangerous or
exhausting for the people doing it. The list of waste types matters less
than the habit of seeing them, but having names makes them easier to
spot.

The categories the book lists:

- **Partially done work**: anything started and not finished, such as
  unreleased code, untested features, unmerged branches. It loses value
  as it ages.
- **Extra processes**: steps that add no value, such as approvals nobody
  reads or documents nobody uses.
- **Extra features**: features nobody needs, which still cost testing,
  maintenance and complexity.
- **Task switching**: moving people between projects or tasks, losing
  context each time.
- **Waiting**: any delay between steps.
- **Motion**: effort spent moving information or people, such as
  handoffs, re-explaining, hunting for files.
- **Defects**: wrong or missing information, and the rework it causes.
- **Nonstandard or manual work**: work that depends on someone doing it by
  hand, differently each time, such as a manual server build.
- **Heroics**: individuals working unreasonable hours to make a release
  happen, which signals a broken stream and burns people out.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I list the waste in my own week. Partially done work: three
exercises started. Waiting: an hour each morning waiting for a slow
dependency install. Manual work: I format notes by hand. Heroics: I stayed
up till two to publish a note on schedule. Each one has an obvious
response: finish before starting, cache dependencies, use a template, and
publish when ready rather than on a deadline I set myself.

**Realistic.** A team runs a waste walk over a month's work. They find a
weekly status report that takes three hours and is read by nobody (extra
process), a test environment rebuilt by hand each sprint (nonstandard
manual work), four features behind flags that were never turned on (extra
features), and a release every month that needs one engineer to work all
weekend (heroics). They drop the report, script the environment, delete
the dead features, and make the release a pipeline job. None of these
needed new technology; all of them needed someone to name the waste.

### Step by step

A waste walk, done in an hour:

1. **Take the value stream map** from chapter 1 and the board from this
   chapter.
2. **Go through each step** and ask, for each category of waste, "where
   does this show up here?". Write each instance on a card.
3. **Estimate the cost** of each instance in hours per month, roughly.
4. **Mark heroics separately.** They are a safety signal, not just a cost.
5. **Pick the top three by cost**, and for each write one change that would
   remove or reduce it.
6. **Put those changes on the board** as real work, with a card each. Waste
   removal that is not on the board does not happen.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat waste as people being lazy or inefficient.
Almost all waste is built into the system: a required approval, a manual
step nobody automated, a release schedule that creates weekend work. The
fix is to look for the system cause and change it, never to ask people to
work harder.

The second mistake is to celebrate heroics. The engineer who saves the
release by working all night is praised, which reinforces the system that
needed the heroics. The fix is to thank the person and then fix the system
so it is not needed next time.

### In real systems

In software teams, the biggest wastes are usually waiting (for reviews,
environments, approvals), partially done work (long-lived branches and
unreleased features) and manual work (hand-built servers, manual deploys,
manual tests). Most of Part III's practices are targeted at exactly these
three.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 2, The First Way: The Principles of Flow">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest version of the First Way is: finish things before starting new
ones, and make the work you have visible. Everything else in the chapter
refines those two ideas.

A worked example for a beginner: I write every task I have on a list,
including the ones nobody asked me to plan. I pick one, finish it, and
only then pick the next. After a week I compare how many I finished with
the week before, when I had six things open at once. The number is usually
higher, and each item took fewer days from start to end.

The beginner mistake is to think that having many things open is being
productive. It feels like progress because everything moves a little. The
fix is to count finished items, not started ones.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level the principles combine on a real board. Columns
for each step, a card for each item including unplanned work, a WIP limit
on the column with the biggest pile, and a rule that when a column is full
people help downstream.

A worked example: a team adds a limit of three to its Review column. On day
two the column is full, and a developer who just finished coding has
nothing to pull. Instead of starting a new item, she reviews one of the
three. The review queue drains in a day, the item she would have started
waits a day, and three items reach production sooner. The board shows the
change: fewer cards in Review, and shorter lead times on the finished ones.

The intermediate mistake is to apply a limit only to the Doing column. Piles
usually form in waiting columns, such as Review or Ready for deploy. The
fix is to limit the columns where work waits, not just where it is done.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath the chapter are two pieces of theory. The first is queueing:
Little's law relates work in progress, throughput and lead time, and a
second result from queueing theory says that as a step's utilisation
approaches full, the wait in front of it grows very sharply, not linearly.
A step that is busy almost all the time has a long queue almost all the
time.

That is why the book does not want every step at full utilisation. A
non-constraint step running at full capacity only feeds the pile in front
of the constraint. A constraint running at full capacity is fine, but it
needs a small buffer in front so it never starves.

The second is the theory of constraints. Throughput of the whole system
equals the throughput of the constraint, so the value of an hour of
improvement depends entirely on where it is made. That turns improvement
into a targeting problem: measure first, then act at one point.

The advanced mistake is to apply these ideas to creative work as if it were
an assembly line, demanding identical cycle times for design. The fix is to
apply them most strongly where work should be repeatable, from commit to
production, and more loosely to design.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

**A WIP limit of one per person.** It works for focused work but breaks
when an item is blocked for a day waiting on another team. The person must
either swarm elsewhere or the limit must allow one blocked item plus one
active one.

**A board with zero unplanned work.** It almost always means unplanned work
is not being recorded. Ask people what interrupted them this week and
compare.

**A constraint outside the team.** The slowest step is a security review run
by another department. The team cannot elevate it directly, but it can
exploit it: send complete, well-prepared requests so the reviewer never
wastes time asking questions.

**A batch of one that is still big.** A single pull request with three
thousand lines is a batch of one in count but huge in size. Batch size is
about the amount of change, not the number of tickets.

**An idle step.** A deploy step that sits idle most of the day is not waste
if it is not the constraint. Making it busier would not help.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

**A WIP limit plus a priority interrupt.** An urgent fix arrives while
Doing is full. If it is simply added, the limit is broken; if it waits,
customers suffer. The rule that handles both is that urgent work pre-empts:
one item goes back to Ready, explicitly, and the board shows it.

**Small batches plus a slow pipeline.** A team switches to small pull
requests, but each one takes an hour to pass CI. Now there are many more
pipeline runs, and the pipeline becomes the constraint. Small batches
expose the setup cost; the next step is to make the pipeline faster.

**Lifting a constraint plus an unchanged rule.** The team automated tests,
so test is no longer the constraint, but the old rule "developers must
wait for the weekly test run" is still in place. The constraint has moved,
but the rule has kept the old one artificially alive. The fifth focusing
step, repeat, exists to catch this.

**Removing a handoff plus losing a safeguard.** Teams get direct access to
production databases to cut a handoff, and an accidental change drops a
table. The handoff was waste, but the safeguard was not. The fix is
self-service with the safeguard automated, such as migrations that go
through review and a pipeline.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production organisations, flow metrics come from tooling. The issue
tracker records when items enter and leave columns, so cycle time and work
in progress can be charted automatically. A cumulative flow diagram, which
plots how many items are in each state over time, shows piles growing as
widening bands, and is one of the most useful views a team can have.

At scale, the biggest queues are often between teams and at the portfolio
level: dozens of projects started, few finished. A WIP limit on projects,
not only on tasks, is often the change with the largest effect.

What breaks: dashboards that show utilisation make people look idle when
they are rightly holding back, so managers push them to start work. The
fix is to show lead time and throughput, not utilisation, at every level.

The production mistake is to treat the board as reporting, not as the
system. When the board and reality drift, every metric on it becomes
wrong. Automating card moves from pull requests and deploys keeps it
honest.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset of the First Way is to care about the flow of the work, not
the busyness of the people. A team where everyone is busy and nothing
ships is failing. A team where someone is occasionally idle but work flows
quickly is succeeding.

It also teaches suspicion of local optimisation. When someone proposes
making a step faster, the first question is "is it the constraint?". This
applies far beyond software. A hospital that speeds up admissions but not
discharges just fills its corridors.

And it teaches respect for the people doing the work. Waste and hardship
are designed into systems, not chosen by people. Heroics are a symptom,
not a virtue.

The mindset mistake is to hear "limit work in progress" as "do less". The
point is to deliver more, sooner, by finishing. The fix is to measure
delivered work and lead time, and let the results argue.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

Flow is a property of the architecture as well as the process. A system
where every change needs coordinated updates to several components has
built-in handoffs and big batches, however good the team's board is.

A concrete example: a monolith with one shared test environment. Every
team must queue for the environment, so it is the constraint, and every
change waits for it. Moving to environments created on demand per branch
removes that constraint completely. Further, splitting the monolith along
team boundaries removes the need for most changes to be tested together at
all.

When I design a system, I ask flow questions alongside the usual ones: how
many teams must touch a typical change? How many shared resources must it
queue for? What is the smallest independently deployable unit? The answers
put a floor under lead time that no process change can beat.

The design mistake is to fix flow problems only with process, such as more
coordination meetings, when the cause is coupling in the architecture. The
fix is to reduce the coupling, which chapter 13 covers.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

How I learn this chapter: I put it into practice in my own work for two
weeks. A board with every task, including unplanned ones. A WIP limit of
one exercise and one note. Small commits pushed daily. And a weekly look at
where things piled up.

Then I run the three scripts on this page with my own numbers: the
envelope comparison with my own batch sizes, Little's law with my own
throughput, and the constraint finder with my own steps. If my results
disagree with what I see on the board, I have measured something wrong.

Finally I do a waste walk on my own setup and fix the top item.

Done when I have run with a WIP limit for two weeks, can show the lead time
before and after, can name my own constraint and the evidence for it, and
have removed one piece of waste from my loop.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**What is the right WIP limit?** There is no universal number. Start
slightly below current work in progress, lower it while lead time keeps
falling, and stop when people are regularly blocked with nothing to help
with. Many teams end up around one or two active items per person.

**Does limiting work in progress mean people will sit idle?** Sometimes, a
little. That is acceptable at non-constraint steps. Usually, though, they
swarm on work downstream, which is more useful than starting new work.

**Is kanban required?** No. Any way of making the work, the queues and the
limits visible works. Kanban is popular because it does all three simply.

**What if the constraint is a person?** Then exploit it first: protect that
person's time from meetings and unneeded work, and send them complete
requests. Then elevate: teach others what they know, or automate parts of
their work.

**How small should a batch be?** As small as the setup cost allows. If a
deploy is one command, a batch of one change is fine. If it is a day of
manual work, reduce that cost first.

**Is all process waste?** No. Process that prevents defects or protects
people adds value. Process that nobody uses, that duplicates another check,
or that only exists because "we've always done it" is waste.

## Key takeaways

- Knowledge work is invisible, so put all work, including unplanned work, on one board.
- Lead time equals work in progress divided by throughput, so lowering work in progress shortens lead time.
- Stop starting, start finishing: when a column is full, help downstream.
- Small batches deliver first results sooner, show defects earlier and fail smaller.
- Every handoff is a queue and a loss of context; remove it or automate it.
- Only improvements at the constraint make the system faster; find it with data.
- The five focusing steps are identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat.
- Waste and heroics are built into systems, so fix the system, not the people.

## Glossary

- **Kanban board**: a visual board with a column per step and a card per item.
- **Work in progress (WIP)**: items that have been started and not finished.
- **WIP limit**: a cap on the number of items allowed in a step at once.
- **Task switching**: moving between items, which costs time to reload context.
- **Batch size**: the amount of work that moves to the next step together.
- **Single-piece flow**: moving each item to the next step as soon as it is done.
- **Setup cost**: the fixed cost of running a step once, which makes big batches tempting.
- **Handoff**: the point where work passes from one person or team to another.
- **Constraint (bottleneck)**: the step that limits the throughput of the whole system.
- **Theory of constraints**: Goldratt's method of improving a system by focusing on its constraint.
- **Five focusing steps**: identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat.
- **Waste**: anything that costs time or effort without adding value for the customer.
- **Heroics**: unreasonable individual effort needed to keep a broken stream working.
- **Cumulative flow diagram**: a chart of how many items are in each state over time.
