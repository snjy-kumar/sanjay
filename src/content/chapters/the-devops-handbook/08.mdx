Chapter 7 argued for teams that own their services end to end. Most
organisations cannot get there overnight: operations skills are scarce,
and there are far more development teams than operations engineers. This
chapter, the last in Part II, describes practical ways to bring operations
into development's daily work anyway. Some build tools that make operations
self-service; some put operations people next to developers; and some
simply change the meetings and boards both groups use, so their work is
shared and visible.

## Why integrate operations into development

### In one sentence

The goal is for development teams to get the operations help they need
quickly and to feel the operational consequences of their work, whatever
the org chart looks like.

### The idea

The book's aim, carried over from chapter 7, is market-oriented outcomes:
each team able to deliver value to customers without waiting in other
teams' queues. In many organisations, operations is a central group serving
many development teams. That group is usually overloaded, so every request
waits, and developers rarely see how their decisions affect production.

The chapter does not ask organisations to dissolve operations. It asks
them to integrate operations into development's daily work, so that:

- routine operations needs, environments, deployments, monitoring, are met quickly, ideally without a ticket;
- operations knowledge flows into design decisions early, not after the code is written;
- developers see and share the operational work their services create;
- operations engineers understand what developers are trying to achieve and why.

The book's examples describe operations groups that reshaped themselves
around development teams, through platforms, embedding and shared rituals,
and the improvements in deploy speed and reliability that followed. It is
careful to show that this works in organisations with central operations
groups, not only in startups where everyone does everything.

#### Why overloaded queues are so slow

A useful way to see the problem is a rule of thumb from queueing theory,
popularised by *The Phoenix Project*: the relative wait for a resource
grows as its share of busy time divided by its share of idle time. A small
script, run with node, shows how quickly that grows:

```js
// Relative wait in a queue grows as busy / idle (a rule of thumb from queueing theory).
for (const busy of [0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99]) {
  const wait = busy / (1 - busy);
  console.log(`${String(Math.round(busy * 100)).padStart(3)}% busy -> relative wait ${wait.toFixed(1).padStart(5)}`);
}
```

It prints:

```text
 50% busy -> relative wait   1.0
 70% busy -> relative wait   2.3
 80% busy -> relative wait   4.0
 90% busy -> relative wait   9.0
 95% busy -> relative wait  19.0
 99% busy -> relative wait  99.0
```

The rule is a simplification, but its shape is the lesson. An operations
team that is 90 percent busy makes each request wait about nine times
longer than one that is half busy; at 99 percent, almost a hundred times.
Organisations often try to keep specialists fully utilised to save money,
and in doing so make every team that depends on them wait. Self-service
removes requests from the queue entirely, which is why it has such a large
effect.

#### What goes wrong without integration

It is worth being concrete about the failure the chapter is trying to
prevent. Without integration, development designs a feature without
knowing how it will be deployed, monitored or scaled. The code is finished
and handed to operations, often close to a deadline. Operations discovers
that the feature needs a new database, a firewall change and more capacity,
none of which were planned. The release slips or goes out with
workarounds. In production, the feature fails in ways nobody anticipated,
operations is paged at night, and developers hear about it days later, if
at all. Nobody learns, so the next feature repeats the pattern.

Each of the chapter's practices cuts this chain at a different point.
Platforms make the infrastructure needs self-service. Embedded engineers
and liaisons raise them during design. Shared rituals carry production
problems back to developers. Shared boards make the operational work
visible and planned. Together they turn a late, surprising hand-off into
an early, ordinary conversation.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A friend helped me with server setup whenever I asked. When
they were busy with their own work, my requests waited days; when they
were free, minutes. The difference was not their skill, only how busy they
were. Once they wrote a script I could run myself, my requests stopped
waiting at all.

**Realistic.** A central operations team of eight serves forty development
teams. It is always busy, so environment requests take a week. Leadership's
first idea is to hire more operations engineers. The team instead
automates the three most common requests as self-service tools. Its
utilisation falls, the remaining requests are handled quickly, and the
team has time to work on the next most common request.

### Step by step

1. **List the requests** development teams send to operations, with
   counts.
2. **Measure how long they wait**, not only how long they take.
3. **Estimate operations' utilisation** honestly, including unplanned
   work.
4. **Choose the most common requests** for self-service.
5. **Choose an integration model** (embedding, liaison, rituals) for the
   rest.
6. **Measure the waits again** after each change.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to fix slow operations by pushing operations staff
to work harder or by keeping them fully utilised. The queueing rule shows
why that makes waits worse. The fix is to reduce demand on the queue
through self-service, and leave slack for the unexpected.

### In real systems

Many operations groups now see themselves as platform teams whose product
is self-service infrastructure. Others pair a platform with embedded or
liaison engineers for the teams with the most complex needs.

## Create shared services to increase developer productivity

### In one sentence

Operations can make its capabilities available as self-service platforms,
so development teams get environments, deployments and monitoring on
demand, without tickets or waits.

### The idea

The first way to integrate operations is to turn operations work into
tools. Instead of developers filing a ticket for an environment and waiting
for an operations engineer, they use a platform that creates one in
minutes. Instead of asking operations to deploy, they run a pipeline that
deploys safely. Instead of requesting monitoring, they add a few lines of
configuration.

The book describes such platforms as products. They have users, the
development teams, and they succeed only if those users choose them. That
means the operations group must think like a product team: understand its
users' needs, make the platform easy to use, document it, support it, and
improve it based on feedback. A platform that teams are forced to use, but
that is slower or harder than working around it, will be worked around.

The book also notes that internal shared services should be optional where
possible: teams can use the platform or build their own if they have good
reason. Making the platform optional forces it to be good, because it has
to compete. In practice, a good platform becomes the default because it is
the easiest path, which the industry now often calls a paved road.

Shared platforms reinforce the book's broader goals. They codify
operations knowledge, so it is not locked in people's heads. They make
environments consistent, which reduces the "works on my machine" problems
chapter 9 tackles. And they free operations engineers from repetitive
requests to work on harder problems.

#### What a good internal platform offers

A good internal platform covers the common needs of a service's life:
creating an environment, building and testing, deploying, collecting logs
and metrics, alerting, managing secrets, and scaling. Each capability is
available through a simple interface, a command, a configuration file or a
pipeline step, with safe defaults. A team should be able to go from a new
repository to a deployed, monitored service quickly, using the platform's
templates, without speaking to anyone.

<Flow caption="A new service on a self-service platform. Read left to right: each step is a tool the team uses on its own, with no ticket in the path." steps={[

  "Deploy through the pipeline",
  "Logs, metrics and alerts on by default",

#### Measuring whether a platform is working

Because a platform is a product, it needs product measures. The most
useful are about its users' outcomes: how long it takes a team to go from
a new repository to a deployed, monitored service; how long routine
requests wait now compared with before; how many teams use each capability
voluntarily; and how often teams have to ask the platform group for help
with something the platform should handle. Measures of the platform
team's own activity, such as tickets closed, are less useful, because a
good platform reduces tickets rather than closing more of them.

#### Self-service with guardrails

A common worry about self-service is safety: if developers can create
environments and deploy on their own, what stops them breaking things? The
answer is guardrails built into the platform. Templates create resources
with safe defaults, such as encryption on, logs collected and sensible
limits. Policies checked automatically reject dangerous configurations
before they are applied. Deploys run through a pipeline with tests and
automated checks rather than by hand. Costs and quotas are visible and
capped. Guardrails let the platform say yes quickly to the common, safe
request, and route only the unusual one to a person. That combination,
fast for the safe path and reviewed for the risky one, is usually safer
than a ticket queue, where every request gets a hurried manual review
regardless of risk.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I used to set up hosting for each new project by hand, which
took an evening and was different every time. I wrote a template
repository with the build, deploy and monitoring already configured. A new
project now takes ten minutes, and every project works the same way.

**Realistic.** An operations group notices that most of its tickets are
requests for test environments, DNS entries and deploy permissions. It
builds a small platform: a command that creates a test environment from a
template, automated DNS for services, and deploy permissions managed by the
pipeline. It documents the platform, runs short demos for development teams,
and gathers feedback monthly. Tickets for those requests nearly disappear,
and the group's time shifts to reliability work.

### Step by step

1. **Count the most common operations requests.**
2. **Pick the top one** and automate it as a self-service tool.
3. **Treat the tool as a product**: documentation, support, feedback.
4. **Make it the easiest path**, not merely a mandated one.
5. **Measure adoption and wait times.**
6. **Repeat** with the next most common request.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to build a platform without asking its users what
they need, then mandate it. Teams find it awkward, work around it, and the
platform team feels unappreciated. The fix is to treat development teams as
customers and earn adoption.

### In real systems

Internal developer platforms, built on infrastructure as code, container
orchestration and pipeline templates, are now common. Successful ones have
product managers, documentation and user research, like external products.

## Embed operations engineers into service teams

### In one sentence

Placing operations engineers inside development teams puts operations
knowledge where design decisions are made, and lets those teams handle most
of their operations work themselves.

### The idea

The second option is structural: an operations engineer becomes a member of
a development team. They attend the team's planning, work on its backlog,
and help design and run its service. The team becomes, in effect,
market-oriented for operations, at least while the engineer is there.

The book points to several benefits. Operations concerns are raised
during design, when they are cheap to address, rather than discovered
after release. The team's operations work, such as monitoring, capacity
planning and deployment automation, happens inside the team instead of
waiting in a central queue. Developers learn operations skills from
working beside the engineer, and the engineer learns the product, which
makes them better at both.

The book also describes a variation where the product team funds the
operations engineer, with the engineer reporting to the product side. That
aligns the engineer with the product's goals rather than with operations'
local measures, though it raises questions of career growth and connection
to other operations engineers, which communities of practice can address.

Embedding is expensive, because there are rarely enough operations
engineers for every team. The book suggests it for the most important or
most operationally demanding value streams, while other teams use
platforms or liaisons.

#### What embedded engineers actually do

An embedded operations engineer's work typically includes: reviewing
designs for operability, such as how a new feature will be monitored,
scaled and recovered; building and improving the team's pipeline and
infrastructure code; setting up telemetry and alerts; leading or
supporting on-call and incident response for the service; and teaching
developers to do more of this themselves. Over time, a good embedded
engineer makes the team more self-sufficient, and spends more time on hard
problems and less on routine ones.

#### Ending an embedding well

Embedding is often most valuable as a phase rather than a permanent
arrangement. When a team has learned to build its own pipelines, write its
own alerts and handle routine incidents, the embedded engineer's marginal
value falls, and another team may need them more. Ending an embedding well
means planning for it: agreeing the self-sufficiency goals at the start,
checking progress against them, writing down what the engineer knows that
the team does not, and moving to a liaison relationship so the team still
has a named contact. Done this way, the engineer leaves behind a more
capable team rather than a gap, and the organisation's limited operations
expertise moves to where it is needed most.

### Worked example

**Simple.** When I worked on a project with a friend who knew servers
well, they sat with me while I designed the deploy. They spotted that my
plan would cause downtime on every release, suggested a simple fix, and
showed me how to read the server logs. I learned more in that afternoon
than from weeks of reading.

**Realistic.** A company embeds an operations engineer in its payments
team, its most critical stream. In the first month, the engineer reviews
the team's plan for a new feature and points out that it will put heavy
load on a shared database at month-end; the design is changed before any
code is written. Over six months, the engineer helps the team build its
own monitoring and runbooks, and pairs with developers on on-call. The
payments team's incidents fall, and two developers can now handle most
operational work without help.

### Step by step

1. **Choose the streams** where embedding pays off most: critical,
   operationally complex, or changing fast.
2. **Make the engineer a full team member**: planning, backlog, rituals.
3. **Decide the reporting line**, and keep a community of practice for
   operations engineers.
4. **Set goals for self-sufficiency**: what should the team be able to do
   alone after six months?
5. **Review regularly** whether embedding is still the right model for
   that team.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to embed an operations engineer and treat them as
the team's personal ticket queue: the developers hand all operational work
to them, and nothing is learned. The fix is to make teaching and
self-sufficiency part of the engineer's goals, and to have developers pair
on operational work.

### In real systems

Embedded site reliability engineers are common for critical services.
Some organisations rotate them between teams every year or two, which
spreads knowledge and keeps the engineers' skills broad.

## Assign an operations liaison to each service team

### In one sentence

When there are not enough operations engineers to embed, each team gets a
named operations liaison who understands its service and represents it
within operations.

### The idea

The liaison model is lighter than embedding. An operations engineer stays
in the central group but is assigned as the contact for one or several
development teams. They are not full team members, but they know the
team's product: its architecture, its dependencies, its operational risks
and its upcoming plans.

The book lists what a liaison should understand about each team:

- the new product functionality and why it is being built;
- how it works with respect to operability, scalability and observability;
- how its progress and success will be monitored and measured;
- what the team's upcoming changes and departures from established architecture are;
- what extra infrastructure it needs and how its use will grow.

With that understanding, the liaison can bring operations knowledge into
the team's decisions, spot problems early, and represent the team's needs
inside operations, for example when platform priorities are set. The team
has a person, not a queue, to talk to.

#### What the liaison does day to day

In practice, a liaison attends the team's planning and design reviews,
reviews changes with operational impact, answers questions, and is the
first operations contact during incidents for the team's service. They
also translate in both directions: explaining operational constraints to
developers, and explaining the team's plans and needs to the rest of
operations. A liaison who is effective often spots that two teams are
about to build similar infrastructure, or that a planned change will
collide with an operations project.

#### Combining the models

The models in this chapter are not exclusive; most organisations combine
them. A sensible pattern is to use self-service platforms for the common
needs of every team, embedded engineers for the few most critical or most
operationally demanding streams, liaisons for the rest, and shared rituals
and boards everywhere. The mix should change over time. A team with an
embedded engineer that has become self-sufficient may move to a liaison,
freeing the engineer for a team that needs them more. A capability that
liaisons keep helping with is a candidate for the platform.

The choice should be driven by the value stream map. Where the map shows
long waits for operations, the first answer is usually self-service; where
it shows late operational surprises, it is earlier involvement through
embedding or a liaison; where it shows lost information, it is shared
rituals and boards.

### Worked example

**Simple.** In a study group, one person who knew deployment well agreed
to be the go-to person for three of us. They did not do our work, but they
joined our weekly planning, and when one of us planned something risky,
they said so early. Problems that used to surprise us at the last minute
stopped happening.

**Realistic.** An operations group of six serves twenty development teams.
It assigns each engineer as liaison to three or four teams. Each liaison
joins those teams' fortnightly planning and design reviews and keeps a
short note of each team's upcoming changes. When one team plans a
migration to a new database, its liaison raises capacity and backup needs
weeks in advance, instead of operations discovering the change at release.

### Step by step

1. **Assign each team a named liaison**, with a manageable number of teams
   each.
2. **Have liaisons join planning and design reviews** for their teams.
3. **Keep a short brief** per team: functionality, operability, plans,
   infrastructure needs.
4. **Bring team needs into operations planning** through the liaisons.
5. **Rotate liaisons occasionally** to spread knowledge, without losing
   continuity.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to name a liaison who never attends anything, so
the role exists only on paper. The fix is to give liaisons time and make
attending planning and design reviews part of the role.

### In real systems

Liaison models are common in organisations with central operations or
SRE groups: each product area has an assigned SRE or operations contact who
joins reviews and coordinates support.

## Integrate operations into development rituals

### In one sentence

Inviting operations engineers to development's stand-ups, retrospectives
and demos is a cheap, effective way to share knowledge, catch problems
early and build relationships.

### The idea

Development teams, especially agile ones, have regular rituals: daily
stand-ups to coordinate, retrospectives to reflect and improve, and demos to
show completed work. The book recommends bringing operations into them,
whether through embedded engineers, liaisons or invited guests.

**Stand-ups.** When operations attends the daily stand-up, it hears about
upcoming changes while there is still time to influence them, and
developers hear about operations issues that affect them. The book notes
that this is cheap and high-value: a few minutes a day replace surprises at
release time.

**Retrospectives.** When operations joins retrospectives, the team reflects
on the whole lifecycle, including deployment and production, not only
development. Problems that operations experienced, such as a deploy that
needed manual fixes or an alert that woke someone at night, become input to
the team's improvements. That feedback is exactly what the Second Way asks
for: the people who create problems hear about them.

**Demos.** When operations sees demos, it understands what is coming, and
developers can show operational improvements, such as better telemetry, as
achievements, not afterthoughts.

The book also mentions making operations' improvements visible in the
team's ritual: if operations fixed something during a sprint, it is shown
in the demo, so its value is recognised.

#### Why rituals matter more than they seem

Rituals look like small things, but they decide what information reaches
whom. A design decision made in a stand-up that operations does not attend
reaches operations at release, when it is expensive to change. A
production problem discussed in an operations meeting developers do not
attend never reaches the people who could prevent it. Changing who attends
changes the information flow, which, by Conway's Law, gradually changes the
system too.

#### A blameless tone for operations feedback

Bringing operations into retrospectives only works if the conversation
stays blameless, as chapter 4 describes. If an operations engineer's
report of a painful deploy is heard as an accusation, developers become
defensive and operations stops raising issues. The useful framing is about
the system: what made this deploy painful, and what would make the next one
easier? Teams that keep this tone find that operations feedback becomes one
of the most valuable inputs to their improvement work, because it shows
the consequences of design decisions that developers cannot see from their
side.

#### Developers in operations rituals

Integration works in both directions. Operations has its own rituals:
incident reviews, capacity planning, on-call hand-overs. Inviting
developers to these is as valuable as inviting operations to development's.
A developer who attends the review of an incident in their service learns
far more about how it fails than any report could convey, and often
suggests a fix operations would not have thought of because they know the
code. A developer who sits in on capacity planning learns what their
design choices cost. The book's broader point is that shared rituals create
shared understanding, and shared understanding is what lets two groups
make decisions that are good for the whole stream rather than for their
own part.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I started including "production" in my weekly project review:
what broke, what alerts fired, what I fixed by hand. Before, my reviews
only covered features. Adding production showed me that the same flaky
deploy step had cost me time three weeks running, and I fixed it.

**Realistic.** A team invites its operations liaison to stand-ups twice a
week and to every retrospective. In the first retrospective, the liaison
mentions that every release needs a manual cache flush, which the
developers did not know. The team adds an automated flush to the pipeline
that sprint. Over the next months, retrospectives regularly include a
production item, and the number of manual steps in releases falls.

### Step by step

1. **Invite operations** (embedded engineer, liaison or guest) to
   stand-ups, at least a few times a week.
2. **Add a production item** to every retrospective: incidents, alerts,
   manual work.
3. **Show operational improvements in demos.**
4. **Invite developers to operations reviews**, such as incident reviews,
   in return.
5. **Watch for changes** in how early operational issues are raised.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to invite operations as a silent observer, or at a
time that does not suit them, so they stop coming. The fix is to give
operations a real voice in the ritual, a slot in the retrospective, a
question in stand-up, and to find a time that works.

### In real systems

Many teams add an "operations" or "production" segment to their
retrospectives and invite on-call engineers to share what they saw.
Cross-team incident reviews with both developers and operations are also
common.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why is inviting operations to stand-ups high value for low cost?</summary>

A few minutes a day let operations hear about changes while they can still
be influenced, and let developers hear about production issues, replacing
surprises at release.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How do retrospectives change when operations joins?</summary>

They cover the whole lifecycle, including deployment and production, so
problems operations experiences become input to the team's improvements.

</details>

## Make relevant operations work visible on shared kanban boards

### In one sentence

Putting the operations work related to a team's service on the same kanban
board as its development work makes the whole picture visible, so
priorities and dependencies are managed together.

### The idea

Chapter 6 recommended a shared board for all work in the stream. This
chapter applies that specifically to operations. When operations work for
a service, such as provisioning, configuration changes, monitoring setup or
capacity work, sits in a separate operations ticketing system, development
cannot see it. The team does not know why its release is waiting, or how
much operational work its changes create.

When operations work for the service is on the team's kanban board, the
book points to several benefits. Everyone can see all the work needed to
deliver value, not just the code. Dependencies are visible: a feature
blocked by an environment change shows the block. The team can see how
much operational work its own decisions generate, which is useful feedback.
And the product owner can prioritise development and operations work
together, making trade-offs explicit rather than accidental.

The book also notes that this reveals the true lead time: a change is not
done when the code is merged; it is done when it is running in production
and delivering value. A board that shows operations work makes that whole
path visible.

<Figure caption="A team's kanban board with development and operations work together. Read left to right: operations cards flow through the same columns, and the blocked card shows a feature waiting on an operations task." art={`
 +------------+-------------+---------------+---------------+------------+
 |  Backlog   |  In dev     |  In review    |  Deploying    |  Live      |

 | [D] promo  | [D] cart    | [D] search    | [O] scale DB  | [D] login  |
 | [O] alert  | [O] new env |               |               | [O] backup |

 |            |    cart)    |               |               |            |
 +------------+-------------+---------------+---------------+------------+
   D = development card    O = operations card
`} />

#### What belongs on the board

Not every operations task needs to be on every team's board. The book
suggests the relevant work: operations work that affects or is caused by
that team's service. Typical examples: environment and infrastructure
changes for the service, monitoring and alerting work, capacity and
scaling tasks, operational fixes found in incidents, and deployment
improvements. Organisation-wide operations work, such as upgrading the
shared network, can live on the operations group's own board, with links
where it affects a team.

#### Reading the board for operational load

Once operations cards share the board, the board becomes a measuring tool.
If a team counts the share of its cards that are operations work each
month, it can see whether its service is getting easier or harder to run.
A rising share often means growing operational debt: manual steps,
fragile infrastructure, noisy alerts. A falling share after an improvement,
such as automating a deploy step, shows the improvement worked. This links
directly to chapter 6's reserved capacity: the board shows where the
reserved time should go, and whether it is paying off.

#### Setting work in progress limits across both kinds of work

Once development and operations cards share a board, they should also
share its work in progress limits. If the limit applies only to
development cards, operations work slips in around it and the team is
overloaded without the board showing it. If it applies to all cards, the
team must choose: when an urgent operations task arrives and the column is
full, something else waits, and that choice is made openly in front of the
product owner. That is exactly the trade-off the book wants visible. It
also protects flow, because chapter 2's argument applies to all work: the
more items in progress at once, the longer each takes and the more context
switching it causes. A shared limit turns "we are always busy but nothing
finishes" into a concrete conversation about which work matters most this
week, and it shows quickly when operational load is crowding out everything
else, which is the signal to invest in the platform or in reserved debt
work.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I added operations cards to my project board: "renew domain",
"rotate API key", "update hosting plan". Before, these surprised me as
emergencies; on the board, they were planned like features.

**Realistic.** A team's board shows only development stories. Its releases
keep slipping for reasons the product owner cannot see. The team adds
operations cards for its service, linked from the operations queue. The
next release's slip is visible a week ahead: a firewall change card has
not started. The product owner and the liaison reprioritise, and the
release goes out on time.

### Step by step

1. **Identify operations work** that affects or is caused by the team's
   service.
2. **Put it on the team's board**, labelled as operations work.
3. **Link blocking relationships** between operations and development
   cards.
4. **Extend the board to "live"**, so done means in production.
5. **Prioritise together** in the team's planning.
6. **Review the mix** to see how much operational work the service creates.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to duplicate work in two systems that drift apart,
so neither board is trusted. The fix is one source of truth, or tooling
that links them automatically.

### In real systems

Teams often use one tracker with labels or swimlanes for operations work,
or integrations that mirror relevant tickets between systems. Extending the
board to a "live in production" column is a common, simple change.

</details>

<summary>2. Why extend the board to a "live" column?</summary>

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 8, How to Get Great Outcomes by Integrating Operations into the Daily Work of Development">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the lesson is that development and operations
should not work in separate worlds. Developers need operations help
quickly, and they need to see what happens to their code in production.
Even without changing the org chart, small things help: inviting
operations to stand-ups, putting operations work on the team's board, and
asking operations what went wrong in the last release.

For my own work, it means treating the running of my projects as part of
the work, not as something that happens afterwards: operations cards on
my board, production in my weekly review.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I know the main integration models and when
each fits. Self-service platforms remove the most common requests from the
queue. Embedded engineers bring operations into design for critical
streams. Liaisons give every team a named operations contact. Shared
rituals and boards make information flow both ways. I can explain, with
the busy-over-idle rule, why an overloaded operations team creates long
waits and why hiring alone does not fix it as well as removing demand.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I design a mix of models from the value stream map
and adjust it over time. I treat the platform as a product with users and
outcome measures, earn its adoption rather than mandating it, and grow it
from the requests liaisons and embedded engineers keep seeing. I give
embedded engineers self-sufficiency goals so teams learn rather than
outsource. I keep retrospectives blameless so operations feedback keeps
flowing. And I extend boards to "live", so done means running in
production.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases include: a regulated environment where developers may not have
production access, so self-service must work through automated, audited
pipelines rather than direct access; a single operations engineer serving
many teams, where the only realistic models are platform work and shared
rituals; teams in different time zones from operations, which need
asynchronous rituals such as written stand-up notes; and services run by an
outsourced provider, where a liaison on the provider side and shared
boards become the main integration tools.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases include: an embedded engineer who drifts into being a
developer and stops doing operations work, leaving the team's operational
health unattended; a platform that becomes so flexible it is as complex as
the infrastructure it hides; liaisons who become a new bottleneck because
every team routes everything through them; and shared boards so crowded
with operations noise that teams stop reading them, which needs a clear
rule for what counts as relevant.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

At production level, the organisation runs a self-service platform with
product management, documentation and outcome measures; embeds operations
or SRE engineers in its most critical streams; assigns liaisons to the
rest; and has operations present in planning, stand-ups, demos and
retrospectives. Every team's board includes its service's operations
work and ends at "live". The platform team tracks time from new repository
to monitored deploy and wait times for routine requests, and the
operations group's utilisation is kept below the point where queues
explode.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is that operations is part of delivering value, not a phase
after it. I would rather operations engineers spend their time making
teams self-sufficient than answering the same request repeatedly. I value
slack in shared teams, because it is what keeps waits short. And I see
information flow as something I can design: who attends which meeting and
which board shows which work decides what each group knows.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design terms, the platform is part of the architecture. Its
interfaces, templates, pipeline steps and configuration formats, shape how
every service is built and run. Good platform design gives services safe
defaults for deployment, telemetry and recovery, so operability is built in
rather than added. Services designed with operations input from the start
include health checks, graceful degradation and clear runbooks.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategy is to integrate operations at every level available, starting
with the cheapest. Begin with shared rituals and boards, which cost almost
nothing. Assign liaisons to every team. Turn the most common requests into
self-service, and keep growing the platform from what liaisons see.
Embed engineers in the streams where operational risk is highest. Review
the mix every few months against the value stream map's waits and
surprises.

Done when every team has fast self-service for common operations needs, a
named operations contact, operations present in its rituals, and its
service's operations work visible on its board through to "live".

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>Does this chapter mean developers should do all operations work?</summary>

No. It means operations knowledge and help should be available inside
development's daily work. Developers take on more operations work over
time, with platforms and operations engineers supporting them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should embedded operations engineers report to the product team or to operations?</summary>

The book describes both. Reporting to the product aligns the engineer with
its goals; reporting to operations helps career growth and consistency. A
community of practice can give the benefits of both.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many teams can one liaison support?</summary>

The book does not give a number. In practice it depends on how complex the
services are and how much time liaison work takes; a few teams each is a
common starting point, adjusted by experience.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if developers do not want operations at their stand-ups?</summary>

Start small: twice a week, with a clear purpose, such as hearing about
upcoming changes. Once the team sees problems caught early, the invitation
usually becomes permanent.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is a self-service platform worth it for a small organisation?</summary>

A small version is: templates, a standard pipeline and scripts for common
tasks. The principle, replacing repeated requests with tools, applies at
any size.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why not just hire more operations engineers?</summary>

It helps, but the queueing rule shows that reducing demand on the queue
through self-service often helps more, and hiring alone keeps knowledge in
people's heads rather than in tools.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does this chapter close Part II?</summary>

Part II moved from choosing a value stream, to mapping it, to designing
teams and architecture, to integrating operations. Together they set up the
organisation for Part III's technical practices, starting with the
deployment pipeline.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I apply this when I work alone?</summary>

I play both roles, so integration means treating operations as part of my
work: templates for setup, operations cards on my board, and production
issues in my weekly review.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between this chapter and site reliability engineering?</summary>

Site reliability engineering is one concrete way to do much of what this
chapter describes: engineers with operations responsibilities who write
software to automate operations, often embedded with or assigned to
product teams, and who share on-call with developers. The chapter's models
are broader and work without adopting the whole SRE approach.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I know which integration model to start with?</summary>

Look at the value stream map. Long waits for operations requests suggest
self-service first. Late surprises at release suggest earlier involvement
through a liaison or embedding. Information that never crosses between
groups suggests shared rituals and boards, which are also the cheapest
place to start.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- The goal is market-oriented outcomes, fast operations help and shared operational awareness, even with a central operations group.
- Overloaded shared teams create long waits; by the busy-over-idle rule, waits climb steeply near full utilisation.
- Self-service platforms, treated as products with users and outcome measures, remove routine requests from the queue.
- Embedded operations engineers bring operations into design for the most critical streams; give them self-sufficiency goals.
- Operations liaisons give every team a named contact who knows its service and represents it within operations.
- Inviting operations to stand-ups, retrospectives and demos is cheap and changes what information reaches whom.
- Put the relevant operations work on the team's kanban board, and extend the board to "live".
- Combine the models, guided by the value stream map, and adjust the mix over time.

## Glossary

- **Self-service platform**: tools that let development teams get environments, deploys, monitoring and other operations capabilities on demand.
- **Internal platform as a product**: treating an internal platform as something with users, needs, documentation and outcome measures.
- **Paved road**: the supported, easiest path for building and running services, provided by a platform.
- **Embedded operations engineer**: an operations engineer who is a full member of a development team.
- **Operations liaison**: a named operations engineer assigned to one or more development teams as their contact and advocate.
- **Community of practice**: a group of people with the same speciality across different teams, who share knowledge and standards.
- **Stand-up**: a short daily coordination meeting.
- **Retrospective**: a regular team meeting to reflect on how work went and choose improvements.
- **Demo**: a meeting where completed work is shown.
- **Utilisation**: the share of time a person or team is busy.
- **Busy-over-idle rule**: the rule of thumb that relative waiting time grows as busy share divided by idle share.
- **Kanban board**: a visual board of work items moving through columns of a workflow.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up.** List the last ten times I needed operations-style help, from
someone else or from my own setup work. For each, note how long I waited.

**Build.** Pick the most common item from the list and turn it into a
self-service tool for myself: a script, a template or a pipeline step.
Measure how long the task takes before and after.

**Stretch.** For an imaginary organisation with fifteen development teams
and five operations engineers, design an integration plan: which teams get
embedded engineers, how liaisons are assigned, which requests the platform
covers first, and which rituals operations joins. Use the busy-over-idle
rule to argue for slack.
