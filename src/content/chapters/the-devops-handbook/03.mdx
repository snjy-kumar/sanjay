The First Way makes work flow forward. The Second Way makes information
flow back: from production, from customers and from every later step to
the people who did the work, fast enough to act on. This chapter explains
why complex systems cannot be made safe by planning alone, and gives the
principles that make them safe in practice: see problems as they happen,
swarm them, push quality toward the source, and design for the people
downstream.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- explain what makes a system complex, and why that means failures cannot all be predicted;
- design feedback loops that surface a problem within minutes of it being created, and say why the delay matters;
- explain the andon cord and apply it to a build: a red main branch stops new merges;
- describe swarming and why fixing a problem while it is fresh builds knowledge;
- move a quality check from a late gate to the source of the work;
- design work, including code, for the people and systems downstream of it.

## Working safely within complex systems

### In one sentence

In a complex system no one person can see the whole thing or predict
every failure, so safety has to come from detecting and correcting problems
quickly rather than from designing them all away in advance.

### The idea

A **complicated** system, like a mechanical watch, has many parts, but an
expert can understand all of them and predict how they interact. A
**complex** system, like a large software service running for millions of
people, has parts whose interactions cannot be fully predicted, even by
experts. The same action can have different effects on different days,
because the state of the system is never quite the same.

Modern software is complex in this sense. A change to a cache setting
interacts with traffic patterns, a dependency's behaviour, a database's
query planner and a load balancer's timeouts. Nobody can test every
combination. Failures often come from several individually harmless
conditions lining up, not from one obvious bug.

The book draws on the work of Steven Spear, who studied organisations that
manage complex, dangerous work safely, such as Toyota's plants and some
hospitals and naval programmes. What they share is not perfect planning.
They share four capabilities, which the book adopts:

1. Work is designed so that problems are **seen as they occur**.
2. Problems are **swarmed and solved** right away, building new knowledge.
3. That new knowledge is **spread** across the organisation.
4. Leaders **develop others** who can keep doing this.

The first two are this chapter's subject. The third and fourth return in
chapter 4 and Part V.

The shift in thinking is from "prevent all failure" to "detect and recover
fast, and learn from each failure". Prevention still matters, but in a
complex system it cannot be complete, so the quality of the feedback loop
decides how safe the system is.

#### The four capabilities in a software team

It helps to translate Spear's four capabilities into what they look like on
an ordinary engineering team, because on paper they sound abstract.

**Seeing problems as they occur** is the team's sensing layer. Tests run on
every push. Production telemetry shows error rates and latency per
endpoint. A deploy marker appears on every graph, so a change in behaviour
can be lined up with the change that caused it. The question this
capability answers is "would we know within minutes if this broke?".

**Swarming and solving** is the team's response layer. A red build stops
merges. An alert pages the owning team, not a central desk. The person on
call has the access and the authority to roll back without asking. The
question it answers is "once we know, how fast do the right people act?".

**Spreading new knowledge** is the team's memory. Each fix comes with a test
or a check. Each incident ends with a short written review that other teams
can read. A pattern found by one team becomes a shared library or a
pipeline step for all of them. The question it answers is "will the next
team hit the same problem?".

**Developing leaders who do this** is the team's continuity. Senior
engineers coach others to run incidents, write reviews and design checks.
Managers ask "what did we learn?" before "who did it?". The question it
answers is "will this still happen when the current people move on?".

The four are layered. A team with good sensing and poor response knows
about problems and does nothing quickly. A team with good response and no
memory fixes the same problem every month. Diagnosing which layer is
weakest tells me which part of the book to read next: Part IV for sensing
and response, Part V for memory and leadership.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My blog builds from static files. It is complicated, not very
complex: I can understand every part. A bug almost always has one cause.
Even so, a dependency update once changed how a date was formatted, which
broke a page I never looked at. Nothing in my design predicted it; a check
that built every page and compared titles would have caught it.

**Realistic.** An online service has a cache in front of a database. On an
ordinary Tuesday, a deploy changes a cache key format, which empties the
cache. That is harmless at low traffic. But it happens at the same time as
a marketing email that triples traffic, and the database cannot handle the
uncached load. Neither the deploy nor the email was wrong. The failure came
from the combination. The team recovers because their telemetry showed
cache hit rate dropping within a minute, and the deploy was small enough to
roll back in two.

### Step by step

How the two halves of safety work together in a complex system, walked
through on the cache incident:

1. **Prevention.** Code review checks the cache key change. It looks fine,
   because on its own it is.
2. **Detection.** Telemetry shows the cache hit rate falling and database
   latency rising within a minute.
3. **Response.** The on-call developer, who made the change, sees the
   alert and the recent deploy on the same dashboard.
4. **Recovery.** A one-command rollback restores the old key format; the
   cache warms; latency recovers.
5. **Learning.** The postmortem finds that cache-key changes can empty the
   cache, and adds a rule: change keys with a gradual migration, and check
   expected traffic before deploys.
6. **Outcome.** The prevention step is better next time, but the reason the
   incident was small was steps 2 to 4.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to respond to every failure in a complex system with
more up-front approval: another review, a longer test cycle, a sign-off.
Those slow feedback down and make changes bigger, which makes complex
failures harder to diagnose. The fix is to invest in detection and
recovery, through telemetry, small changes and fast rollback, as much as in
prevention.

### In real systems

This view underlies modern site reliability practice. Service-level
objectives, alerting on symptoms customers feel, fast rollback and
blameless postmortems all assume that failures will happen and focus on
finding and fixing them quickly. Chaos engineering, deliberately injecting
failure, is the same idea taken further: if the system is too complex to
predict, run experiments to find out.

## See problems as they occur

### In one sentence

Build feedback into every step of the work so that a problem is noticed by
the person who caused it within minutes, not weeks.

### The idea

Every problem has a moment it is created and a moment it is noticed. The
gap between the two is the **feedback delay**, and it decides almost
everything about the cost of the problem. Noticed in minutes, it is fixed
by the person who just made it, who still remembers what they were doing.
Noticed in months, it is fixed by someone else, who has to reconstruct the
context, and meanwhile more work has been built on top of it.

In a traditional release process, feedback is slow at every step.
Developers find out their code does not integrate weeks later at a merge.
Testers find defects weeks after the code was written. Operations finds
out about performance problems in production. Customers find out about
bugs before the team does.

The Second Way aims for fast feedback at every step: automated tests on
every commit, a build that runs in minutes, telemetry from production that
shows the effect of a change immediately, and alerts that reach the people
who can act. Every later practice in Part IV is a way of shortening a
feedback loop.

Good feedback has three properties. It is **fast**, arriving while the
context is fresh. It is **reliable**, so people trust it rather than
learning to ignore it. And it is **routed** to the right person, the one
who made the change and can fix it.

### Worked example

**Simple.** Here is how much work gets built on a bug before anyone finds
out, for a team that commits about six times a working day:

```js
const commitsPerDay = 6;
for (const [label, hours] of [["pre-commit check", 0.1], ["CI on push", 0.5], ["nightly build", 12], ["weekly QA", 60], ["quarterly release", 480]]) {
  const workingDays = hours / 8;
  const onTop = Math.round(commitsPerDay * workingDays);
  console.log(`${label.padEnd(18)} feedback after ~${hours} h, commits built on top: ${onTop}`);
}
```

Output (the hours are working hours):

```text
pre-commit check   feedback after ~0.1 h, commits built on top: 0
CI on push         feedback after ~0.5 h, commits built on top: 0
nightly build      feedback after ~12 h, commits built on top: 9
weekly QA          feedback after ~60 h, commits built on top: 45
quarterly release  feedback after ~480 h, commits built on top: 360
```

With feedback on every push, the bug is found before anything else is
built on it. With a quarterly release, hundreds of commits sit on top of
it, any of which might depend on the bug or hide it.

**Realistic.** A team adds a single dashboard that shows, for each deploy,
error rate and latency for the ten minutes before and after. Before, a
developer heard about a slow endpoint when a customer complained days
later. After, they see a latency jump on the dashboard as their deploy
completes, and roll back before most customers notice.

### Step by step

Shortening a feedback loop, for any kind of problem:

1. **Pick a kind of problem** that hurt recently: a broken build, a slow
   page, a wrong calculation.
2. **Find when it was created** and **when it was noticed**. The difference
   is the current feedback delay.
3. **Ask what signal existed earlier.** Could a test, a type check, a
   metric or a log line have shown it sooner?
4. **Add that signal at the earliest step** where it is cheap and reliable.
5. **Route it to the person who made the change**, not to a shared inbox.
6. **Measure the new delay** next time the problem happens. If it did not
   shrink, the signal is not reaching anyone.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is noisy feedback: flaky tests that fail at random,
alerts that fire for things that do not matter, dashboards nobody reads.
People learn to ignore them, so the feedback loop exists on paper and is
dead in practice. The fix is to treat every false alarm as a defect in the
feedback system and fix or remove it, so that a red signal always means
something.

The second mistake is fast feedback routed to the wrong place, such as
alerts that go to an operations team who did not make the change. They
cannot fix it quickly, and the developer who could never hears about it.

### In real systems

Fast feedback shows up as pre-commit hooks, CI that runs on every push in
a few minutes, required checks before merge, canary deploys that compare a
new version's error rate with the old one, and alerts on service-level
objectives that page the owning team. Teams often track "time to green",
how long a commit waits for its checks, as a measure of the feedback loop
itself.

## Swarm and solve problems to build new knowledge

### In one sentence

When a problem appears, stop and bring people to it right away, so it is
fixed while the evidence is fresh and the lesson is learned, instead of
working around it.

### The idea

The famous example comes from Toyota's plants: the **andon cord**. Any
worker who sees a problem pulls a cord. The team leader comes at once. If
the problem cannot be solved within the cycle time, the line stops until it
is. That sounds expensive. It is less expensive than the alternative, which
is to let defective work move down the line, where it becomes harder to fix
and its cause becomes harder to find.

**Swarming** means that when a problem shows up, people with the relevant
knowledge gather and work on it together, immediately. The point is not
only to fix the problem. It is to understand it while the evidence is
fresh: the logs are still there, the person who made the change remembers
what they did, and the conditions can still be reproduced.

The alternative, working around the problem, has a hidden cost. The
workaround becomes part of the process. Nobody learns why it happened. The
same problem comes back, each time looking like a new one. Over time the
system accumulates workarounds and nobody can say why they exist.

In software, the andon cord is a rule that a broken build or a failing
main branch is everyone's first priority. Nobody merges on top of red.
People who can help stop what they are doing and help. The build is
restored, the cause understood, and a test added so it cannot recur
silently.

<Figure caption="The andon cord as a merge gate. Follow both paths from the top: when main is red, the only allowed action is to fix it; when it is green, normal work continues." art={`

               +---------------------+

       +----------------+   +--------------------------+
       | merge changes  |   | stop merging             |

       +----------------+   | add a test for the cause |
                            +--------------------------+

### Worked example

**Simple.** A merge gate is a tiny piece of logic that enforces the andon
cord in code:

```js
function canMerge(mainStatus, prStatus) {
  if (mainStatus !== "green") return { ok: false, why: "main is red: fix it before merging anything" };
  if (prStatus !== "green") return { ok: false, why: "this change's checks are failing" };
  return { ok: true, why: "main and change are green" };
}
console.log(canMerge("green", "green"));
console.log(canMerge("red", "green"));
console.log(canMerge("green", "red"));
```

Output:

```text
{ ok: true, why: 'main and change are green' }
{ ok: false, why: 'main is red: fix it before merging anything' }
{ ok: false, why: "this change's checks are failing" }
```

The second line is the important one: even a perfectly good change cannot
merge while main is red. Everyone's fastest path back to merging is to help
fix main.

**Realistic.** Main breaks at 10:30 because a test depends on the current
date and today is the first of the month. Without swarming, three
developers each notice, each assume someone else is on it, and one merges
anyway, adding a second failure on top. With swarming, the first person to
notice posts in the team channel, two people stop what they are doing, they
find the date dependency in fifteen minutes, fix it, and add a test that
fixes the clock. Main is green by 11:00 and the date bug is gone for good.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to swarm only on big incidents and to let small
problems slide: a flaky test that is re-run until it passes, a warning that
everyone ignores. Small problems are exactly where the learning is
cheapest. The fix is to treat small failures with the same reflex: stop,
understand, fix the cause, add a check.

The second mistake is to swarm without ever stopping the line, so people
keep merging while some of them try to fix main. Every new merge makes the
fix harder. The gate is what makes the swarm work.

### In real systems

Teams implement this with branch protection that requires checks to pass,
merge queues that test changes against the latest main before merging,
chat alerts when main goes red, and a habit that the person who broke it,
or whoever is free, takes it first. Many teams also have a rule that a
flaky test is quarantined and fixed within a day, so the signal stays
trustworthy.

## Keep pushing quality closer to the source

### In one sentence

Quality is cheapest and most reliable when it is built in by the people
doing the work, at the moment they do it, rather than inspected in later
by someone far away.

### The idea

Many organisations try to get quality through inspection and approval. A
change is written, then reviewed by a separate test team, then approved by
a change board, then checked by security, then accepted by operations. Each
gate is far from the work, in time and in knowledge. The people at the gate
did not write the change, often do not understand it in depth, and see it
weeks after it was made.

The book argues this has three problems. First, it is slow: each gate adds
a queue. Second, it is weak: a person far from the work can only check what
is easy to see, such as whether the form is filled in. Third, it moves
responsibility away from the people doing the work. A developer who knows
a test team will check their work has less reason to check it themselves.

Pushing quality to the source means that the people doing the work are
responsible for its quality, and have the tools to check it as they go.
Developers write automated tests with their code. Security checks run in
the pipeline on every change, not in a review at the end. Operational
requirements, such as logging, metrics and runbooks, are part of the
definition of done. Specialists stop being gatekeepers and become coaches
and tool builders, making it easy for everyone to do the right thing.

This does not mean removing review. It means moving review as close to the
work as possible: peer review by someone who understands the code, done
within hours, on a small change. Chapter 18 develops this in detail.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I used to check my notes for broken links once a month, by
running a link checker over the whole site. Every month it found several
broken links, often in notes I barely remembered writing. I moved the
check into the build, so a broken link fails the build the moment I add
it. Now I fix it in seconds, while I still know what I meant to link to.

**Realistic.** A team's releases go through a manual QA phase of two weeks.
QA finds many defects, but each is found weeks after it was written, and
developers have moved on. The team changes approach: developers write
automated tests for every change, QA engineers pair with developers to
design those tests and build shared test tooling, and the manual phase
shrinks to exploratory testing of new features. Defects are found earlier,
the release cycle shortens, and QA's knowledge spreads into every change
rather than being applied at the end.

### Step by step

Moving one check toward the source:

1. **Pick a check that happens late**, such as manual regression testing,
   a security review or an operations sign-off.
2. **List what it actually catches.** Look at the last few months of
   findings.
3. **For each kind of finding, ask** whether it could be detected
   automatically, or by the person writing the change, at the time of
   writing.
4. **Build that check into the place the work is done**: a test, a linter
   rule, a pipeline step, a template.
5. **Keep the late check for what cannot be automated**, and make it
   smaller and faster.
6. **Have the specialist who owned the late check own the new tool**, so
   their knowledge is encoded rather than lost.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to remove the late gate without building the early
checks, in the name of speed. Quality then drops, and the organisation
concludes that the gate was necessary. The fix is to build the checks at
the source first, prove they catch what the gate caught, and only then
shrink the gate.

The second mistake is to make developers responsible for quality without
giving them tools, time or knowledge. "You own testing now" with no test
framework and no time is a recipe for skipped tests. Specialists turning
into coaches is what makes the shift work.

### In real systems

Quality at the source shows up as test-driven development, static analysis
and dependency scanning in CI, infrastructure policies checked as code,
security teams publishing shared libraries and pipeline steps, and
operations teams providing logging and metrics libraries that are on by
default. The pattern is always the same: encode the specialist's judgment
where the work happens.

## Enable optimising for downstream work

### In one sentence

Design every piece of work for the people and steps that come after it,
especially operations, treating their needs as requirements as real as the
customer's.

### The idea

In Lean manufacturing, the **customer** of a step is the next step, not
just the end buyer. A part that the next station can fit easily is good
quality, even if a buyer never sees it. Lean designers apply this
deliberately: they design products not only for the end user but for
manufacturing, so that a part can be assembled quickly and correctly. This
is sometimes called design for manufacturing.

The software equivalent is to design for operations. A feature is not done
when it works on a developer's machine. It is done when it can be deployed
safely, observed in production, diagnosed when it misbehaves, scaled when
traffic grows, and supported by whoever is on call. Those needs are
sometimes called non-functional requirements, and they are easy to skip,
because the person writing the feature is rarely the one who will be woken
up when it fails.

The book's principle is to treat these downstream needs as first-class
requirements, prioritised alongside customer features. Operations, support
and security are customers of development too. In practice that means
writing code that logs what it does, exposes useful metrics, fails with a
clear error, handles its own configuration, and can be rolled back.

Doing this well also improves the First Way, because work that arrives
ready for the next step has higher percent complete and accurate, and
flows faster.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My command-line tool used to print "Error" and exit with code
0 when it failed. A script that called it could not tell failure from
success. I changed it to print what failed and why to standard error, and
exit with a non-zero code. Nothing changed for a person running it by hand,
but every script downstream became reliable.

**Realistic.** A team adds a new payment feature. The first version works,
passes tests and ships. The first night it fails, the on-call engineer
finds no logs mentioning the payment provider, no metric for failed
payments, and no way to switch the feature off. The team adds structured
logs with a request identifier, a metric for payment outcomes by provider,
a feature flag to turn it off, and a short runbook. Those four items take a
day. The next failure is diagnosed in ten minutes and turned off in one.

### Step by step

Turning downstream needs into requirements for a feature:

1. **List the downstream customers** of the feature: reviewer, pipeline,
   operations, security, support.
2. **For each, ask what they need** to do their job when this feature
   misbehaves at three in the morning.
3. **Write those needs as acceptance criteria**, next to the functional
   ones: "payment failures are logged with provider and request id".
4. **Add the operational checks to the definition of done**: logs, metrics,
   a way to turn it off, a runbook entry.
5. **Ask someone from operations to review** the operational criteria, as
   the customer of those requirements.
6. **After the first incident, compare** what they needed with what they
   had, and update the criteria for next time.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to leave operational needs for "later", after the
feature ships. Later rarely comes, because there is always another
feature. The fix is to make them part of the definition of done, so a
feature without logs and metrics is not done.

The second mistake is to guess what operations needs without asking them.
Developers add verbose logs nobody can search, or metrics nobody alerts on.
The fix is to treat the downstream team as a customer and ask.

### In real systems

Many teams have a production-readiness checklist covering logging, metrics,
alerts, dashboards, runbooks, rollback and capacity. Some, following the
practice the book describes in chapter 16, require new services to pass a
launch review against that checklist before operations will support them,
or have the developers carry the pager until they do.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 3, The Second Way: The Principles of Feedback">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest version of the Second Way is: find out quickly when something
is wrong, and fix it before doing more work on top of it. The quicker you
find out, the cheaper the fix.

A worked example for a beginner: I add a script, `npm test`, and run it
before every commit. One day it fails because I renamed a function and
forgot one caller. I fix it in a minute. Without the script, I would have
found out days later, when the page that called that function broke, and
spent an hour working out which of my twenty commits since then caused it.

The beginner mistake is to run checks occasionally, when there is time.
Feedback only works if it runs every time. The fix is to make the check
automatic, through a pre-commit hook or CI, so it cannot be skipped.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, the principles combine into a working loop for
a team. Every push runs the tests in CI within a few minutes. A red main
branch blocks merges. The person who broke it, or whoever is free, fixes it
within the hour. Each fix comes with a test that would have caught it.

A worked example in a team: CI turns red on main after a merge. The merge
gate stops new merges. A message goes to the team channel naming the
commit. The author looks first; a colleague joins. They find that a
database migration was not applied in the test environment. They fix the
pipeline so migrations always run before tests, which fixes this failure
and every future one like it.

The intermediate mistake is to fix the immediate failure without fixing
the system that let it through. The test passes again, but the pipeline
gap is still there. The fix is to ask, every time, "what would have caught
this earlier?" and build that.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath the chapter is a claim about complex systems: you cannot reason
your way to safety in advance, because behaviour emerges from interactions
you cannot fully model. That makes feedback the primary safety mechanism,
and its speed and reliability the main design variables.

There is a useful way to see this as control. Any system steered by
feedback, from a thermostat to a team, can only correct errors as fast as
its feedback arrives. If the loop is slow, the system overshoots: it keeps
doing the wrong thing until the signal arrives, then corrects sharply. A
team with monthly feedback behaves the same way, building the wrong thing
for a month and then scrambling.

Spear's four capabilities give a precise structure. Seeing problems and
swarming them are the fast inner loop. Spreading knowledge and developing
people are the slower outer loop, which improves the inner one. A team can
have the first without the second, fixing problems fast but never getting
better at avoiding them.

The advanced mistake is to optimise feedback speed and neglect its
reliability. A very fast, very noisy signal is worse than a slightly
slower clean one, because people stop believing it. The fix is to track
false-alarm rates on tests and alerts as carefully as their speed.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

**A test suite that always passes.** It gives no feedback at all. A test
that has never failed may not be testing anything. Break the code on
purpose and check that the test catches it.

**An alert that never fires.** Either the system is very healthy or the
alert is broken. Test alerts by triggering their condition in a safe
environment.

**A problem with no owner.** Main is red but the failing commit came from
an automated dependency update, not a person. The andon rule still applies;
the team needs a rule for who swarms when there is no obvious author.

**A one-person team.** There is nobody to swarm with. The equivalent is to
stop my own work and fix the failure first, and to write it in my bug log,
which is my record of what I learned.

**A failure that cannot be reproduced.** The swarm cannot find the cause.
The right move is to add telemetry so the next occurrence leaves evidence,
not to close it as "could not reproduce".

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

**A flaky test plus a merge gate.** The test fails at random, so the gate
blocks good merges and people learn to re-run until it passes. Now the gate
is noise. The rule that handles both: a flaky test is quarantined at once
and fixed within a day, so the gate stays meaningful.

**Fast feedback plus the wrong recipient.** Alerts fire within a minute,
but to a central operations inbox. The developer who could fix it in five
minutes hears about it the next day. Fast signal, slow loop. Route alerts
to the owning team.

**Quality at the source plus no time.** Developers are told to write tests
but the sprint plan has no room for them. The tests get skipped under
deadline pressure, and the old late gate is gone. Both changes must happen
together: remove the late gate only when the early checks are funded.

**Designing for downstream plus a deadline.** Operational requirements are
the first thing dropped when a release is late. The feature ships without
logs, and the first incident takes hours. Making operational criteria part
of done, enforced by review, is what protects them under pressure.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, feedback means a layered system of signals. Type checks and
unit tests in seconds, the full suite in CI in minutes, canary analysis
during rollout, service-level objective alerts after rollout, and customer
reports as the last resort. Each layer is measured: how long it takes, how
often it is wrong, and what it misses.

Teams track a few numbers about the feedback system itself: time from push
to green, the share of CI runs that fail for reasons unrelated to the
change (flakiness), time from an alert to someone acknowledging it, and the
share of incidents first reported by customers rather than by telemetry.
The last one is a blunt measure of how good feedback is.

What breaks at scale: CI gets slow as the test suite grows, so feedback
slows. Alert counts grow with services until on-call engineers drown. The
answers are parallel tests, test selection based on what changed, and
alerting on user-facing symptoms rather than on every internal metric.

The production mistake is to add alerts for everything after each
incident, until nobody can tell important ones from noise. The fix is to
alert on symptoms users feel and investigate causes with dashboards.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset of the Second Way is that problems are information, and the
sooner you get them the more they are worth. A failing test is not an
annoyance; it is the system telling you something while it is cheap to
hear.

It also changes how you think about responsibility. The person doing the
work owns its quality, and owes the people downstream work they can use.
That applies outside software: a doctor writing a clear discharge note, a
writer delivering a draft an editor can work with.

And it changes how you respond to failure: stop, gather, understand, fix
the cause, encode the lesson. Not blame, not workaround.

The mindset mistake is to treat stopping the line as a failure of
discipline. It is the discipline. The fix is to praise people for pulling
the cord, and to treat silence about a problem as the real failure.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

The Second Way puts requirements on architecture. A system can only give
fast feedback if its parts can be tested in isolation, deployed in small
pieces and observed individually. A monolith whose tests take two hours,
whose only environment is shared, and whose logs are one undifferentiated
stream gives slow, noisy feedback, whatever the team's intentions.

A concrete example: a team splits a payment flow into a service with its
own tests, its own deploy pipeline and its own metrics for each payment
provider. A bug in one provider's integration now shows up as a red test in
that service within minutes, and in production as one provider's failure
rate rising on one dashboard. Before, it showed up as "payments seem slow
today".

When I design a component, I ask feedback questions: how will I know it is
broken? How fast? Who will be told? Can it be turned off without a deploy?
If I cannot answer, the design is incomplete.

The design mistake is to treat observability as something added later by
operations. It has to be designed in, because the most useful signals come
from inside the code.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

How I learn this chapter: I build the feedback loop in my own repository.
One command runs type checks, lint and tests in under a minute. A
pre-commit hook runs it. CI runs the same command on every push. A red main
branch means I fix it before anything else.

Then I measure my own feedback delay. For each bug in my bug log, I record
when it was created and when I noticed it. I look for a pattern: which
kinds of bugs took longest to surface, and what signal would have caught
them earlier. I add one check for the worst kind.

Finally I review my code for downstream needs: does every script exit with
a meaningful code, does every error say what to do next, could I diagnose a
failure from the logs alone?

Done when every commit runs one check under a minute, CI runs the same
check, my bug log records detection delay for every entry, and I have added
one check because of it.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**Does "stop the line" really mean stopping all work?** It means stopping
work that would be built on the problem, such as merging into a red main
branch. People can keep working locally, but the first priority of anyone
who can help is the failure.

**Isn't it wasteful to have several people swarm one problem?** Less than
it looks. The swarm usually solves it much faster than one person, and the
alternative, letting the problem persist and grow, costs more.

**If quality moves to developers, do we still need testers?** Yes, but in
a different role: designing test strategy, building tooling, coaching, and
doing exploratory testing that automation cannot.

**What is the difference between feedback and monitoring?** Monitoring is
one source of feedback, from production. Feedback includes every signal
that reaches the person who did the work: tests, reviews, builds, canary
results and customer reports.

**How do I start designing for operations if I have never been on call?**
Ask the people who are. Or take a turn on call. Nothing teaches downstream
needs faster than being woken up by your own code.

**Isn't a complex system just a badly designed one?** No. Any large system
that interacts with real users, traffic and dependencies becomes complex.
Good design reduces complexity, but cannot remove it.

**How fast is fast enough for feedback?** Fast enough that the person
who made the change is still thinking about it. For checks on a commit that
means minutes, ideally under ten. For production signals after a deploy, it
means the same session the deploy happened in. Anything that arrives the
next day reaches someone who has moved on.

**What if the problem is in another team's system?** Swarming still
applies, but across the boundary: raise it with the owning team at once,
with the evidence, and offer to help. Working around another team's bug
silently is the same mistake as working around your own, with the added
cost that the owner never learns about it.

**Can feedback be too fast?** Feedback can be too noisy, not too fast. A
signal that fires on every small fluctuation trains people to ignore it.
Fast and quiet is the goal: alert only when something needs a human, and
let dashboards carry the rest.

## Key takeaways

- Complex systems cannot be made safe by prediction alone, so safety comes from fast detection, response and learning.
- The feedback delay, from a problem's creation to its discovery, drives the cost of fixing it.
- Good feedback is fast, reliable and routed to the person who can act.
- The andon cord in software is a red main branch that stops merges until it is fixed.
- Swarming fixes problems while evidence is fresh and turns each one into knowledge.
- Quality at the source beats inspection far from the work; specialists become coaches and tool builders.
- Operations, support and security are customers of development; their needs are requirements.
- Noisy feedback is as bad as no feedback, because people learn to ignore it.

## Glossary

- **Complex system**: a system whose behaviour emerges from interactions no one can fully predict.
- **Complicated system**: a system with many parts that an expert can fully understand.
- **Feedback delay**: the time between a problem being created and being noticed.
- **Feedback loop**: the path by which information about the effects of work returns to the people doing it.
- **Andon cord**: a signal anyone can use to stop the line when they see a problem.
- **Swarming**: gathering people with relevant knowledge on a problem immediately to solve it.
- **Merge gate**: a rule or tool that blocks merges when checks fail or main is red.
- **Flaky test**: a test that fails at random, not because of the change under test.
- **Quality at the source**: building quality checks into the work, done by the people doing it.
- **Inspection**: checking work after it is done, usually by someone else.
- **Downstream work centre**: any later step that receives the output of a step.
- **Design for operations**: building features so they can be deployed, observed and supported.
- **Non-functional requirements**: needs such as logging, performance, security and operability.
- **Production-readiness checklist**: a list of operational criteria a service meets before launch.
