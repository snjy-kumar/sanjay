Part III made changes flow quickly into production. Part IV is about the
other direction: feedback from production back to the people who build
it. The first requirement for feedback is being able to see what is
happening. This chapter explains **telemetry**: the logs, events and
metrics that systems emit so people can understand their behaviour. It
covers building a central place where telemetry is collected, writing logs
that actually help, using telemetry to solve problems with evidence rather
than guesses, making new metrics part of everyday work, giving everyone
self-service access, filling the gaps at every layer from business to
infrastructure, and overlaying events such as deployments onto the graphs.

## Create centralised telemetry infrastructure

### In one sentence

Collect logs, events and metrics from every part of the system into one
place where they can be stored, searched, graphed and alerted on, so
anyone can see what is happening in production.

### The idea

The book opens Part IV with a familiar scene: something goes wrong in
production, and nobody can tell what. People log in to servers one by
one, read scattered log files, and argue about causes. Each team has its
own partial view. Problems take a long time to diagnose, and the same
problem is often diagnosed differently by different people.

The book's answer is a **centralised telemetry infrastructure**. Every
component, application, database, server, network device, pipeline,
sends its telemetry to a common system. The book describes the pieces of
such a system.

- **Collection** at the source: applications and infrastructure emit
  events, logs and metrics, often through agents or libraries.
- **An event router** that receives telemetry and sends it on, so sources
  do not need to know where it ends up.
- **Storage** suited to each kind of data: searchable logs, time series of
  metrics.
- **Analysis and display**: search, graphs, dashboards.
- **Alerting**: rules that notify people when something needs attention.

The key word is "everyone". Telemetry locked in one team's tool, or on
servers only operations can reach, cannot provide feedback to developers,
testers or product people. Centralising it makes one shared view of the
truth.

The book also distinguishes kinds of telemetry. **Logs** and **events**
record that something happened, with details: a payment failed for order
81 with code U30. **Metrics** are numbers measured over time: payments per
minute, error rate, response time. Both are needed. Metrics show that
something changed; logs and events explain what.

#### Logs, metrics and traces

Many teams today add a third kind of telemetry, the **trace**, which
follows a single request through every part of a system and records how
long each part took. In a loosely coupled system from chapter 13, where
one user action may touch several services, traces show where the time
went and where an error began. The book's principle covers all three:
collect them centrally and make them available to everyone.

#### Following one request through many services

Centralising telemetry pays off most when one request touches several
parts of the system. If every log line and trace carries the same
**request identifier**, generated at the edge and passed along on every
call, one search returns the whole story of that request.

Without the shared identifier, the same investigation would mean matching
timestamps across three services' logs by hand, which is slow and easy to
get wrong when many requests run at once.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My site's host keeps build logs and access logs in separate
places. I add a simple step that sends build results and a daily summary of
errors to one place I check. When a page breaks, I start from one view
instead of three.

**Realistic.** A team runs a dozen services, each logging to its own
servers. During an incident, three people spend an hour collecting logs by
hand. The team adds a log collector to every server, sends everything to a
central search tool, and adds a service name and request identifier to
every log line. The next incident's diagnosis starts with one search
across all services.

**In production: one view for all teams.** An organisation gives
developers, operations, testers and product people access to the same
telemetry system. Product managers start checking feature usage
themselves; developers check how their changes behave after deployment;
operations stop being the only people who can answer "what is
happening?".

### Step by step

1. **List every telemetry source**: applications, databases, servers,
   network, pipeline.
2. **Choose central stores** for logs, metrics and, where useful, traces.
3. **Send everything there**, through collectors or libraries.
4. **Add common fields**: service, environment, version, request
   identifier.
5. **Give everyone access**, with sensible permissions for sensitive data.
6. **Set retention deliberately**, by how long each kind is useful.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to centralise only infrastructure metrics, CPU and
memory, and leave application logs and business events scattered. The
most useful telemetry for solving problems usually comes from the
application. The fix is to centralise all kinds.

### In real systems

Log aggregation tools, time-series databases, tracing systems and
dashboard tools are combined, or provided together by observability
platforms.

## Create application logging telemetry that helps production

### In one sentence

Write logs for the people who will read them during a problem: structured,
at the right level, with the context needed to understand what happened.

### The idea

The book points out that every developer writes logs, but often for
themselves while developing, not for the people who will need them in
production. The result is logs that are either too noisy, filled with
details nobody needs, or too sparse, missing the one fact that would
explain a failure.

The book describes **logging levels**, each with a purpose:

- **DEBUG**: detailed information useful while developing or diagnosing a
  specific problem; normally off in production.
- **INFO**: normal, significant events, such as a user logging in or a
  payment starting.
- **WARN**: something unexpected that the system handled, but which may
  become a problem, such as a retry.
- **ERROR**: a failure of an operation, such as a payment that could not be
  processed.
- **FATAL**: a failure that stops the application or a major part of it.

Choosing the right level matters, because alerts and searches depend on
it. If everything is logged as ERROR, real errors are lost. If real
errors are logged as INFO, nobody notices them.

The book also lists the kinds of events worth logging, including
authentication decisions, access to sensitive data, system and data
changes, invalid input, resource limits, health and availability, startup
and shutdown, faults and errors, and calls to and from other systems.
Each is a point where someone, later, may need to know what happened.

#### Structured logs

Logs are most useful when machines can read them as well as people. A
**structured log** writes each event as a set of named fields, often as
JSON, rather than a sentence. Then a search can ask "all payment_failed
events for gateway upi in the last hour" directly. Here is a sketch, run
with node, with a minimum level that can be changed without a code change:

```js
// Structured logging with levels: one event per line, machine-readable fields.
const LEVELS = ["DEBUG", "INFO", "WARN", "ERROR", "FATAL"];
const minLevel = process.env.LOG_LEVEL || "INFO";
function log(level, event, fields = {}) {
  if (LEVELS.indexOf(level) < LEVELS.indexOf(minLevel)) return;
  console.log(JSON.stringify({ ts: "2026-10-23T09:00:00Z", level, event, service: "checkout", ...fields }));
}
log("DEBUG", "cart_loaded", { items: 3 });
log("INFO", "payment_started", { order: 81, amount: 499 });
log("WARN", "payment_retry", { order: 81, attempt: 2, reason: "gateway_timeout" });
log("ERROR", "payment_failed", { order: 81, gateway: "upi", code: "U30" });
```

With the default level it prints:

```text
{"ts":"2026-10-23T09:00:00Z","level":"INFO","event":"payment_started","service":"checkout","order":81,"amount":499}
{"ts":"2026-10-23T09:00:00Z","level":"WARN","event":"payment_retry","service":"checkout","order":81,"attempt":2,"reason":"gateway_timeout"}
{"ts":"2026-10-23T09:00:00Z","level":"ERROR","event":"payment_failed","service":"checkout","order":81,"gateway":"upi","code":"U30"}
```

Run with `LOG_LEVEL=DEBUG`, it also prints the first line:

```text
{"ts":"2026-10-23T09:00:00Z","level":"DEBUG","event":"cart_loaded","service":"checkout","items":3}
```

The three default lines tell a story someone can follow at three in the
morning: a payment for order 81 started, the gateway timed out, a retry
was made, and the payment failed with a specific code. Each line carries
the order number, so all events for one order can be found with one
search.

#### What not to log

Helpful logs are also careful logs. Passwords, full card numbers, secret
keys and unnecessary personal data should never appear in logs, because
logs are copied, kept and read by many people. A common practice is to
log identifiers rather than contents, and to mask sensitive fields in the
logging library so a mistake in one place cannot leak them.

#### Reviewing a log line like code

Because logs are read by other people under pressure, it helps to review
new log lines in code review with a few questions in mind.

<Compare left="Review question" right="What a good answer looks like" caption="Questions to ask of a new log line. Read across each row: each question checks one property that makes the line useful later." rows={[
  ["Who will read this, and when?", "on-call, during a failure; or analysts, later"],
  ["Is the level right?", "matches the agreed meaning of each level"],
  ["Can it be found?", "a stable event name and searchable fields"],
  ["Can it be connected?", "request id, order id, service, version"],
  ["Is anything sensitive in it?", "no secrets; personal data only if necessary and masked"],
  ["How often will it fire?", "not once per item in a tight loop"],
]} />

The last question catches a common problem: a log line inside a loop that
runs a million times a minute, which floods storage and hides everything
else. Such detail belongs in a metric, or in a single summary event.

#### Log volume and its cost

Logs are not free. Every line is sent, stored, indexed and searched, and
at scale the cost becomes a real budget item. Teams keep this under
control without losing value by keeping debug output off in production by
default and turning it on for one service or one request when needed,
sampling very frequent success events while keeping every error, and
setting retention by value: errors and audit events kept longer, routine
information shorter. The goal is not fewer logs for its own sake, but logs
whose cost matches how often they help someone answer a question.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My build script used to print "failed" when a page had a broken
link. I changed it to print the page, the link and the status code on one
line. Fixing broken links went from a hunt to a glance.

**Realistic.** A team's logs are mostly free text at INFO, with real
failures mixed in. They adopt structured logging with event names and
levels, add the request identifier to every line, and agree what each
level means. Alerts on ERROR events become meaningful, and searches for one
customer's problem take seconds.

**In production: a level change without a deploy.** A rare bug affects a
few customers. The team raises the log level to DEBUG for one service, for
an hour, through configuration, catches the detailed events around the
failure, and sets the level back. No deployment, and no permanent flood of
debug logs.

### Step by step

1. **Agree what each level means**, and write it down.
2. **Log structured events** with names and fields.
3. **Add identifiers**: request, order, user where allowed, service,
   version.
4. **Log at the points that matter**: authentication, changes, calls to
   other systems, errors.
5. **Keep secrets out** with masking in the logging library.
6. **Make the level configurable** at run time.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to log everything at one level, so the logs are a
wall of noise. The fix is clear level definitions and reviews of new log
lines like any other code.

### In real systems

Structured logging libraries exist for every major language, and log
search tools index JSON fields automatically. Many teams review log lines
in code review.

## Use telemetry to guide problem solving

### In one sentence

Use telemetry to form and test hypotheses about a problem, the way a
scientist uses experiments, instead of relying on guesses, opinions or
blame.

### The idea

The book argues that without telemetry, problem solving in production
tends to become a contest of opinions. Each team suspects another, people
reboot things to see if it helps, and the loudest voice decides. This is
slow, and it damages trust, because when the cause is not known, blame
fills the gap.

With good telemetry, problem solving can follow something like the
**scientific method**. State a hypothesis: "the failures are in one
region". Look at the data that would confirm or refute it. Discard
hypotheses the data refutes, and refine the ones it supports. Each step
narrows the search, and the conclusion rests on evidence everyone can see.

The book connects this to culture. When the facts are visible to all,
discussion moves from "whose fault is it?" to "what does the data show?".
That shift supports the blameless culture the Third Way needs, which
chapter 19 develops.

#### Narrowing down a cause with grouped telemetry

Here is a sketch, run with node. Five thousand payment events have a
gateway, an app version and a region. A hidden cause makes card payments
from app version 5.2 fail far more often. The script tests one hypothesis
per field by grouping failures:

```js
// Using telemetry to test hypotheses: group failed payments by each field.
let seed = 21;
const rand = () => ((seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2147483648) / 2147483648);
const pick = (a) => a[Math.floor(rand() * a.length)];
const events = [];
for (let i = 0; i < 5000; i++) {
  const e = { gateway: pick(["upi", "card", "netbanking"]), app: pick(["5.1", "5.2"]), region: pick(["north", "south", "west"]) };
  const failRate = e.gateway === "card" && e.app === "5.2" ? 0.2 : 0.01; // hidden cause
  e.failed = rand() < failRate;
  events.push(e);
}
for (const field of ["region", "gateway", "app"]) {
  const groups = {};
  for (const e of events) {
    const g = (groups[e[field]] ??= { n: 0, f: 0 });
    g.n++; if (e.failed) g.f++;
  }
  console.log(`by ${field.padEnd(8)}`, Object.entries(groups).map(([k, g]) => `${k}: ${((g.f / g.n) * 100).toFixed(1)}%`).join("  "));
}
const both = events.filter((e) => e.gateway === "card" && e.app === "5.2");
console.log(`card AND app 5.2: ${((both.filter((e) => e.failed).length / both.length) * 100).toFixed(1)}% of ${both.length}`);
```

It prints:

```text
by region   south: 4.2%  north: 3.8%  west: 4.0%
by gateway  netbanking: 0.6%  upi: 0.9%  card: 10.5%
by app      5.2: 7.4%  5.1: 0.8%
card AND app 5.2: 21.2% of 775
```

Read as a sequence of hypotheses, the output is a small investigation.
"It is one region": refuted, all regions are similar. "It is one
gateway": supported, card is far worse. "It is the new app version":
also supported. Combining the two supported hypotheses shows the real
pattern: card payments from version 5.2 fail about one time in five, while
everything else is near normal. The next step is obvious, look at what
changed in card handling in 5.2, and nobody had to guess or blame.

#### Keeping the evidence

Telemetry also protects evidence. In the first story above, the restart
erased the state that would have explained the failure. A team that
relies on telemetry learns a simple habit: before restarting, rolling back
or scaling, capture what is needed, a snapshot of the graphs, the relevant
log search, a thread dump if the process is stuck. It takes a minute and
it means the later review, the subject of chapter 19, works from facts
rather than memory. Restoring service still comes first; the habit is to
take that one minute when it is safe to do so.

### Worked example

**Simple.** Readers told me some pages were slow. I guessed it was my
images. My host's analytics showed the slow pages were all ones with
embedded code playgrounds, regardless of images. The guess was wrong; the
data pointed to the real cause in one look.

**Realistic.** An outage meeting starts with the database team blaming a
recent application release and the application team blaming the
database. Someone shares the telemetry: database response times were
normal throughout, but one application endpoint began making ten times
more queries after the release. The discussion ends in minutes, and the
fix follows.

**In production: telemetry ends a long argument.** A team has argued for
weeks about whether a cache is helping. They add metrics for cache hits,
misses and the response time of each. The data shows the cache serves most
requests and makes them much faster, but one kind of request always
misses. The argument becomes a specific improvement.

### Step by step

1. **State the problem** as a measurable symptom.
2. **List hypotheses**, including unpopular ones.
3. **For each, say what data would refute it.**
4. **Check the data**, starting with the cheapest hypotheses.
5. **Combine supported hypotheses** to narrow further.
6. **Share the evidence** with everyone involved.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to change things, reboot, roll back, scale up,
before looking at data, so the evidence is lost and the cause never
found. Where safety allows, capture the telemetry first, then act.
Restoring service always comes first when users are suffering, but even a
quick snapshot of key graphs helps later.

### In real systems

Log search with grouping, metric dashboards that can be split by any
field, and traces that show where time went are the main tools.
Incident reviews, chapter 19, often reconstruct the investigation from
the telemetry.

## Enable creation of production metrics as part of daily work

### In one sentence

Make adding a new metric as easy as adding a line of code, so developers
measure what they build as a normal part of building it.

### The idea

The book argues that if creating a metric requires a request to another
team, a ticket and a wait, few metrics will be created, and those that are
will be the ones someone thought of long ago. Instead, the telemetry
system should make it trivial for any developer to add a metric from code:
a counter for an event, a timer for an operation, a gauge for a level.
The metric should then appear in the central system automatically, ready
to graph and alert on.

When metrics are this cheap, developers add them as they write features:
how often is the new button used, how long does the new query take, how
often does the new fallback trigger. The book describes organisations
where developers routinely add metrics for every feature, and where the
volume and variety of metrics become a strength, because almost any
question about production can be answered from existing data.

#### Why percentiles, not averages

The most common metric is response time, and the most common mistake is
to graph its average. Here is a sketch, run with node, with a thousand
requests, most fast and a few very slow:

```js
// Why percentiles: 1000 request timings, most fast, a few very slow.
let seed = 5;
const rand = () => ((seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2147483648) / 2147483648);
const times = Array.from({ length: 1000 }, () => (rand() < 0.04 ? 2000 + rand() * 3000 : 80 + rand() * 60));
times.sort((a, b) => a - b);
const pct = (p) => times[Math.ceil((p / 100) * times.length) - 1];
const mean = times.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) / times.length;
console.log(`mean ${mean.toFixed(0)} ms | median ${pct(50).toFixed(0)} ms | p95 ${pct(95).toFixed(0)} ms | p99 ${pct(99).toFixed(0)} ms | max ${times.at(-1).toFixed(0)} ms`);
console.log(`requests slower than 1 s: ${times.filter((t) => t > 1000).length} of 1000`);
```

It prints:

```text
mean 262 ms | median 110 ms | p95 139 ms | p99 4452 ms | max 4935 ms
requests slower than 1 s: 43 of 1000
```

The numbers disagree in an instructive way. The mean, 262 ms, describes
no real request: typical ones take about 110 ms, and the slow ones take
seconds. The 95th percentile, 139 ms, looks healthy because the slow
requests are only about four percent of the total, just under its
threshold. The 99th percentile, 4452 ms, reveals them. Forty-three users
in a thousand waited more than a second, and neither the mean nor p95
shows that clearly. The lesson is to graph several percentiles and to
count requests over a limit, not to trust one summary number.

#### The kinds of metric a developer adds

When everyone can add metrics, consistent naming keeps the result usable.

#### Making metrics part of the definition of done

The simplest way to make metrics daily work is to put them in the
definition of done. A feature is not finished until it reports whether it
is being used and whether it is working: a counter for its main action, a
counter for its failures, and a timer for its slowest step. Reviewers ask
for these the same way they ask for tests. Over time this produces a
system where every feature can answer two questions from a dashboard, is
anyone using this, and is it healthy, without a special project to add
monitoring later. It also feeds chapter 17, because a feature that
already reports its usage is ready for an experiment.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I add a counter to my site's search component for searches
with no results. A week later, the counts show which topics readers look
for that I have not written about. That metric cost one line.

**Realistic.** A team adopts a metrics library that sends counters and
timers to the central system with no extra setup. Their definition of done
gains a line: "new features emit usage and timing metrics". Within a few
months, questions that used to need a special report are answered from a
dashboard in minutes.

**In production: the fallback counter.** A developer adds a counter each
time a service falls back to cached data because a dependency timed out,
from chapter 13's resilience patterns. The counter is normally near zero.
One afternoon it rises sharply, giving the team an early warning that the
dependency is struggling, well before users notice anything.

### Step by step

1. **Provide a metrics library** that makes counters, timers and gauges
   one line each.
2. **Send metrics automatically** to the central store, tagged with
   service and version.
3. **Add metrics to the definition of done** for new features.
4. **Graph percentiles**, not averages, for timings.
5. **Review metrics occasionally** and retire unused ones.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to make metrics an operations concern, created by
request. The fix is self-service metrics in code, created by the people
who build the feature.

A second mistake is to tag metrics with unbounded values, such as user
identifiers, which can overwhelm metric stores. Such detail belongs in
logs and events; metrics use a small set of tags.

### In real systems

Metrics libraries and agents for common languages send data to
time-series stores; dashboards show percentiles from histograms.

## Create self-service access to telemetry and information radiators

### In one sentence

Make telemetry visible to everyone without asking, through self-service
tools and information radiators placed where people work.

### The idea

Telemetry only provides feedback if people look at it. The book argues
for two kinds of visibility.

**Self-service access** means anyone in the value stream can search logs,
build a graph or set up a dashboard without asking another team. If
developers must ask operations for data, they will rarely ask, and
feedback from production will stay thin.

**Information radiators** are displays placed where people work, showing
the most important measures continuously, a term from agile practice. A
screen in the team area, or a dashboard pinned in the team chat, shows
deployment status, error rates, response times and key business numbers.
Nobody has to remember to look; the information radiates.

The book also describes a cultural effect. When the state of production is
visible to everyone, including people outside engineering, the
organisation shares one picture of reality. Problems are harder to hide,
and the visibility itself is a signal of trust: the team has nothing to
conceal.

#### What a good radiator shows

A radiator that shows everything shows nothing. The most useful ones show
a few measures that the team acts on, each with a clear sense of normal.

#### Radiating to users: status pages

The same idea extends beyond the organisation. A public **status page**
shows users whether the service is healthy and, during an incident, what
is known and when the next update will come. It reduces the flood of
support requests during problems and builds trust, because users see that
the team knows and is acting. Its content should come from the same
telemetry the team uses, so it is not a separate story that drifts from
the truth.

#### A radiator nobody looks at

A common failure is the dashboard on a big screen that everyone walks past.
It usually happens for one of three reasons: it shows too many graphs to
read at a glance, it shows measures nobody acts on, or it is always red so
red means nothing. The fix follows from the cause.

  ["Only engineers understand it", "plain labels and a business measure on top"],

A good test is to ask someone walking past what the screen tells them. If
they can say whether things are healthy within a few seconds, it works.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I keep a small dashboard of my site's build status, page
views and broken links on my desktop's home screen. When something
changes, I notice without going looking.

**Realistic.** A team puts a screen in its area showing the four panels
above. In the first week, a developer notices the error rate step up a few
minutes after their deployment and rolls back before any customer reports
arrive. The radiator becomes the first place everyone glances in the
morning.

**In production: support and engineering on one screen.** Support staff
gain access to the same dashboards as engineers. When customers call about
slow payments, support can see whether payments are slow for everyone or
just one customer, and tells engineers with evidence attached. Incidents
are noticed sooner, and support calls are shorter.

### Step by step

1. **Give everyone access** to search and dashboards.
2. **Build a team dashboard** with a few measures the team acts on.
3. **Show it where people work**: a screen or the team chat.
4. **Define normal and act-when** for each panel.
5. **Share with support and product** so they see the same picture.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is a radiator with dozens of graphs that nobody reads.
The fix is a few panels with clear normals and triggers.

### In real systems

Dashboard tools, chat integrations that post key graphs and status pages
are common forms of radiators.

## Find and fill any telemetry gaps

### In one sentence

Check that telemetry covers every layer, business, application,
infrastructure, client software and the deployment pipeline, and add
what is missing, because problems hide in the layers nobody measures.

### The idea

The book lists the levels at which telemetry is needed and suggests that
teams look systematically for gaps.

**Business level.** Measures of what the business cares about: sales,
signups, active users, revenue, conversion through a funnel, the success
of a campaign. These tell whether the system is achieving its purpose.

**Application level.** Measures of how the application behaves: response
times, error rates, throughput, the behaviour of individual features and
dependencies. These tell whether the software is working.

**Infrastructure level.** Measures of servers, databases, networks and
storage: CPU, memory, disk, connections, saturation. These tell whether
the platform has the capacity and health the application needs.

**Client software level.** Measures from the user's device: page load in
the browser, app crashes, errors in client code, how long screens take to
become usable. These tell what users actually experience, which can
differ greatly from what the servers report.

**Deployment pipeline level.** Measures of the pipeline itself: build
status, test times, deployment frequency and duration, failed deployments.
These tell whether the flow of changes is healthy.

The book stresses connecting the levels. An infrastructure metric alone,
"CPU at 90 percent", does not say whether it matters. Linked to the
application, "response time rising", and the business, "orders falling",
it becomes a clear signal. The reverse also holds: a drop in orders is
easier to explain when application and infrastructure telemetry is on the
same view.

  { name: "Application", note: "response times, errors, throughput, feature use" },
  { name: "Infrastructure", note: "CPU, memory, disk, network, saturation" },
  { name: "Client software", note: "page load, crashes, client errors, as users see them" },
  { name: "Deployment pipeline", note: "build status, test times, deploys, failures" },
]} />

#### Finding gaps with a coverage table

A simple way to find gaps is to take the most important user journeys and
check, for each level, whether there is telemetry that would show a
problem.

The gaps in the table are typical. Client-side problems are invisible to
server metrics: if the payment screen crashes in the app, the server sees
fewer payment requests, not an error. Business gaps, such as searches
with no results, hide product problems that no error rate will show.

#### Business and application metrics together

The book encourages teams to put business measures next to technical ones
and to look at them together. A useful habit is to graph a business
measure against the same time last week, because many businesses have
strong daily and weekly patterns, and a drop is only visible against what
is normal for that hour.

The last row is the most important. When business measures fall and all
technical measures look normal, there is a gap: something users
experience is not being measured.

#### Telemetry for the pipeline itself

The deployment pipeline is the level teams most often forget, yet it is
the one that shows whether the flow from Part III is healthy. A few
pipeline measures, collected in the same central system as everything
else, make slow drift visible before it becomes a crisis.

<Trace columns={["Pipeline measure", "Healthy", "Drifting"]} rows={[
  ["Commit stage duration", "steady within budget", "creeping up a little every week"],
  ["Share of runs that are red", "low, and red is short", "rising; red lasting hours"],

  ["Deployments per day", "steady or rising", "falling as people batch"],
  ["Lead time, commit to production", "hours", "days"],
]} caption="Pipeline telemetry. Read across each row: each drift is gradual and easy to miss without a graph, and each erodes flow." />

These measures connect directly to chapter 10's andon cord and chapter
11's small batches. A commit stage that grows by a few seconds a week
will, unwatched, double within a year, and people will start batching to
avoid the wait.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My site's server metrics were all fine, but readers emailed
that a page was broken. The page rendered; a script in the browser was
failing. I added a tiny client-side error report, and the next browser
error appeared in my logs before anyone emailed.

**Realistic.** A team builds the coverage table for its three main
journeys and finds five gaps, mostly client-side and business. They fill
them over two iterations: browser error reporting, app crash reporting, a
"no results" counter for search, and a signup funnel. A month later, a
drop in the signup funnel at one step leads them to a confusing form
field that no technical metric had flagged.

**In production: the silent failure.** One evening, orders fall to half
the usual level for that hour, but every technical dashboard is green.
Because the team graphs orders against last week, the drop is noticed in
minutes. The cause is a change by a third-party payment provider that
made a button invisible on some phones. No server ever saw an error.
Client telemetry is added afterwards so the next such problem shows
directly.

### Step by step

1. **List the key user journeys.**
2. **Build a coverage table** across the five levels.
3. **Fill the gaps**, starting with client and business ones.
4. **Graph business measures** against the same time last week.
5. **Put levels side by side** on dashboards.
6. **Repeat the table** when journeys change.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to trust server-side metrics as the whole picture.
Users experience the client; many problems never reach the servers. The
fix is client telemetry and business measures as early warnings.

### In real systems

Real user monitoring in browsers, crash reporting in mobile apps,
business event streams and pipeline dashboards fill the different levels.

## Overlay other relevant information onto metrics

### In one sentence

Mark deployments, configuration changes and other events directly on
metric graphs, so a change in behaviour can be linked to its likely cause
at a glance.

### The idea

The book's final practice is simple and powerful. When a graph shows a
sudden change, the first question is "what changed?". If deployments,
configuration changes, feature toggle flips, infrastructure changes and
even marketing campaigns are drawn on the same graph as vertical markers,
the answer is often visible immediately.

The book notes that changes are a frequent cause of production problems,
so connecting changes to effects is the fastest way to diagnose many of them.
It also supports the culture of small, frequent deployments: when each
deployment is small and marked, a problem that appears just after one
points to a short list of changes.

Here is a sketch, run with node, of an error-rate series with a deployment
marked at minute 30:

```js
// Overlay deployments on a metric: did the error rate change after a deploy?
const minutes = [];
let seed = 9;
const rand = () => ((seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2147483648) / 2147483648);
for (let m = 0; m < 60; m++) {
  const base = m < 30 ? 0.5 : 2.5; // deploy at minute 30 raises errors
  minutes.push(+(base + (rand() - 0.5)).toFixed(1));
}
const deploys = { 30: "build 412 to production" };
for (let m = 24; m < 36; m++)
  console.log(`${String(m).padStart(2)}  ${"#".repeat(Math.round(minutes[m] * 4)).padEnd(14)} ${minutes[m].toFixed(1)}%${deploys[m] ? "   <-- deploy: " + deploys[m] : ""}`);
const avg = (a) => (a.reduce((x, y) => x + y, 0) / a.length).toFixed(2);
console.log(`avg error rate 30 min before: ${avg(minutes.slice(0, 30))}% | after: ${avg(minutes.slice(30))}%`);
```

It prints:

```text
24  ####           0.9%
25  #              0.2%
26                 0.1%
27  ##             0.6%
28  ###            0.8%
29  ##             0.5%
30  #########      2.2%   <-- deploy: build 412 to production
31  #########      2.3%
32  #########      2.3%
33  ############   2.9%
34  #########      2.2%
35  #########      2.3%
avg error rate 30 min before: 0.46% | after: 2.47%
```

Without the marker, the step at minute 30 is a mystery to investigate.
With it, the first hypothesis writes itself: build 412. The averages
confirm a jump from under half a percent to about two and a half. The
team can look at the small set of changes in that build, or simply roll
it back and investigate calmly, using chapter 12's patterns.

  ["Deployment", "build 412 to production", "many problems follow a change"],
  ["Feature toggle", "newCheckout to 50 percent", "a release without a deployment"],
  ["Configuration", "cache size raised", "changes behaviour without code"],
  ["Infrastructure", "database failover", "explains sudden latency shifts"],
  ["Business event", "sale starts at noon", "explains traffic surges that are not problems"],
]} caption="Events worth marking on graphs. Read across each row: each kind of change can explain a shift in a metric, and the last kind prevents false alarms." />

#### From markers to automatic correlation

Once changes are recorded as events in the telemetry system, the next
step is to let the system do the first comparison. When a key metric
shifts, the dashboard can list the changes in the preceding minutes,
deployments, toggles, configuration, ordered by how close they are in
time and whether they touched the affected service. This does not replace
judgement, since a change near a shift is a suspect, not a proven cause,
but it gives the on-call person a short, ranked list of hypotheses to test
straight away.

  "Metric shifts beyond normal",
  "Collect changes in the last 30 minutes",
  "Rank by time and affected service",
  "Show the list beside the graph",
  "Person tests the top suspect",

#### What else is worth overlaying

Deployments and toggles are the obvious markers, but other events explain
shifts just as often: a marketing email going out, a partner's outage, a
holiday, a database maintenance window, or a change in a third-party
library's configuration. Recording these as events too, even by hand at
first, saves the on-call person from chasing a code cause for a traffic
spike that a campaign explains.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I mark each site deployment on my page-view graph. When views
dropped one day, the marker showed a deploy an hour earlier; I had
accidentally removed a page from the sitemap. Without the marker, I would
have blamed a quiet news day.

**Realistic.** A team's dashboards show markers for every deployment,
toggle change and configuration change, posted automatically by the
pipeline and the toggle system. Incident diagnosis often starts and ends
with "the step lines up with this marker", and rollback follows.

**In production: a false alarm avoided.** At noon, traffic triples and
response times rise. On-call engineers start an investigation, then see
the marker: a promoted sale started at noon. The system is busy, not
broken. They watch capacity instead of hunting for a fault.

### Step by step

1. **Have the pipeline post a marker** for every deployment.
2. **Post markers for toggles and configuration changes** too.
3. **Add business events**, such as campaigns, to the same timeline.
4. **Show markers on every key graph** by default.
5. **Start investigations** by checking markers near the change.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to keep deployment records in a separate tool from
metrics, so connecting the two needs manual comparison of timestamps. The
fix is automatic markers on the graphs.

### In real systems

Most dashboard tools support annotations posted by pipelines and toggle
services; some show deployments and changes on every graph by default.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 14, Create Telemetry to Enable Seeing and Solving Problems">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the lesson is that we cannot fix what we cannot
see. I learn the difference between logs, metrics and traces, the meaning
of each log level, and why telemetry should be collected in one place
rather than scattered across servers. I learn to write a log line that
someone else could act on.

An example: I replace "error" in one of my scripts with a structured line
naming the event, the item affected and the cause, and I notice how much
faster I find the next failure.

The habit to build here is asking, for anything I write, "how would I
know if this stopped working in production?".

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I write structured logs with identifiers and
consistent levels, keep secrets out of them, and make the level
configurable at run time. I add counters and timers to features as I
build them, and I graph percentiles rather than averages. I use a team
dashboard with a few panels that have clear normals.

An example: I add a timer around a new database query and graph its p50
and p99, which shows a slow tail that the average had hidden.

I also add the request identifier to every log line in my service and
check that one search returns a full request's story, which is the first
test of whether my logs will help during a real problem.

I also start testing hypotheses with grouped telemetry during
problems, rather than guessing.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I design centralised telemetry for a system of
services, with common fields, request identifiers and traces across
service boundaries. I build coverage tables for key journeys across all
five levels and fill the gaps, especially client and business ones. I
make the pipeline and toggle system post markers on every graph.

An example: I find that a checkout journey has no client-side crash
reporting, add it, and connect it to the same dashboard as payment
errors and orders per minute.

I choose retention for each kind of telemetry deliberately, keeping cheap
metrics for a long time and detailed logs for as long as they are useful.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases include: very high-volume events, where logging every one is
too costly and sampling is needed; batch jobs and scheduled tasks, which
need telemetry about whether they ran at all, not just how they behaved;
offline-capable clients, whose telemetry arrives late; and third-party
services we cannot instrument, where we measure our calls to them
instead.

An example: a nightly report job emits a "completed" event with a count.
An alert fires if the event does not arrive by morning, catching the
silent failure where the job never started.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases include: telemetry that fails at the same time as the system
it watches, such as a logging pipeline overloaded during an incident;
clocks that disagree across servers, scrambling the order of events;
metrics with unbounded tags that overwhelm the store; and personal data
leaking into logs through error messages that echo user input.

An example: during a traffic surge, the log pipeline drops events just
when they are needed. The team gives error-level events priority and
keeps a small, separate path for the most important metrics.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

At production level, every component sends structured logs, metrics and
traces to central systems, with common fields and request identifiers.
Developers add metrics in one line as part of the definition of done.
Everyone has self-service access, and teams keep radiators with a few
actionable panels. Coverage tables exist for key journeys across all five
levels, with gaps tracked. Deployments, toggles and configuration
changes appear as markers on every graph, and business measures are
compared with the same time last week.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is that telemetry is part of the product, not an
afterthought. I measure what I build as I build it. I prefer evidence to
opinion, and I share data openly so discussions start from the same facts.
When something surprises me in production, I ask what telemetry would have
shown it sooner, and I add it.

An example of the mindset: after every incident, one follow-up is always
"what signal would have told us earlier?", and it is added that week.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design terms, telemetry is a cross-cutting capability that
should be built into the platform: libraries that make logging, metrics
and tracing automatic, collectors on every host, a central router and
stores, and dashboards and alerts on top. It connects to chapter 13:
loosely coupled services need request identifiers and traces to be
understandable as a whole.

An example: a shared service template includes structured logging,
standard metrics for every endpoint and trace propagation, so every new
service is observable from its first deployment.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategy is to make production visible to everyone, then make it
understandable, then make it part of daily work. Centralise all kinds of
telemetry. Standardise log levels and structure. Make metrics
self-service in code. Build coverage tables and fill gaps at every level.
Overlay changes on graphs. Put radiators where people work. And use
telemetry to replace opinion and blame with evidence.

Done when any person in the value stream can see, without asking, how
production is behaving at every level, any shift in a key graph can be
linked to a change at a glance, and new features ship with their own
metrics.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>How much should we log?</summary>

Enough to understand what happened during a problem, at levels that let
people filter. Log significant events at INFO, handled surprises at WARN,
failures at ERROR, and keep DEBUG off in production unless diagnosing.
Review new log lines like code.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is telemetry the same as monitoring?</summary>

Monitoring usually means watching known signals and alerting on them.
Telemetry is the data that makes monitoring, and much more, possible:
investigation, learning about users and checking changes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Who owns the telemetry system?</summary>

Often a platform team builds and runs it, while every team owns the
telemetry its own services emit and the dashboards it uses.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What about the cost of storing all this?</summary>

Costs are real. Keep metrics, which are compact, for a long time; keep
detailed logs for shorter periods; sample very high-volume events. Choose
retention by how long each kind of data is useful.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Do we need traces?</summary>

For a single application, logs and metrics may be enough. For many
services, traces become very valuable, because they show where a slow or
failing request spent its time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do we stop dashboards multiplying?</summary>

Keep a few team dashboards with clear purposes and owners, and let people
build ad hoc views when investigating without saving them all.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should non-engineers see production telemetry?</summary>

The book argues yes, for the most part. Support, product and leaders
make better decisions with the same facts. Sensitive data can be
restricted while the overall picture is shared.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does this chapter connect to the next?</summary>

Chapter 15 analyses telemetry: finding anomalies, setting good alerts and
anticipating problems before they affect users.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the first telemetry to add to a system with almost none?</summary>

Usually three things: structured error logs with request identifiers, a
response time and error rate metric for the main entry points, and one
business measure such as orders or signups per minute, graphed against the
same time last week. Together they show whether users are being served and
point to where failures are.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do we keep personal data safe while sharing telemetry widely?</summary>

Log identifiers rather than contents, mask sensitive fields in the logging
library, restrict access to the few logs that must contain personal data,
and share aggregated metrics and dashboards broadly. Most questions can
be answered without personal data at all.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Should every team pick its own telemetry tools?</summary>

Usually not. Teams can add their own metrics, logs and dashboards freely,
but sharing one central system means a request can be followed across
team boundaries and everyone learns one set of tools. Variety in what is
measured is healthy; variety in where it is stored makes cross-team
problems much harder to solve.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Centralise logs, events, metrics and traces so everyone shares one view of production.
- Metrics show that something changed; logs and events explain what; traces show where.
- Write structured logs at meaningful levels, with identifiers, and never with secrets.
- Use telemetry to test hypotheses, replacing guesses and blame with evidence.
- Make metrics one line of code and part of the definition of done.
- Graph percentiles and counts over a limit, not averages.
- Give everyone self-service access, and keep a few radiators where people work.
- Fill gaps at every level: business, application, infrastructure, client and pipeline.
- Overlay deployments and other changes on graphs to connect causes and effects.

## Glossary

- **Telemetry**: data that systems emit about their behaviour: logs, events, metrics and traces.
- **Centralised telemetry**: one system that collects, stores and shows telemetry from every source.
- **Event router**: the part that receives telemetry and forwards it to stores.
- **Log**: a record of something that happened.
- **Structured log**: a log entry written as named fields rather than free text.
- **Log level**: a label, such as DEBUG, INFO, WARN, ERROR or FATAL, describing an entry's importance.
- **Metric**: a number measured over time.
- **Counter**: a metric that counts occurrences.
- **Gauge**: a metric that shows a level that rises and falls.
- **Histogram**: a metric that records the distribution of values, such as response times.
- **Percentile**: the value below which a given share of measurements fall, such as p99.
- **Trace**: the path and timing of one request through a system's components.
- **Request identifier**: a value carried through all telemetry for one request.
- **Information radiator**: a display of key measures placed where people work.
- **Telemetry gap**: a part of the system or user experience with no telemetry to show problems.
- **Annotation or marker**: an event, such as a deployment, drawn on a graph.
