Once a value stream has been chosen, the next job is to understand it:
who works on it, what steps a change goes through, where it waits, and
where it goes wrong. This chapter is about seeing the work clearly, then
organising a team and a set of habits to improve it: a dedicated
transformation team, short planning horizons, capacity reserved for paying
down technical debt, and work made visible on shared boards and tools.

- draw a value stream map with lead time, process time and percent complete and accurate for each step;

- explain why a dedicated transformation team, a shared goal and short planning horizons help;
- argue for reserving a fixed share of capacity for non-functional work and technical debt;
- use shared boards, chat and backlogs to make work visible and reinforce the behaviour I want.

## Identify the teams supporting our value stream

### In one sentence

Before a value stream can be improved, everyone who contributes to it has
to be identified, because a change usually passes through far more hands
than any one team realises.

### The idea

A value stream is rarely owned by one team. A single change to a product
may involve a product owner who decides what to build, developers who
write it, testers who check it, operations engineers who run the
infrastructure, security who review it, a release manager who schedules
it, and sometimes a change board that approves it. Each sees their own
part. Nobody sees the whole.

The book lists the kinds of people who typically support a value stream:

- **Product owner**: the voice of the business, who decides what to build next.
- **Development**: the team that builds the application.
- **QA**: the people who make sure feedback loops exist to check the product works as intended.
- **Operations**: the people who keep the production environment running and help the service meet its goals.
- **Infosec**: the people responsible for securing systems and data.
- **Release managers**: the people who coordinate and manage the production deployment and release process.
- **Technology executives or value stream manager**: the person accountable for the stream meeting its goals from start to finish.

The point of the list is not the job titles, which vary by organisation.
It is that every one of these roles touches a change, so every one of them
has to be in the room when the stream is mapped and improved. Leaving out
operations or security means the map misses some of the longest waits,
because those are often where hand-offs to other teams happen.

#### Why the whole set matters

Each role has its own measures of success. Development is often measured
on features shipped, operations on uptime, security on vulnerabilities
avoided, QA on defects found. Those measures pull in different directions:
more features means more change, which operations sees as risk. The book's
argument, from chapter 1's core chronic conflict, is that these conflicts
only get resolved when the people involved see the whole stream and share
a goal for it. Identifying everyone is the first step to that.

It also shows where the invisible work is. When operations is in the room,
the map includes the environment requests, firewall changes and on-call
work that development never sees. When security is in the room, it
includes the review queue. Those are often the steps with the longest
waits.

### Worked example

**Simple.** For my blog, the "teams" are all me, but in different roles: I
decide what to write (product owner), write and build it (development),
proofread and run the checks (QA), publish it (release) and keep the site
up (operations). Listing them separately showed me that "operations" work,
fixing a broken build after a dependency update, was taking time I never
planned for.

**Realistic.** A team maps its checkout service and initially invites only
developers and the product owner. The map shows a two-day path from commit
to production. When operations and security join, the map grows: a
security review that waits for a weekly meeting, an environment request
ticket that waits days, and a change approval that waits for a fortnightly
board. The real lead time is weeks, and most of it is in steps the first
group did not know existed.

### Step by step

1. **Start from a recent change** that went to production, and trace who
   touched it.
2. **List every role**, including approvals and reviews, not only the
   people who wrote code.
3. **Name a person** for each role who can speak for it.
4. **Find the value stream manager**, or agree who will be accountable for
   the whole.
5. **Invite all of them** to the mapping session.
6. **Ask each what they need** from the steps before them, and what they
   produce for the steps after.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to map the stream with development alone. The result
looks short and fast, because the waits are in other teams' queues. The fix
is to include everyone a change passes through, especially operations,
security and whoever approves changes.

### In real systems

Many organisations formalise the value stream manager role, sometimes as a
product or service owner with end-to-end accountability. Without it,
improvements tend to stop at team boundaries, because nobody is responsible
for the waits between teams.

## Create a value stream map to see the work

### In one sentence

A value stream map draws every step a change goes through, with how long
it is worked on, how long it waits and how often it arrives right, so the
biggest problems become visible and measurable.

### The idea

A value stream map comes from lean manufacturing, and the book adapts it
for technology work. It is a diagram of the steps from a request to value
delivered to a customer. For each step it records three numbers:

- **Process time**: how long the work is actually being worked on at this step.
- **Lead time**: how long the work spends at this step in total, from arriving to leaving, including waiting.
- **Percent complete and accurate (%C/A)**: the share of the time the next step can use this step's output as it is, without correcting it, asking for missing information, or sending it back.

From those numbers come the measures that matter for the whole stream.
**Total lead time** is the sum of the step lead times. **Total process
time** is the sum of the step process times. Their ratio, sometimes called
the **activity ratio** or flow efficiency, shows how much of the elapsed
time anyone is actually working on the change. And multiplying the
%C/A of every step gives the **rolled %C/A**: how often a change gets
through the whole stream with no rework at all.

The book emphasises that the map should be drawn by the people who do the
work, in a room together, and that it should show the current state
honestly, not the documented process. The aim is not precision; rough
numbers are enough to show where the waits and the rework are. Then the
team draws a future state: what the stream should look like after
improvement, typically within a year or so, which sets the direction for
the transformation.

#### Working the numbers

Here is the arithmetic for the map above, as a small script run with node.
The numbers are invented for illustration; real maps use the team's own
estimates.

```js
// Value stream map arithmetic: lead time, process time, and rolled %C/A.
const steps = [
  { name: "Triage request", processH: 1, leadH: 40, pctCA: 0.9 },
  { name: "Develop", processH: 16, leadH: 80, pctCA: 0.8 },
  { name: "Manual test", processH: 8, leadH: 120, pctCA: 0.7 },
  { name: "Change approval", processH: 1, leadH: 72, pctCA: 0.95 },
  { name: "Deploy", processH: 2, leadH: 24, pctCA: 0.85 },
];
const lead = steps.reduce((s, x) => s + x.leadH, 0);
const proc = steps.reduce((s, x) => s + x.processH, 0);
const rolled = steps.reduce((p, x) => p * x.pctCA, 1);
console.log(`total lead time   ${lead} h`);
console.log(`total process time ${proc} h`);
console.log(`activity ratio    ${((proc / lead) * 100).toFixed(1)}%`);
console.log(`rolled %C/A       ${(rolled * 100).toFixed(1)}%`);
const worst = [...steps].sort((a, b) => (b.leadH - b.processH) - (a.leadH - a.processH))[0];
console.log(`biggest wait      ${worst.name} (${worst.leadH - worst.processH} h waiting)`);
```

It prints:

```text
total lead time   336 h
total process time 28 h
activity ratio    8.3%
rolled %C/A       40.7%
biggest wait      Manual test (112 h waiting)
```

Three lessons come out of these numbers. First, the change is being worked
on for a small fraction of the elapsed time; the rest is waiting. Making
developers code faster would barely move the total. Second, even though
every step gets it right most of the time, fewer than half of changes get
through with no rework, because the shares multiply. Small quality
problems at each step add up to a large one overall. Third, the biggest
single wait is the manual test step, which makes it the obvious first
target: automating tests attacks both the longest wait and the lowest
%C/A.

The change approval row is worth noticing. It waits a long time and almost
never sends anything back. That pattern, a long wait with a very high
%C/A, often means the step is adding delay without adding much safety,
which chapter 18 returns to.

#### Drawing the future state

The current-state map shows what is. The future-state map shows what the
team intends the stream to look like, usually within six months to a year.
It is not a fantasy of perfection. It is a version of the stream that the
people in the room believe they can reach, drawn with the same boxes and
numbers, so the gap between the two maps becomes the improvement plan.

A good future state usually has fewer steps, because some hand-offs are
merged or removed; shorter waits, because queues are replaced by
self-service or automation; and higher %C/A, because problems are caught
earlier, closer to where they are made. The team then asks, for each
difference between current and future, what would have to change, and
orders those changes by how much of the gap each one closes. That ordered
list becomes the transformation team's backlog.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I mapped how a blog post goes from idea to published: idea
note (waits days), draft (a few hours of work, waits a week), proofread
(an hour, then waits), build and publish (minutes). The process time was
under a day; the lead time was weeks. The biggest wait was between draft
and proofread, because I batched proofreading. I started proofreading the
day after drafting, and the lead time halved.

**Realistic.** A team maps its stream together on a whiteboard over two
hours. The current state shows a lead time of several weeks with a small
fraction spent working. The future state they agree on removes the manual
test hand-off by automating regression tests, replaces the weekly change
board for routine changes with automated checks, and creates
self-service environments. They do not try to get there at once; the map
gives them the order: tests first, environments second, approvals third.

### Step by step

1. **Gather everyone** who supports the stream, in one room or call.
2. **Pick a typical change** and walk through it from request to
   production.
3. **Draw each step as a box**, with who does it.
4. **Estimate process time, lead time and %C/A** for each, roughly.
5. **Total them** and compute the activity ratio and rolled %C/A.
6. **Mark the biggest waits and the lowest %C/A.**
7. **Draw the future state** the team wants within a year.
8. **Choose the first improvement** from the gap between them.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to spend weeks making the map precise, or to map the
documented process instead of the real one. The fix is to draw it in hours
with rough numbers, from the people who do the work, and to treat it as a
tool for choosing improvements rather than a document to perfect.

A second mistake is to focus on process time, because it is the part people
are responsible for. The waits are usually where the time goes.

### In real systems

Teams often redraw the map every few months to see progress, and many now
measure parts of it automatically: commit-to-deploy time from the pipeline,
queue times from the ticket system. The hand-drawn map remains useful
because it captures steps tools do not see, such as waiting for a meeting.

## Build a dedicated transformation team

### In one sentence

Improvement work competes with daily work and usually loses, so the book
recommends a dedicated team, with a shared goal and short planning
horizons, that is protected from the pressures of running the business.

### The idea

Every organisation already has people doing their daily jobs: shipping
features, keeping production up, answering tickets. Asking those same
people to also transform how the work is done, in their spare time, rarely
works. Urgent daily work always wins over important improvement work, so
the improvements never get finished.

The book draws on the work of Vijay Govindarajan and Chris Trimble on
innovation inside established organisations. Their argument is that the
organisation that runs today's business, which they call the performance
engine, is built for repeatability and efficiency, and that it naturally
resists the experiments needed for change. To make change happen, they
recommend a dedicated team that works alongside the performance engine,
with its own goals and rules, and a deliberate partnership between the two.

For a DevOps transformation, the book suggests that the dedicated team:

- is assigned to the transformation, not to daily operations, so its time is protected;
- includes generalists with skills across development, operations and other areas, rather than specialists from one silo;
- has members with good, long-standing relationships across the organisation, so they can get help and be trusted;
- sits physically or logically apart from the daily work, where it can try new ways of working.

The team is not there to do the transformation *to* everyone else. It is
there to change the chosen value stream with the people who run it, build
the first tools and practices, and help spread them.

#### Agree on a shared goal

The dedicated team needs one clear, measurable goal, agreed with leadership,
with a deadline. The book suggests goals in terms of the stream's outcomes,
for example reducing the share of budget spent on maintenance, reducing
lead time from commit to production, or making a certain kind of release
possible without downtime. Goals should be within reach, typically in a
time frame of months to a year, and clear enough that everyone, inside and
outside the team, can tell whether they have been met.

A shared goal does two jobs. It focuses the team: when there are many
possible improvements, the goal decides which come first. And it aligns the
other groups in the stream: if the goal is "lead time from commit to
production under one day", then operations, security and QA all have a
reason to help remove their waits, because they share the target.

#### Keep improvement planning horizons short

The book recommends planning improvement work in short cycles, a few weeks
at a time, rather than in multi-year programmes. Short horizons bring the
benefits of small batches from chapter 2 to the transformation itself:
faster feedback on whether an improvement works, less wasted effort when
it does not, the ability to change direction as the team learns, and
visible progress that sustains support. A transformation planned as one
large project, delivered at the end, has all the problems of a large batch.

#### Choosing the people

The book's emphasis on generalists and relationships deserves a moment.
Generalists matter because the problems in a value stream sit between
specialities: a slow deploy may involve build scripts, infrastructure, test
data and an approval process. A team of people who each know only one of
those areas spends its time handing problems to each other, which recreates
the very silos the transformation is trying to remove.

Relationships matter because the dedicated team needs help from people who
do not report to it: an operations engineer who can open a firewall, a
security reviewer who can agree a new approach, a manager who can protect
the stream's team during the learning dip. People with long-standing,
trusted relationships get that help quickly. A team of newcomers, however
skilled, has to earn it first, which costs months.

Finally, the team should include people who are respected for delivering
results, not only for ideas. Their involvement tells the rest of the
organisation that the transformation is serious, and their judgement keeps
the team's improvements practical.

### Worked example

**Simple.** When I tried to improve my build setup "whenever I had time",
it never happened, because there was always a post to write. When I
blocked one evening a week only for build improvements, with one goal
(publish in under five minutes from commit), it happened in a month.

**Realistic.** A company forms a small transformation team: two
developers, an operations engineer, a tester, all known and trusted across
the organisation. Leadership agrees one goal: lead time for the checkout
stream from several weeks to under two days within nine months. The team
works in two-week cycles, each delivering one or two improvements with the
checkout team. Every cycle ends with a demo and the current lead time.
When an outage pulls other engineers into firefighting, the transformation
team keeps going.

### Step by step

1. **Choose a small team of generalists** with good relationships across
   the organisation.
2. **Assign them full time** to the transformation, with leadership's
   backing.
3. **Agree one measurable goal** and a deadline with leadership.
4. **Plan in short cycles** of a few weeks.
5. **Work with the stream's own team**, not around it.
6. **Show progress at the end of every cycle**, in the goal's measure.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is a part-time transformation: a few people with "20
percent of their time" for improvement, which disappears at the first
crisis. The fix is protected, dedicated people.

A second mistake is a dedicated team that works in isolation, builds tools
nobody uses, and is resented as an ivory tower. The fix is to work inside
the chosen stream, with its people, on its real problems.

### In real systems

Dedicated teams often evolve into platform or enablement teams: once the
first stream is improved, they keep building shared tools and coaching
other teams. The book's later chapters, especially chapter 8 and chapter
20, return to this shape.

## Reserve capacity for non-functional requirements and technical debt

### In one sentence

Technical debt grows unless time is set aside to pay it down, so a fixed
share of every cycle should be reserved for non-functional work and debt
reduction rather than hoping for a quiet period that never comes.

### The idea

**Technical debt** is the accumulated cost of shortcuts and decisions that
make future change harder: missing tests, duplicated code, outdated
dependencies, fragile scripts, manual steps. Like financial debt, it
charges interest. Each change takes a little longer and is a little riskier
because of it. Left alone, the interest grows until most effort goes into
working around the debt rather than delivering value.

**Non-functional requirements** are the qualities a system needs beyond its
features: reliability, performance, security, operability, testability,
deployability. They are often neglected because they are invisible to
users until they fail.

The book's recommendation is simple and firm: reserve a fixed share of
each development cycle for this work. A common guideline, which the book
cites from product management practice, is around a fifth of capacity. The
exact number matters less than the principle that it is reserved, every
cycle, rather than negotiated against features each time. When it is
negotiated, features always win, because their benefit is visible and the
debt's cost is not.

The book links this directly to the Third Way from chapter 4: improvement
of daily work is more important than daily work itself. Reserved capacity is
the mechanism that makes that true.

#### What counts as debt work

Debt work is anything that makes future change easier, faster or safer
without adding a feature. Examples: adding tests around fragile code,
removing duplication, updating dependencies, automating a manual deploy
step, improving logging and monitoring, fixing flaky tests, deleting dead
code, writing the runbook nobody has written. Choosing what to do first is
easiest with the value stream map: work that shortens the biggest wait or
raises the lowest %C/A comes first.

#### How the interest compounds

It helps to think about why debt grows rather than staying flat. A shortcut
taken today makes the next change slightly harder. Under deadline pressure,
that next change is also done with a shortcut, because the right way is now
harder still. So debt creates the conditions for more debt. Reserving
capacity breaks the loop: each cycle, some of the oldest and most costly
debt is removed, so the next change starts from a slightly better place.

### Worked example

**Simple.** Every fourth study session I spend on maintenance of my
projects: updating dependencies, fixing warnings, adding a missing test.
Before I did this, a dependency update every few months broke everything
at once and cost a weekend. Now each update is small and boring.

**Realistic.** A team plans two-week sprints. It agrees that one day per
developer per sprint is reserved for debt and non-functional work, chosen
by the team from a visible debt backlog ordered by impact on the value
stream map. In the first months, the team automates its deploy and fixes
the flakiest tests. Over the following year, features take less time,
because the friction they used to meet is gone.

### Step by step

1. **Make a debt backlog**: every known shortcut, missing test, manual step
   and fragile script, visible to everyone.
2. **Order it** by impact on the stream: biggest waits and rework first.
3. **Agree a fixed share** of every cycle with the product owner and
   leadership.
4. **Protect it** from negotiation: it is reserved, like a tax.
5. **Show the results**, for example the fall in deploy time or flaky tests.
6. **Revisit the share** if debt is still growing.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to plan a "technical debt sprint" once a year, or to
wait for a quiet period. The quiet period never comes, and a one-off sprint
cannot keep up with debt added every week. The fix is a fixed, continuous
share.

A second mistake is to hide debt work inside feature estimates. It then gets
cut whenever a deadline tightens, and the product owner never sees what it
achieves. The fix is to make it visible and reserved.

### In real systems

Many teams reserve a fixed percentage of each sprint, or rotate a "health"
role through the team. Some organisations use error budgets from site
reliability engineering for the same purpose: when reliability falls below
target, feature work pauses and reliability work takes over.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why is negotiating debt work against features every cycle a problem?</summary>

Features have visible benefits and debt's cost is invisible, so features
win every negotiation and debt keeps growing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. How does the value stream map help choose debt work?</summary>

It shows the biggest waits and the lowest %C/A, so debt work that shortens
those steps has the most effect on the stream.

</details>

## Increase the visibility of work

### In one sentence

Work that cannot be seen cannot be managed, so all the work in the stream,
features, operations, debt and improvements, should be visible to everyone
in shared, up-to-date places.

### The idea

In chapter 2, visible work was the first principle of flow: knowledge work
is invisible, so it piles up unseen unless it is put on a board. This
chapter applies the same idea to the transformation and to the whole value
stream. The book recommends that the progress of the transformation and
the work of the stream be visible in near real time, so everyone can see
the current state, what is being worked on, and where it is stuck.

Three things in particular need to be visible.

**All kinds of work.** Features are usually tracked. Operations work,
unplanned work, debt work and improvement work often are not. When they
are invisible, they compete for the same people without anyone seeing the
trade-off, and the product owner wonders why features are slow.

**The measures of the stream.** Lead time, deploy frequency, change failure
rate and time to restore, ideally updated automatically and shown where the
team works. When the measures are visible, progress toward the shared goal
is obvious, and so is backsliding.

**The transformation's progress.** Which improvements are planned, in
progress and done, and what effect each one had. That sustains support
and helps other teams learn.

#### A shared board across development and operations

The book recommends a shared work queue for development and operations, so
that work from both appears in one place and is prioritised together. When
operations has its own ticket system and development its own backlog, each
side's work is invisible to the other. An environment request that blocks
a feature sits in an operations queue the developer cannot see; an
operability fix that operations needs sits in a development backlog nobody
prioritises. One shared view makes the dependencies obvious and lets the
product owner and value stream manager balance them.

`} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I added two labels to my project board: "maintenance" and
"improvement". Within a month I could see that a third of my project time
went to maintenance I had never planned. That explained why my feature
estimates were always wrong, and made the case for reserving time for it.

**Realistic.** A team puts operations tickets and development stories on
one board for the checkout stream, with a work in progress limit. On a
screen by the team's area, a dashboard shows lead time and deploy frequency,
updated from the pipeline. In the first week, an environment request blocked
for six days is visible to everyone, and the operations engineer and
developer resolve it in an hour once they see it.

### Step by step

1. **Put every type of work** on one board for the stream, with labels for
   type.
2. **Add a blocked column** and make blocked items impossible to ignore.
3. **Set work in progress limits** for the in-progress column.
4. **Show the stream's measures** where the team works, updated
   automatically.
5. **Track the transformation's improvements** on the same board or one
   next to it.
6. **Review the mix of work** regularly: is unplanned work rising?

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to make only planned feature work visible. The
unplanned and operations work then consumes capacity invisibly, and the
team looks slow for reasons nobody can see. The fix is one board with all
types of work.

### In real systems

Teams commonly use one tracker with labels or swimlanes for work type, and
dashboards driven by pipeline and incident data. The specific tool matters
less than having one place everyone looks at.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why should operations and development share one board?</summary>

So each side's work, and the dependencies between them, are visible and
prioritised together, instead of hiding in separate queues.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What three things does the book want visible?</summary>

All kinds of work, the measures of the stream, and the progress of the
transformation.

</details>

## Use tools to reinforce desired behaviour

### In one sentence

Tools shape how people work, so shared tools, a common backlog, shared
chat rooms and shared automation should be chosen to reinforce the
collaboration and visibility the transformation wants.

### The idea

The book points out that culture and tools influence each other. If
development and operations use different tools for tracking work,
different chat channels and different deployment scripts, the tools keep
them apart even if everyone agrees they should collaborate. If they share
tools, the tools draw them together.

The book highlights several kinds of shared tooling:

- **A shared work backlog**, as in the previous section, so work from both sides is prioritised together.
- **Shared chat rooms**, where conversations about a service happen in the open. The book discusses ChatOps: running automation from a shared chat room, so that a deploy, a test run or a diagnostic command is visible to everyone in the room, with its result. That makes work transparent, spreads knowledge as people watch each other's commands, and creates a searchable history.
- **Shared automation**: the same deployment and environment scripts used by developers and operations, so knowledge is in code rather than in one team's heads.

The underlying principle is that the desired behaviour should be the easiest
behaviour. If posting in a private channel is easier than the shared one,
people use the private one. If running a deploy from chat shows the whole
team what happened, people learn from each other without trying.

  "Chat bot runs the automation",
  "Result posted back to the room",
  "Teammates see the command and output",
  "History is searchable later",

### Worked example

**Simple.** I set up a small notification so that every successful or
failed deploy of my blog posts a message to a channel I read. Before, a
failed deploy could go unnoticed for days. Now I see it within minutes,
and the history shows when each change went live.

**Realistic.** A company sets up a chat room per service. Deploys,
rollbacks and common diagnostics run through a chat bot in that room. When
an incident happens, the room shows exactly which commands were run, by
whom, and what they returned. New team members learn the operational
commands by watching. After a few months, the post-incident reviews use
the chat history as their timeline.

### Step by step

1. **List the tools** each group in the stream uses for work tracking,
   chat and automation.
2. **Find where tools separate people**: separate trackers, private
   channels, duplicated scripts.
3. **Consolidate** onto shared tools for the stream.
4. **Run common actions through shared automation**, ideally visible in a
   shared room.
5. **Make the shared path the easiest one.**
6. **Use the history** in reviews and onboarding.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to expect culture change from tools alone: buy a
chat tool and a shared tracker, and expect collaboration to follow. Tools
reinforce behaviour; they do not create it. The fix is to pair the tools
with the shared goal, the shared board and the dedicated team from earlier
sections.

A second mistake is the opposite: to ignore tools, and keep separate ones
for each team while asking people to collaborate. The tools then work
against the intent every day.

### In real systems

ChatOps is common in operations teams, with bots that deploy, scale and
query services from chat. Even without bots, a shared channel per service
with automated notifications from the pipeline and monitoring achieves much
of the same transparency.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. What is ChatOps, and why does it help?</summary>

Running automation from a shared chat room so commands and results are
visible to everyone. It makes work transparent, spreads knowledge and
leaves a searchable history.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why can tools alone not change culture?</summary>

Tools reinforce behaviour that people already intend; without a shared goal
and practices, shared tools are used in the old, separate ways.

</details>

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Understanding the Work in Our Value Stream">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the lesson is to draw the work. Take one change and
trace every step from request to release: who does it, how long it is
worked on, and how long it waits. Most people are surprised by how much of
the elapsed time is waiting. A map drawn in an hour is enough to see where
the time goes, and that is the starting point for every later improvement.

For my own projects, the map is short, but the same surprises appear: the
draft that waits a week for proofreading, the dependency update that waits
until it breaks something.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I add numbers to each step: process time, lead
time and percent complete and accurate. From them I calculate total lead
time, the activity ratio and the rolled %C/A, and I can explain why small
quality problems at each step compound into a large one overall. I can
read the map to choose the first improvement: the biggest wait, the lowest
%C/A, or a step with a long wait and almost no rejections.

I also know who to involve: every role a change passes through, including
operations, security and whoever approves changes.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I organise the improvement, not just the map. A
dedicated team of trusted generalists, protected from daily work, with one
measurable goal and short planning cycles. A fixed share of every cycle
reserved for debt and non-functional work, chosen from a visible backlog by
impact on the map. One board for all kinds of work, the stream's measures
on display, and shared tools that make collaboration the easy path. I know
these pieces reinforce each other: the map chooses the work, the team and
reserved capacity do it, visibility shows its effect.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases include: a stream where one role, often security or a change
board, refuses to take part in mapping, so their steps are estimated from
outside and the estimate becomes the reason to invite them again; a
stream with several very different paths, such as hotfixes versus features,
which needs a map per path; work that loops back many times, where the
%C/A of the step that sends it back matters more than any wait; and a
stream with heavy dependence on a vendor, whose steps cannot be changed
directly and must be designed around.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases include: a map that makes one team look bad, which then
disputes the numbers, so the session must be clearly blameless and about
the system; reserved debt capacity that the team spends on interesting
rewrites rather than on the biggest waits, which needs the map to keep it
honest; a dedicated team that becomes a bottleneck itself because every
change now needs its help; and visible dashboards that are gamed, for
example deploy frequency raised by splitting deploys without reducing
lead time.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

At production level, the map is partly automated. Commit-to-deploy time,
deploy frequency, change failure rate and time to restore come from the
pipeline and incident tools, and appear on a dashboard the team sees every
day. The hand-drawn map is redrawn every few months to catch the steps
tools cannot see, such as meetings and approvals. The debt backlog is
visible and reserved capacity is tracked, so leadership can see what it
buys. The transformation team reports progress against its goal at the
end of every cycle.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is that the problem is almost always in the waits and the
hand-offs, not in how hard people work. A map is a tool for seeing the
system, not for judging people. I treat improvement as real work that
deserves protected time, not as something done when there is nothing more
urgent. And I trust what I can see: if work, debt or progress is
invisible, I assume it is being traded off silently.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design terms, the map shows where the architecture forces
hand-offs. A step that waits for another team often exists because the
system is coupled across team boundaries: a shared database, a shared
environment, a single release. The map therefore feeds directly into the
next chapter's question of how team structure and architecture should
match. The aim of the future-state map is usually a stream where one team
can take a change from commit to production with few hand-offs.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategy is to make the stream visible, organise around improving it,
and keep it visible as it improves. Map the current state with everyone
involved; agree a future state and one measurable goal; give a dedicated
team protected time and short cycles; reserve a fixed share of capacity for
debt; put all work and the stream's measures where everyone can see them;
and choose shared tools that make collaboration the default. Then redraw
the map regularly and let it choose the next improvements.

Done when the stream has a current-state map with numbers, a future state
and goal agreed with leadership, a dedicated team, reserved capacity for
debt, and all work and measures visible on shared tools.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>How accurate do the numbers on a value stream map need to be?</summary>

Rough is fine. The map's purpose is to show where the biggest waits and the
most rework are, and those are usually obvious even with estimates. Spend
hours, not weeks.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Who should draw the map?</summary>

The people who do the work, from every role a change passes through,
together. A map drawn by managers alone tends to show the documented
process and miss invisible work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between lead time and process time again?</summary>

Process time is how long someone actually works on the item at a step.
Lead time is how long it spends at the step in total, including waiting.
The difference is the wait.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does a dedicated transformation team mean everyone else is excluded?</summary>

No. The team works with the chosen stream's people on their real problems.
Its dedication protects the improvement work from daily pressure; it does
not replace the stream's own team.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if the product owner will not agree to reserve capacity for debt?</summary>

Show the map and the debt's cost: how much time goes to workarounds,
failures and slow deploys. Start with a small reserved share and show
what it buys, then grow it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is a shared chat room really important, or just a nice extra?</summary>

It matters because it makes work transparent by default. Commands,
results and discussions happen where everyone can see and learn, and the
history supports reviews and onboarding.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I map a value stream when I work alone?</summary>

Yes. Separate the roles I play, trace one recent change, and estimate the
times. The map shows where my own work waits, which is often just as
surprising.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How often should the map be redrawn?</summary>

Every few months, or after a significant improvement, so the team can see
the effect and choose the next target.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Identify everyone who supports the stream, including operations, security and approvers; the longest waits are often in their queues.
- A value stream map records process time, lead time and %C/A for each step; draw it quickly, with the people who do the work.
- Most of a change's elapsed time is usually waiting; improving process time alone barely moves lead time.
- Rolled %C/A multiplies across steps, so small quality problems compound into a large one.
- A dedicated transformation team of trusted generalists, with one measurable goal and short planning cycles, protects improvement from daily pressure.
- Reserve a fixed share of every cycle for non-functional work and technical debt, rather than negotiating it against features.
- Make all work, the stream's measures and the transformation's progress visible on shared tools.
- Choose shared tools, backlogs, chat rooms and automation, so that collaboration is the easiest behaviour.

## Glossary

- **Value stream map**: a diagram of the steps from request to delivered value, with time and quality numbers for each.
- **Process time**: how long work is actively being done at a step.
- **Lead time**: total time an item spends at a step, or in the whole stream, including waiting.
- **Percent complete and accurate (%C/A)**: the share of a step's output the next step can use without correction.
- **Rolled %C/A**: the product of every step's %C/A; the share of items that pass through the whole stream with no rework.
- **Activity ratio**: total process time divided by total lead time; how much of the elapsed time is spent working.
- **Current state and future state**: the map of the stream as it is, and as the team intends it to be after improvement.
- **Value stream manager**: the person accountable for the stream meeting its goals end to end.
- **Dedicated transformation team**: a protected team assigned to improving the stream, working alongside the daily organisation.
- **Performance engine**: Govindarajan and Trimble's term for the part of an organisation that runs today's business efficiently.
- **Technical debt**: the accumulated cost of shortcuts that make future change harder.
- **Non-functional requirements**: qualities such as reliability, security, performance and operability.
- **ChatOps**: running automation from a shared chat room, so commands and results are visible to everyone.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up.** Pick one recent change in a project of mine and list every
step it went through, with who did it.

**Build.** Add process time, lead time and %C/A estimates to each step.
Adapt the script above to my numbers and run it. Which step has the
biggest wait, and which has the lowest %C/A?

**Stretch.** Draw a future-state map I could reach in three months, write
one measurable goal for it, list the first three improvements in order,
and decide what share of my project time to reserve for debt work.

### Self-check

<details>
<summary>1. A stream's steps have %C/A of 90, 80 and 70. What is the rolled %C/A?</summary>

0.9 times 0.8 times 0.7, which is about 0.50, so about half of items pass
through with no rework.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Process time is 10 hours and lead time is 200 hours. What does that tell me?</summary>

The activity ratio is 5 percent: the item is worked on for a twentieth of
the elapsed time. Removing waits will help far more than working faster.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Name two properties of a good dedicated transformation team.</summary>

Any two of: protected full-time assignment, generalist skills, trusted
relationships across the organisation, one measurable goal, short planning
cycles.

</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Why reserve debt capacity rather than plan a debt sprint once a year?</summary>

Debt is added continuously, so it must be paid continuously; a yearly
sprint cannot keep up, and the quiet period it waits for never comes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>5. How do shared tools reinforce collaboration?</summary>

They put work, conversations and automation where everyone sees them, so
collaboration happens by default rather than by effort.

</details>
