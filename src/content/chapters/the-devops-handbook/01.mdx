This chapter gives the whole book its vocabulary. It borrows the idea of a
value stream from manufacturing, applies it to the path a code change takes
from an idea to running software, and shows how to measure that path with
three numbers: lead time, process time and the share of work that arrives
right first time. It ends with the Three Ways, the three principles every
later chapter turns into practice.

## Where the ideas come from

### In one sentence

DevOps is not a new invention but the meeting point of several older
movements, Lean manufacturing, Agile software development and continuous
delivery, applied to the whole path from idea to production.

### The idea

Most of the ideas in this book were first worked out in factories. Lean
manufacturing, which grew out of the Toyota Production System, asked a
simple question: how do you get a product from an order to a customer
quickly, cheaply and with high quality, all at once? Its answer was to
watch the flow of work rather than the busyness of each machine, to keep
batches small, and to stop and fix problems the moment they appear.

Software borrowed those ideas in stages. The Agile Manifesto, written in
2001, pushed teams toward small increments, working software and close
contact with the people who use it. Agile mostly changed how development
worked inside a team. It did not say much about what happened after the
code was written: testing environments, releases, operations.

Continuous delivery filled part of that gap. Jez Humble and David Farley's
book of that name, published in 2010, described the deployment pipeline: an
automated path that builds every change, tests it, and leaves it in a state
where it could be released at any time. The key move was to treat "can we
ship this?" as something the system answers continuously, not a question
for a release weekend.

The last piece was operations. In 2009, talks such as John Allspaw and Paul
Hammond's on developers and operations working together at Flickr, and the
first DevOpsDays conference organised by Patrick Debois in Ghent, gave the
movement its name. The argument was that development and operations share
one goal, working software in front of customers, and that the wall between
them was the biggest source of delay and failure left.

The handbook's claim is that these are one idea seen from different sides.
Lean gives the theory of flow. Agile brings small batches and customer
feedback into development. Continuous delivery automates the path to
production. DevOps extends all of it across every team a change touches,
including operations, security and the business.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I write a small command-line tool alone. Before I knew any of
this, I wrote features for two weeks, then spent a weekend "releasing":
fixing the build on a clean machine, writing a changelog, discovering that
two features broke each other. Agile would tell me to work in smaller
increments. Continuous delivery would tell me to build and test every
commit on a clean machine automatically. DevOps would add: the person who
runs the tool in production, even if that is also me, should see the same
signals I see while coding.

**Realistic.** A team of eight builds a web application. Development runs
two-week sprints and is proud of its velocity. Operations is a separate
group that receives a release once a quarter, with a hand-written runbook.
Every quarter the release slips, a weekend is lost, and something fails in
production that never failed in testing. Development is Agile; the
organisation is not. The handbook's view is that the sprint is not the
unit that matters. The unit that matters is the trip a change takes all the
way to customers, and on that trip the quarterly handoff dominates
everything.

### Step by step

How each movement would describe the same broken release, in order:

1. **Lean** sees inventory: three months of finished but undelivered work
   piled up before the release. Inventory hides defects and ties up value.
2. **Agile** sees a feedback gap: customers did not touch the work for
   three months, so nobody knows if it was the right work.
3. **Continuous delivery** sees a missing pipeline: nobody could say, on
   any given day, whether the main branch was releasable.
4. **DevOps** sees a wall: development and operations had different goals,
   different tools and different incentives, so the handoff was where
   everything broke.

The four diagnoses do not compete. Each one names a part of the same
system, and the book's practices address all four.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat DevOps as a tool or a job title: "we bought
a CI server, so we do DevOps", or "we hired a DevOps engineer". A tool can
automate a pipeline, and a person can build one, but neither changes who
owns the outcome or how work flows between teams. The fix is to judge any
change by what it does to the whole path: does a change now reach
customers sooner, safer and with more learning?

### In real systems

In practice the history shows up as layers that teams adopt at different
speeds. Many organisations run Agile ceremonies in development while
releases still go through a monthly change board. Others have a fast
pipeline into a test environment and a slow manual step into production.
Knowing the lineage helps me name which layer is missing instead of
blaming "process" in general.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. What did continuous delivery add that Agile on its own did not?</summary>

An automated deployment pipeline that keeps every change in a releasable
state, so "can we ship this?" is answered continuously rather than at a
release event.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why is "we hired a DevOps engineer" not the same as adopting DevOps?</summary>

DevOps is a change in how work flows and who owns outcomes across teams. One
person cannot change that alone, and a separate DevOps role can become one
more handoff.

</details>

## The manufacturing value stream

### In one sentence

A value stream is the full sequence of steps that turns a customer's
request into something delivered, and Lean teaches us to improve the
stream as a whole rather than each step on its own.

### The idea

Picture a factory that makes bicycles. A customer orders one. The order is
recorded, parts are pulled from stock, frames are welded, painted, dried,
assembled, inspected, packed and shipped. Every one of those steps, plus
every wait in between, is the **value stream**: the path from the
customer's request to the customer holding the bicycle.

The key observation in Lean is that most of the time in a value stream is
spent waiting, not working. A frame might take an hour to weld and an hour
to paint, but sit for days between the two in a pile of other frames. The
customer does not care how fast the welder is. They care how long they
wait.

That changes what "improving" means. A manager who measures each machine's
utilisation will push every machine to run all the time, which produces
piles of half-finished frames. A Lean manager measures how long an order
takes to get through, and will happily let a machine sit idle if running it
would only add to a pile.

Lean factories found that the way to shorten the trip was to keep batches
small, limit the work allowed between steps, and stop the whole line when a
defect appeared, so it was fixed before more bad parts were made. These
ideas come back in chapters 2 and 3 as the principles of flow and
feedback. Here they set up the analogy.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A bakery bakes bread in batches of a hundred loaves. Each batch
mixes for an hour, proves for two hours and bakes for one. A customer who
orders at the wrong moment waits for the current batch to finish, then for
theirs. If the bakery bakes in batches of ten, each batch is quicker, and
the average customer waits less, even though the oven is busy for the same
total time.

**Realistic.** A furniture workshop measures its value stream for one
table. Cutting takes two hours, finishing three, assembly one, inspection
half an hour. But the table spends four days waiting for the finishing room
and two days waiting for inspection. The total work is six and a half
hours; the total trip is over six days. Buying a faster saw would save
minutes. Fixing the queue for the finishing room would save days.

<Figure caption="One table's trip through the workshop. The boxes are work, the gaps are waiting. Look at how much of the line is gaps." art={`
 order                                                          delivered
   |                                                                |
   [cut 2h]......4 days......[finish 3h]....2 days....[assemble 1h][inspect .5h]
   ^ work                     ^ work                  ^ work        ^ work

 work: 6.5 hours            trip: more than 6 days
`} />

### Step by step

How a Lean team maps a value stream, which the handbook asks us to copy:

1. **Pick one product or request type.** Not "everything the factory
   makes", but one kind of table.
2. **Walk the path physically.** Follow one order from request to delivery.
   Do not trust the process document; watch what happens.
3. **Record each step's work time.** How long someone is actively working.
4. **Record each wait.** How long the item sits between steps.
5. **Record quality at each step.** How often the next step has to send it
   back.
6. **Add it up.** Total work time, total trip time, and where the biggest
   waits and the most rework are.
7. **Pick the biggest wait or the worst quality step and fix that first.**

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to optimise a step locally: make the fastest machine
faster, or keep every worker at full utilisation. Both feel productive and
both make the trip longer, because they create piles in front of the next
step. The fix is to measure the trip, find the step where work piles up,
and improve that one, even if it means other steps sit idle for a while.

### In real systems

The same pattern shows up in any software organisation with specialised
teams. The development team finishes stories quickly, the test team has a
backlog of weeks, and the operations team batches deploys monthly. Each team
looks efficient on its own report. A change still takes months to reach
customers, because nobody owns the trip.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A machine is idle half the day. Is that waste?</summary>

Not necessarily. If running it would only add to a pile in front of a slower
step, idle is better. What matters is the trip time of the whole stream.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why walk the path rather than read the process document?</summary>

The document describes the intended process. The waits, rework and
workarounds that make up most of the trip time only show up when you
follow a real item.

</details>

## The technology value stream

### In one sentence

In software, the value stream is the path from a business idea, through
design, code, test and release, to a change running in production and
helping a customer.

### The idea

The handbook defines the technology value stream as the process of turning
a business hypothesis into a technology-enabled service that delivers
value to the customer. The words are chosen carefully. A **hypothesis** is
an idea that might be right: "customers will buy more if checkout has one
fewer page". The **service** is the running software that tests it. And
**value** only happens when a customer uses it.

The stream has two halves that behave very differently. The first half is
design and development: understanding the idea, designing it and writing
the code. That work is creative and uncertain, a bit like product design in
a factory. Nobody knows exactly how long it will take, and trying to make it
perfectly predictable usually makes it worse.

The second half runs from a change being committed to version control to
it running in production: build, test, deploy, operate. This half should
behave like manufacturing: predictable, repeatable, automated, with low
variation. A change that has been written should go through it the same way
every time, quickly and safely.

The book focuses mostly on that second half, because it is where
organisations lose the most time and where engineering practices help
most. Chapter 9 onwards builds it out in detail: environments on demand,
automated tests, continuous integration and low-risk releases.

<Flow caption="The technology value stream. The first half (idea to commit) is creative and variable; the second half (commit to customer) should be fast, automated and predictable." steps={[
  "Business hypothesis",
  "Design and development",
  "Commit to version control",
  "Build and automated test",
  "Deploy to production",
  "Running for customers",
  "Feedback on the hypothesis",
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** My own blog. The idea is "add a reading-time estimate to each
note". I design it, write a function, commit it, the site builds, the
build runs checks, the site deploys to a static host, and a reader sees the
estimate. The first half took an hour of thinking and coding. The second
half took a few minutes and needed nothing from me after the push.

**Realistic.** An online shop wants to test whether showing delivery dates
on product pages raises conversion. Product writes the hypothesis.
Engineering designs it, writes code against two internal services and adds
tests. The change is committed. Then: a build, a test suite, a staging
deploy, a security scan, a change approval, a production deploy behind a
feature flag, a slow rollout, and an analytics dashboard to judge the
result. Every one of those steps is part of the value stream, and every
wait between them adds to how long the business waits to learn whether the
idea was any good.

### Step by step

Mapping the technology value stream for one kind of change:

1. **Choose the change type.** For example, "a small feature on the web
   app", not "all work".
2. **List every step from idea to production**, including approvals and
   handoffs between teams, not just the technical ones.
3. **Mark the commit.** Everything after it is the part the book wants to
   make predictable.
4. **For each step after the commit, ask: is it automated, manual, or
   waiting on a person?**
5. **For each manual or waiting step, record who owns it and how long it
   usually takes.**
6. **Mark the step where changes pile up.** That is the first candidate
   for improvement.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat the stream as ending at "merged" or "done in
the sprint". A change that is merged but not deployed has delivered no
value, and nobody has learned whether the hypothesis was right. The fix is
to define "done" as running in production, and to measure the stream to
that point.

### In real systems

Most teams' tooling tracks the first half well: tickets, branches, pull
requests. The second half is often spread across a CI system, a deploy
tool, a change-management system and a monitoring tool that nobody
connects. Getting a single view of a change from commit to production is
often the first concrete DevOps project a team takes on.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why does the book treat the two halves of the stream differently?</summary>

Design and development is creative and uncertain, so some variation is
natural. Build, test and deploy should be the same every time, so the goal
there is speed, automation and low variation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Is a merged pull request "done"?</summary>

Not in value-stream terms. It is done when it runs in production for
customers, because only then does it deliver value or produce feedback.

</details>

## Lead time and process time

### In one sentence

Lead time is the clock time from when a piece of work is requested to when
it is done; process time is only the part of that time when someone is
actually working on it.

### The idea

Two clocks run on every piece of work. The first starts when someone asks
for it, a ticket is created or a customer request arrives, and stops when
the work is delivered. That is the **lead time**, and it is what the
customer experiences. The second clock only runs while somebody is working
on the item. That is the **process time**, sometimes called touch time or
work time.

The gap between the two is waiting: in a queue for review, for a test
environment, for an approval, for the next release train. In most
organisations the gap is large. Work spends far more time waiting than
being worked on, which means the fastest way to shorten lead time is
usually to remove waits, not to make people work faster.

A useful derived number is **flow efficiency**: process time divided by
lead time. If a change needs thirteen hours of work and takes eleven days
to arrive, its flow efficiency is about five percent. That number tells me
where to look. With low flow efficiency, coding faster barely matters; the
queues are the problem.

The handbook singles out one segment of lead time as the one to watch: the
time from a change being committed to it running in production. It calls
this the deployment lead time. It is the part of the stream engineering can
make predictable, and the part where most organisations lose the most time.

<Compare left="Lead time" right="Process time" caption="The two clocks on one piece of work. Customers feel the left column; teams usually only see the right." rows={[
  ["Starts when the work is requested", "Starts when someone starts working on it"],
  ["Stops when it is delivered", "Stops when that person stops"],
  ["Includes every queue and wait", "Excludes waiting entirely"],
  ["What the customer experiences", "What the worker experiences"],
  ["Shortened mostly by removing waits", "Shortened by better tools or skill"],
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I fix a typo on my blog. I notice it at 9:00, fix it at 9:02,
push at 9:03, and the site is live at 9:08. Lead time from noticing to
live: eight minutes. Process time: about three minutes. Flow efficiency is
high because nothing waited on a person.

**Realistic.** A change on a team web app goes through five steps. I
recorded the work and wait for each from the ticket history, then
computed the totals with a short script:

```js
const steps = [
  { name: "plan", waitH: 40, workH: 2 },
  { name: "code", waitH: 8, workH: 6 },
  { name: "review", waitH: 30, workH: 1 },
  { name: "test", waitH: 50, workH: 3 },
  { name: "deploy", waitH: 120, workH: 1 },
];
const work = steps.reduce((s, x) => s + x.workH, 0);
const lead = steps.reduce((s, x) => s + x.waitH + x.workH, 0);
console.log(`process time: ${work} h`);
console.log(`lead time:    ${lead} h`);
console.log(`flow efficiency: ${(100 * work / lead).toFixed(1)}%`);
const worst = steps.reduce((a, b) => (b.waitH > a.waitH ? b : a));
console.log(`longest wait: ${worst.name} (${worst.waitH} h)`);
```

Running it with Node prints:

```text
process time: 13 h
lead time:    261 h
flow efficiency: 5.0%
longest wait: deploy (120 h)
```

Thirteen hours of work took eleven days to reach customers. The single
biggest wait is the deploy queue, five days, which is more than all the
work combined many times over. That is where the first improvement goes.

### Step by step

Measuring lead time and process time from real data:

<Trace columns={["Step", "Wait (h)", "Work (h)", "Running lead time (h)", "Running process time (h)"]} rows={[
  ["plan", "40", "2", "42", "2"],
  ["code", "8", "6", "56", "8"],
  ["review", "30", "1", "87", "9"],
  ["test", "50", "3", "140", "12"],
  ["deploy", "120", "1", "261", "13"],
]} caption="The example change, step by step. Watch the two right-hand columns drift apart: lead time grows by the wait plus the work, process time only by the work." />

1. **Pick one completed item** and find its request time and delivery time.
   That difference is the lead time.
2. **Find every state change in between**: created, started, in review,
   approved, deployed. Ticket systems and version control record these.
3. **For each step, split elapsed time into work and wait.** Where the tool
   cannot tell you, ask the person; rough numbers are fine.
4. **Sum the work.** That is process time.
5. **Divide process time by lead time** to get flow efficiency.
6. **Repeat for ten items**, because one item can be unusual. Look at the
   median and the worst case, not the average alone.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to measure only process time, because it is what
people can see on their own desk: "the fix only took two hours". The
customer waited eleven days. The fix is to always report both numbers, and
to start every improvement conversation from lead time.

A second mistake is to start the lead-time clock at "work started" rather
than "work requested". That hides the biggest queue of all, the backlog
before anyone picks the item up.

### In real systems

The research behind the book, carried on in the State of DevOps reports and
the book *Accelerate*, uses lead time for changes, measured from commit to
production, as one of four key delivery measures, alongside deployment
frequency, change failure rate and time to restore service. Many teams now
compute it automatically from version-control and deploy timestamps, which
is cheap and removes arguments about what the number is.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. A change took 3 hours of work and 6 days to deliver. Where should you look first?</summary>

At the waits, not the work. Six days of lead time against three hours of
process time means queues dominate; speeding up the work would barely
change the delivery date.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Why look at ten items rather than one?</summary>

One item may have hit an unusual delay. Several items show the typical wait
and the worst case, which is what customers actually live with.

</details>

## Deployment lead time: months versus minutes

### In one sentence

In many organisations a committed change takes weeks or months to reach
production because of manual, risky releases; the ideal the book aims for
is minutes, through automation and small batches.

### The idea

The book describes a common scenario. Development writes code for weeks or
months against a version of the system that is not like production. When
the release finally comes, all the changes are merged at once, the merge
conflicts and integration bugs pile up, testing finds defects that are
expensive to fix because their cause is buried among hundreds of changes,
and deployment is a long, manual, error-prone event. Operations dreads it.
Customers wait.

The problem feeds itself. Because releases are painful, teams do them
rarely. Because they are rare, each one is bigger. Because each one is
bigger, it is riskier and more painful. The longer the deployment lead
time, the larger and more dangerous every release becomes.

The ideal the book describes is the opposite loop. Developers get fast,
constant feedback because their code is integrated, tested and deployable
all the time. Each change is small. Deployment is a routine, automated,
low-risk event that can happen at any time, often many times a day. When
something goes wrong, the change is small enough that its cause is obvious
and the fix is quick.

The point of contrast is not that every company must deploy constantly. It
is that the ability to deploy quickly and safely, on demand, changes what
the organisation can do: run experiments, fix problems the same day, and
release when the business wants rather than when the release calendar
allows.

<Compare left="Deployment lead time of months" right="Deployment lead time of minutes" caption="The two loops. The left one gets worse with every cycle; the right one gets better." rows={[
  ["Big batches merged late", "Small changes merged daily"],
  ["Test environments unlike production", "Environments built the same way every time"],
  ["Defects found late, cause buried", "Defects found within minutes, cause obvious"],
  ["Manual deploy over a weekend", "Automated deploy at any time"],
  ["Fear makes releases rarer and bigger", "Routine makes releases smaller and safer"],
  ["Recovery is slow and dramatic", "Rolling back or forward is quick"],
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I keep a side project's changes on a long-lived branch for a
month, then merge. The merge takes an afternoon, two features conflict, and
I find a bug that could have come from any of forty commits. If I had
merged and deployed each change as I made it, each would have conflicted
with at most a day's work, and a bug would have pointed at one small
commit.

**Realistic.** A team ships a mobile backend quarterly. Each release has
several hundred changes. In the week after each release, the on-call
engineer handles a spike of incidents, and nobody can tell quickly which
change caused which incident, so the team rolls back the whole release,
delaying every good change too. The team moves to a pipeline that deploys
each merged change within the hour. Incidents still happen, but each one
maps to a change of a few dozen lines, which can be reverted alone.

### Step by step

Why batch size and risk move together, walked through:

1. Suppose each change has a small, independent chance of containing a
   defect that reaches production.
2. A release with one change has roughly that small chance of containing a
   defect, and if it does, the culprit is obvious.
3. A release with a hundred changes is very likely to contain at least one
   defect, because the chances add up across changes.
4. When that release fails, the team has to search a hundred changes for
   the cause, which is slow.
5. While they search, every good change in the batch is held back or rolled
   back too.
6. So big batches raise both the chance of a bad release and the cost of
   each one. Small batches lower both.

<Flow caption="The loop that long deployment lead times create. Follow the arrow back to the start: each pass makes the next release bigger." steps={[
  "Releases are painful",
  "So releases are rare",
  "So each release is big",
  "So each release is risky",
  "So releases are more painful",
]} />

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to respond to painful releases by adding more
process: longer freezes, more sign-offs, bigger test phases. That lengthens
the lead time and makes the next release bigger, which feeds the loop. The
fix is the counter-intuitive one: release more often, in smaller pieces,
with more automation, until releasing is boring.

### In real systems

Static sites, web services and internal tools can usually deploy many times
a day. Mobile apps and embedded firmware face store reviews and device
updates, so they separate deploying the backend from releasing the client,
and use server-side feature flags to change behaviour without a new build.
The principle still holds: shorten the path wherever the platform allows,
and make each step routine.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. Why does adding a longer test phase often make releases riskier?</summary>

It lengthens deployment lead time, so more changes pile up in each release.
Bigger releases are more likely to contain a defect and harder to debug when
one appears.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A mobile app cannot ship to its store many times a day. Does the chapter still apply?</summary>

Yes. The backend can still deploy often, and feature flags can change the
app's behaviour without a store release. The goal is the shortest safe path
the platform allows, not a fixed deploy count.

</details>

## Percent complete and accurate

### In one sentence

Percent complete and accurate, written %C/A, is the share of work a step
receives that it can use as is, without correcting, adding missing
information or sending it back.

### The idea

Lead time tells me how long work takes. It does not tell me how good the
work is when it arrives. A step can hand off quickly and still hand off
work that the next step has to fix, clarify or bounce back. That hidden
rework is invisible in lead time until it shows up as delays later.

The measure the handbook borrows for this, from value stream mapping
practice, is **percent complete and accurate**. For each step, ask the
people in the *next* step: of the work you get from the step before you,
what share can you use as it is? If a test team says that a third of the
builds it receives cannot be tested without going back to development,
the development step has a %C/A of about two thirds, as judged by its
customer.

Two details make the measure honest. First, it is judged by the
downstream step, not by the step itself, because the people who receive
the work are the ones who feel its defects. Second, it compounds. If each
of five steps passes on work that is right first time ninety percent of the
time, the chance that a piece of work flows through all five with no
rework is the product of the five numbers, well under ninety percent.

So %C/A tells me where quality is lost, and the rolled-up product tells me
how much rework the whole stream carries. A step with low %C/A upstream
poisons everything downstream, because every later step inherits the
problem.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I write notes for my blog, then a checker script validates the
links. If one note in five fails the checker and has to be reopened, my
writing step has a %C/A of about eighty percent from the checker's point
of view. The fix is not a faster checker. It is checking links while I
write.

**Realistic.** A team estimates %C/A at each step by asking the next step
and rolls it up with a short script:

```js
const pca = { plan: 0.8, code: 0.9, review: 0.95, test: 0.7, deploy: 0.9 };
let rolled = 1;
for (const [step, p] of Object.entries(pca)) {
  rolled *= p;
  console.log(`${step.padEnd(7)} %C/A ${(p * 100).toFixed(0)}%  rolled ${(rolled * 100).toFixed(1)}%`);
}
```

Output:

```text
plan    %C/A 80%  rolled 80.0%
code    %C/A 90%  rolled 72.0%
review  %C/A 95%  rolled 68.4%
test    %C/A 70%  rolled 47.9%
deploy  %C/A 90%  rolled 43.1%
```

No step looks terrible on its own. But fewer than half the changes get
through the whole stream without rework somewhere. The weakest step is
test, at seventy percent, which in practice often means builds arrive
without the data, configuration or environment needed to test them.

### Step by step

  ["review", "95%", "68.4%", "few reviews miss something test catches"],

1. **Ask each downstream step**: of what you receive, what share is usable
   as is?
2. **Record the answers** next to each step on the value stream map.
3. **Multiply them** to get the rolled-up first-time-right rate.
4. **Find the lowest step.** That is where the most rework enters.
5. **Ask what is usually missing or wrong** from that step's output.
6. **Move the check earlier**: the fix is almost always to catch the
   problem at its source rather than to inspect harder downstream.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to let each step score itself. Developers will
reasonably say their code is done; the test team, which cannot run it
without a missing configuration file, will disagree. The fix is to always
take the number from the receiving step.

The other mistake is to treat a low %C/A as a reason to add an inspection
step. Inspection finds problems after they are made; it does not stop them
being made, and it adds a wait. Push the check back to where the problem
starts.

### In real systems

In software, %C/A shows up as reopened tickets, failed builds, pull
requests that need several rounds of changes for missing context, deploys
that need a manual fix, and incidents caused by configuration that worked in
one environment but not another. Change failure rate, one of the four key
delivery measures, is effectively the %C/A of the deploy step as judged by
production.

## The Three Ways

### In one sentence

The Three Ways are the book's three principles: make work flow fast from
development to operations to the customer, create fast feedback from right
to left at every step, and build a culture of continual learning and
experimentation.

### The idea

The rest of the handbook is organised around three principles, which the
authors call the Three Ways, a framing that comes from Gene Kim's earlier
work and *The Phoenix Project*.

The **First Way** is about flow from left to right: from development,
through operations, to the customer. It says make work visible, keep
batches small, limit the work in progress, reduce handoffs, and optimise
for the whole stream rather than any one team. Chapter 2 covers its
principles and Part III its practices.

The **Second Way** is about feedback from right to left: from the customer
and production back to the people who made the change. It says see
problems as they happen, swarm them until they are fixed, build quality in
at the source, and design for the people downstream, including operations.
Chapter 3 covers its principles and Part IV its practices.

The **Third Way** is about a culture of continual learning and
experimentation. It says that in complex systems we cannot prevent every
failure, so we learn from each one, share what we learn across the
organisation, practise failure on purpose, and treat improvement of daily
work as more important than the daily work itself. Chapter 4 covers its
principles and Part V its practices.

The three depend on each other. Flow without feedback ships problems
faster. Feedback without flow gives information nobody can act on soon.
Both without learning fix the same problem again and again.

<Figure caption="The Three Ways on one value stream. Read the top arrow left to right (flow), the bottom arrow right to left (feedback), and the loop around both (learning)." art={`
        First Way: flow, left to right
   +------+    +------+    +------+    +----------+
   | Dev  |--->| Test |--->| Ops  |--->| Customer |
   +------+    +------+    +------+    +----------+
       ^                                     |
       +----------- feedback ----------------+
        Second Way: feedback, right to left

   Third Way: around both, a loop of learning,
   experiments and practice that improves the stream
`} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** My study repository. First Way: one exercise in progress at a
time, committed in small steps, and pushed the same day. Second Way: a
single check command runs the tests on every commit, and a red result
stops me. Third Way: a bug log where each entry ends with a rule I will
follow next time, reviewed every few weeks.

**Realistic.** A team running an online service. First Way: a deployment
pipeline that takes every merged change to production behind a flag.
Second Way: dashboards and alerts on business and technical signals, with
the developers who made the change on call for it. Third Way: blameless
postmortems after every incident, published to the whole company, plus a
fixed share of each cycle for fixing what the postmortems found.

### Step by step

Using the Three Ways to diagnose a problem, in order:

1. **Ask the flow question.** Where does work wait, and why? Is the batch
   too big, or is work stuck at a handoff?
2. **Ask the feedback question.** How soon after a mistake does the person
   who made it find out? Minutes, days or months?
3. **Ask the learning question.** When this went wrong before, what did we
   change, and did it spread beyond the people who were in the room?
4. **Pick the weakest of the three** and start there. Usually it is flow,
   because fast feedback and learning both need short cycles to work.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat the Three Ways as a sequence: do flow, then
feedback, then learning. They work together from the start. A fast
pipeline without monitoring ships breakage faster; monitoring without the
safety to report problems produces alerts nobody acts on. The fix is to
make a small improvement in all three early, then deepen each.

### In real systems

The four key delivery measures map neatly onto the Ways. Deployment
frequency and lead time for changes measure flow. Change failure rate and
time to restore service measure how well feedback catches and fixes
problems. Learning shows up as those numbers improving over time instead of
staying flat.

<details>
<summary>2. Why is a fast pipeline with no monitoring dangerous?</summary>

nobody finds out quickly. The Second Way has to grow alongside the First.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 1, Agile, Continuous Delivery, and the Three Ways">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest version of this chapter is one sentence: a change is only
worth something once it runs for the people who need it, and most of the
time between the idea and that moment is waiting. Everything else in the
chapter is a way to see and measure that waiting.

Here is the smallest worked example. I want to add a dark-mode toggle to a
small site. I write it down at 10:00. I start coding at 14:00 because I
was busy. I finish at 15:00, push at 15:05, the build runs until 15:12 and
the site is live at 15:15. Lead time: five hours and fifteen minutes.
Process time: about an hour and five minutes. Most of the lead time was the
four hours before I started.

The beginner mistake is to think the hour of coding is "the work" and the
rest does not count. It counts to whoever wanted the toggle. The fix is to
note the time an item is requested and the time it is live, and look at
that gap first.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level I combine the three measures on one map. For one
kind of change I list each step, its work time, its wait time and its
%C/A. Then I compute lead time, process time, flow efficiency and the
rolled-up first-time-right rate, as in the two scripts above.

The two kinds of number point at different fixes. A step with a long wait
needs flow work: a smaller batch, fewer handoffs, an automated step instead
of a manual one. A step with low %C/A needs quality at the source: the
check or the missing information moved upstream. A step with both is the
first one to fix.

A worked case: my review step has a two-day wait and 95% %C/A, my test step
has a two-day wait and 70% %C/A. Review is slow but good; test is slow and
bad. I fix test first, by making the build produce everything test needs,
which raises its %C/A and usually shortens its wait too, because bounced
builds were part of the queue.

The mistake here is to fix whatever is loudest. The fix is to put the
numbers on the map and let them choose.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath the chapter is queueing. The relationship called Little's law
says that, on average and in a stable system, the number of items in
progress equals the rate they arrive times the time each spends in the
system. Rearranged: time in system equals work in progress divided by
throughput. If a team finishes five changes a week and has twenty in
progress, each one spends about four weeks in the system, however fast the
coding is.

That is why the handbook cares so much about batch size and work in
progress. Lead time is not mainly a property of how fast each step works.
It is a property of how much work is queued between steps. Halving work in
progress at the same throughput halves lead time.

The second advanced idea is variation. A step whose time varies a lot,
sometimes an hour and sometimes a week, creates queues downstream even when
its average is fine, because work arrives in bursts. That is why the book
wants the commit-to-production half to be automated and repeatable: low
variation there keeps the queues short.

The advanced mistake is to set lead-time targets without changing work in
progress or variation, which just pushes people to start the clock later.
The fix is to change the system, then watch lead time fall as a result.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

**Zero wait.** A step with no waiting time looks perfect on a lead-time
map. If its %C/A is low, it is quickly producing work that will be sent
back, which is churn, not flow. Always read wait and %C/A together.

**A single item.** Measuring lead time on one change gives a number, not a
picture. That change might have hit a holiday or a broken build server. Use
the median of at least ten recent items, and look at the slowest one
separately.

**Work that is never requested.** Refactoring, upgrades and fixes I start
myself have no "request" timestamp. I start the clock when I first write
the item down. Without that rule, improvement work becomes invisible on the
map.

**A step with 100% %C/A.** It is rare, and usually means the downstream
step is not looking closely, or is quietly fixing things itself. Ask what
the downstream step does to the work before using it.

**Lead time of zero.** If a step reports zero lead time, the clock is
probably starting and stopping in the same automated job. That is fine for
an automated step, but check that no human wait has been hidden inside it.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

**Fast lead time plus heavy rework.** A team deploys within an hour of
merge, but a fifth of deploys need a follow-up fix. The lead-time number
looks excellent; the change failure rate says otherwise. Counted honestly,
lead time for a feature that needed three follow-ups includes all four
deploys.

**Low %C/A upstream plus a strict gate downstream.** Requirements are vague
(low %C/A) and the release gate is strict. Work bounces between the gate
and development several times, so the gate's wait time inflates and looks
like the bottleneck. The real problem is upstream; tightening or loosening
the gate will not fix it.

**A batch release plus a single bad change.** One defective change in a
release of a hundred forces a rollback of all hundred. Lead time for the
ninety-nine good changes jumps by a whole release cycle, through no fault
of their own. This is the corner case that makes small batches worth the
effort.

**Automated step plus a hidden manual approval.** A pipeline is "fully
automated" except that one job waits for someone to click approve. The map
shows the pipeline as minutes; in reality it is minutes plus however long
the approver takes to notice. Put the human step on the map explicitly.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

When this runs for real, the numbers need to come from systems, not from
memory. Version control gives commit times, the CI system gives build and
test times, the deploy tool gives production times, and the ticket system
gives request times. Joining them by commit hash gives lead time for
changes for every change, automatically.

A minimal production setup records, for each deploy: the commit hashes it
contains, the time of each commit, the time of the deploy, and whether the
deploy needed a follow-up fix or rollback. From that I can compute the four
key delivery measures every week without asking anyone.

What breaks at scale: clocks in different systems disagree, so I normalise
everything to UTC. Monorepos ship many services at once, so I attribute
commits to the service they touch. Squash merges lose the original commit
times, so I use the first commit on the branch or the pull request's
creation time, and say which I chose.

The production mistake is to turn these numbers into individual targets.
People then game them, splitting commits or starting clocks late. The fix
is to use them as team and system measures, to find constraints, never to
rank people.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit this chapter teaches is to look at the whole trip instead of my
own step. My step can be fast, tidy and well tested, and the customer can
still wait a month. When I catch myself saying "my part is done", I ask
"is it running for anyone yet?".

The second habit is to treat waiting as the first suspect. When something
is slow, I list where it waits before I list how to work faster. That
works far outside software: a visa application, a home renovation, a
hospital discharge. The work is rarely the long part.

The third is humility about my own quality. The person downstream is the
judge of whether my work is complete and accurate. If they keep sending it
back, my work is not done, however good it looks to me.

The mindset mistake is to hear all this as blame for the slow steps. The
slow step is usually slow because of the system around it: batches,
handoffs, missing information. The fix is to treat every slow step as a
question about the system, not about the people in it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

The value stream is shaped by architecture as much as by process. If a
change to the checkout page needs coordinated changes to four services
owned by four teams, the lead time includes four queues and a coordinated
release, however good each team's pipeline is. Chapter 7 and chapter 13
return to this.

A concrete example: an online shop where pricing, inventory, cart and
checkout are separate services with versioned interfaces. A pricing change
deploys alone, through pricing's own pipeline, in under an hour. The same
shop with one shared database and a single release for everything needs a
monthly release window for the same change. The process is identical; the
architecture sets the lead time.

So when I design a system I ask the value-stream question early: what is
the smallest change a team can ship on its own? If the answer is "nothing,
everything ships together", lead time will be long and batches big,
whatever tools we buy.

The design mistake is to split a system into many services but keep one
release train. That gets the costs of distribution without the flow. The
fix is independent deployability, which chapter 13 covers.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

How I learn this chapter: I map one real change from my own work as a
value stream, with timestamps from git and my notes. I compute lead time,
process time and flow efficiency with the script on this page, and I ask
myself where the biggest wait was. Then I repeat it for ten changes over
two weeks.

Next I estimate %C/A for my own steps. For my study repository, the steps
are "write exercise", "run checks", "write note", "publish". I count how
often each step sends work back to the previous one. Low numbers tell me
which habit to fix first.

Finally I explain the Three Ways to someone else without the book open,
with one practice for each from my own setup. If I cannot give a concrete
example for one of them, that Way is missing from my workflow.

Done when I have a written value stream map of my own loop with work,
wait and %C/A for each step, the median lead time of ten changes, and one
change I made because of it.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**Is DevOps only for large companies?** No. The ideas scale down to one
person. A solo developer still has a value stream, still waits on
themselves, and still benefits from small batches, automated checks and a
habit of learning from failures. The organisational chapters matter less
alone; the flow and feedback chapters matter just as much.

**Is the goal to deploy as often as possible?** The goal is to be able to
deploy quickly and safely whenever it is useful. A high deployment
frequency is usually a sign of that ability, not the goal itself.

**What is the difference between DevOps and continuous delivery?**
Continuous delivery is a set of engineering practices for keeping software
always releasable through a deployment pipeline. DevOps includes those
practices but also the organisation around them: shared goals between
development and operations, telemetry, learning culture and security.

**How do I measure lead time if my team does not track request dates?**
Start with lead time for changes, from first commit to production, which
version control and deploy logs already record. Add request dates later.

**Why does the book care about manufacturing?** Because manufacturing
worked out, decades earlier, how to get high quality and speed together by
watching flow and fixing problems at the source. The analogy breaks for
design work, which is why the book applies it mainly to the
commit-to-production half.

**Is %C/A the same as a defect rate?** Close, but wider. It counts any
work that has to be corrected, clarified or sent back, including missing
information and unclear requirements, not only bugs.

## Key takeaways

- A value stream is every step and every wait from a request to a customer using the result.
- Lead time is what customers feel; process time is what workers see; the gap is waiting.
- Flow efficiency, process time divided by lead time, shows whether to fix the work or the queues.
- Deployment lead time, from commit to production, is the part the book makes predictable first.
- Long deployment lead times create a loop of bigger, rarer, riskier releases.
- Percent complete and accurate is judged downstream and compounds across steps.
- The Three Ways are flow, feedback, and continual learning and experimentation, and they work together.
- Architecture sets a floor on lead time: if everything ships together, batches stay big.

## Glossary

- **Value stream**: every step and wait from a customer's request to their receiving value.
- **Technology value stream**: the path from a business hypothesis to a change running for customers.
- **Lead time**: clock time from when work is requested to when it is delivered.
- **Process time**: the part of lead time during which someone is actively working on the item.
- **Flow efficiency**: process time divided by lead time.
- **Deployment lead time**: time from a change being committed to it running in production.
- **Percent complete and accurate (%C/A)**: the share of work a step receives that it can use without rework, judged by that receiving step.
- **Rolled-up %C/A**: the product of every step's %C/A; the share of work that flows through the whole stream right first time.
- **Batch size**: how much work moves through a step together.
- **Work in progress (WIP)**: items started but not finished.
- **Little's law**: average time in system equals work in progress divided by throughput.
- **First Way**: the principles of flow, left to right.
- **Second Way**: the principles of feedback, right to left.
- **Third Way**: the principles of continual learning and experimentation.
- **Four key delivery measures**: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and time to restore service.
