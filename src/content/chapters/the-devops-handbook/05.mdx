Part II of the book turns from principles to the practical question every
transformation starts with: where do we begin? This chapter is about
choosing the first value stream to improve. The choice matters, because the
first effort has to succeed visibly enough to earn the right to the second,
and some starting points make that much easier than others.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- explain why a transformation should start with one value stream rather than the whole organisation;
- weigh greenfield against brownfield services as starting points, and say why brownfield is not ruled out;
- distinguish systems of record from systems of engagement, and why both need the same practices;
- choose a starting team by sympathy and innovation, using the technology adoption curve;
- plan how improvements spread from early adopters to the majority, and how to deal with holdouts;
- score candidate value streams against explicit criteria rather than by gut feeling.

## Why the first value stream matters

### In one sentence

A transformation cannot change everything at once, so it starts with one
value stream, and that first choice decides whether the effort builds
momentum or burns out.

### The idea

Large organisations have hundreds of value streams: every product, service
and internal system. Trying to change all of them at once spreads effort
thin, creates resistance everywhere, and produces no clear result anyone can
point to. Change efforts that start that way often stall within a year.

The book's approach is to start with one value stream, or a small number,
and make it visibly successful. Success there does three things. It proves
the practices work in this organisation, which is more convincing than any
book or conference talk. It produces people who know how to do it and can
help the next team. And it creates demand: other teams see the results and
ask to be next.

That means the first choice is a strategic one, not a technical one. The
best starting point is not necessarily the most important system or the
most broken one. It is the one where a meaningful improvement is
achievable, visible and likely to be repeated.

The book frames the choice with a few questions, which the rest of the
chapter takes in turn: is the service new or old? Is it a system that must
be stable and correct, or one that must change quickly? And, most
importantly, are the people who run it willing and able to try something
new?

#### What a good first win looks like

A first win has four properties. It is **meaningful**: it removes real
pain that the team and its stakeholders feel, such as a release that takes
a weekend, or a test cycle that takes weeks. It is **measurable**: there is
a before and an after in numbers the organisation already trusts, such as
lead time from commit to production, how often deployments happen, how
often they fail, and how long recovery takes. It is **visible**: people
outside the team notice, ideally because their own work gets easier. And
it is **repeatable**: what the team learned can be packaged and used by the
next team, rather than depending on one heroic person.

A win that has only some of these properties is weaker. A meaningful win
nobody can measure becomes an anecdote. A measurable win nobody sees
persuades nobody. A visible win that depended on one expert who then
leaves cannot be repeated. Thinking about all four before choosing a
stream helps avoid starting somewhere that can only produce a partial win.

There is also a question of scope. The first effort should be big enough
to matter and small enough to finish. "Make the whole order system
continuously deployable" is too big for a first effort. "Automate the
order system's build and test, and move from quarterly to monthly
releases" may be right. A useful test is whether the team can describe
the target in one sentence and believe they can reach it in one or two
planning cycles.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I want to apply the book's practices to my side projects. I
have five. Instead of adding CI, tests and automated deploys to all five
in one weekend and finishing none, I pick one: the blog, which I change
most often and care about most. Once its pipeline works and I trust it, I
copy what I learned to the next project, which takes a fraction of the
time.

**Realistic.** A company with forty development teams decides to "adopt
DevOps". Leadership's first instinct is a company-wide programme with a
tool rollout and mandatory training. The transformation lead pushes back:
instead, pick two value streams, give them support and a year, and measure
their lead time, deploy frequency and change failure rate. After six months
both teams have improved sharply, several other teams have asked how, and
the people from the first two teams become coaches for the next wave.

### Step by step

Framing the first choice:

1. **List candidate value streams**, product by product, with an owner for
   each.
2. **For each, note the basics**: new or old, system of record or
   engagement, who runs it, how often it changes.
3. **Rule out the impossible**: streams that are about to be retired, or
   where there is no team to work with.
4. **Score the rest** on the criteria the next sections explain.
5. **Pick one or two**, not ten.
6. **Agree what success looks like**, in measurable terms, and by when.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to start with the most critical, most broken system
because "that is where the pain is". If the team there is overloaded and
sceptical, and the system is hard to change, the first effort fails, and
the organisation concludes DevOps does not work here. The fix is to start
where success is likely and visible, and come to the hardest systems once
the practices and people are proven.

### In real systems

Many organisations start with a single product team that is already
frustrated with slow releases and has a supportive manager. They provide
that team with platform help, coaching and air cover for a few months, and
publish its results internally. The internal story often matters more than
any external case study, because people trust examples from their own
company.

## Greenfield versus brownfield services

### In one sentence

New (greenfield) services are easier places to apply DevOps practices,
but old (brownfield) services often benefit the most, and the book argues
that age alone should not rule a service out.

### The idea

A **greenfield** service is a new one, built from scratch. There is no
legacy code, no existing release process, no established habits. A team
can choose a pipeline, tests and architecture from the beginning. That
makes it easy to apply the practices, and it is a common choice for pilot
projects.

A **brownfield** service already exists, often for years. It has customers,
a codebase that may lack tests, a release process that may be manual, and
people with established habits. Changing it is harder: the team has to keep
it running while changing how it is built and shipped.

The book's point is that brownfield should not be dismissed. Much of an
organisation's value usually sits in existing systems, so improving them
can matter more than building something new. And brownfield systems often
have the most to gain: if a release takes months today, getting it to weeks
is a large, visible win. The book describes transformations that started
with old systems and succeeded, though they needed more investment, often
in automated testing and in decoupling the architecture.

There is also a middle ground. A brownfield system can be improved
incrementally: add a pipeline that builds and tests what exists, wrap the
oldest parts with tests before changing them, and carve new functionality
into new services alongside the old one. Chapter 13's strangler fig
pattern is exactly this.

#### Characterisation tests in practice

The safest first step in a brownfield codebase is often to record what the
code does today before changing anything. A characterisation test does
exactly that: it calls the existing code with a range of inputs, records the
outputs, whatever they are, and then fails if a later change alters them. It
does not claim the current behaviour is correct, only that it is the
behaviour customers currently rely on. Here is a tiny version, run with
node:

```js
// A characterisation test: record what legacy code does today, then lock it in.
function legacyShipping(weightKg, express) {
  let cost = 5;
  if (weightKg > 2) cost += (weightKg - 2) * 1.5;
  if (express) cost = cost * 2 - 1;
  return Math.round(cost * 100) / 100;
}
const cases = [[1, false], [2, false], [3, true], [10, false], [10, true]];
const recorded = cases.map(([w, e]) => ({ w, e, out: legacyShipping(w, e) }));
console.log(JSON.stringify(recorded));
// Later, after refactoring, the same cases must give the same outputs:
const failures = recorded.filter((r) => legacyShipping(r.w, r.e) !== r.out);
console.log(failures.length === 0 ? "all characterisation cases still pass" : failures);
```

It prints:

```text
[{"w":1,"e":false,"out":5},{"w":2,"e":false,"out":5},{"w":3,"e":true,"out":12},{"w":10,"e":false,"out":17},{"w":10,"e":true,"out":33}]
all characterisation cases still pass
```

In a real brownfield system the recorded outputs are saved to a file and
checked into the repository, and the cases are chosen from real traffic or
from the inputs that matter most to the business. The point for this
chapter is that the first brownfield step can be small, safe and useful on
its own: it does not change behaviour, it only makes later change safe.
That is why brownfield streams are less frightening as starting points than
they first appear.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I am starting a new tool. Before writing the first feature I
set up the repository with a test command, a CI workflow and an automated
release. It takes an hour because there is nothing to work around. Adding
the same to an old project of mine took a weekend, because its tests did
not exist and its build depended on my laptop's setup.

**Realistic.** A company has a ten-year-old order management system that
ships quarterly. Leadership assumes DevOps is for new microservices. A team
proposes a brownfield pilot: first, put the existing build into a
pipeline, unchanged. Second, add characterisation tests around the most
changed modules. Third, automate the deploy to a test environment. Fourth,
move to monthly releases, then fortnightly. Each step is a visible
improvement on a system the business depends on, which earns more support
than a new service would have.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to choose only greenfield pilots, then struggle to
apply the lessons to the systems that matter. The new service succeeds, but
everyone says "that would never work on our real systems". The fix is to
include at least one brownfield stream early, chosen for a sympathetic team,
so the organisation sees the practices work on old code too.

The opposite mistake is to start a brownfield effort with a rewrite. A
rewrite delays all value for months or years and often fails. The fix is
incremental improvement of what exists.

### In real systems

Brownfield efforts usually start with the pipeline and with tests, because
those reduce the fear of change. Teams often use characterisation tests,
which capture what the system currently does, before refactoring. They
then extract new functionality into new services rather than adding it to
the old system, gradually shrinking the part that is hard to change.

## Systems of record and systems of engagement

### In one sentence

Some systems must above all be correct and stable (systems of record) and
others must above all change quickly (systems of engagement), and the book
argues that both benefit from the same DevOps practices.

### The idea

The book uses a distinction popularised by the analyst firm Gartner. A
**system of record** is one where correctness and stability are paramount:
an accounting ledger, a payroll system, a bank's core transaction system.
It changes less often, and when it does, mistakes are very costly. A
**system of engagement** is one that customers or employees interact with
directly: a website, a mobile app, a sales tool. It needs to change often,
to respond to what users want.

Gartner's associated idea of **bimodal IT** suggested running the two
differently: careful, slow and predictable for systems of record; fast and
experimental for systems of engagement. The book disagrees with the
conclusion. It argues that the same practices, small batches, automated
testing, fast feedback, make systems of record *more* stable, not less.
Slow, large releases are riskier for a ledger, not safer.

There is a second reason both matter: they are connected. A new feature in
the mobile app (engagement) usually needs a change in the order system
(record). If the record system releases quarterly, the app's fast cycle is
capped by it. The slowest connected system sets the pace.

So the choice of first stream can be either type. Systems of engagement
are often easier to start with because their teams are used to change.
Systems of record often deliver larger gains in stability and give
evidence that the practices are not only for "fast" systems.

#### Why the slowest system sets the pace

It helps to see the coupling argument as simple arithmetic. Suppose a
customer-facing feature needs a change in the mobile app, a change in an
API, and a change in the order system. The feature reaches customers only
when all three changes are live. If the app releases weekly, the API
fortnightly and the order system quarterly, then the feature waits, on
average, for the next quarterly release, however fast the other two teams
are. Making the app team twice as fast changes almost nothing for this
feature; making the order system release monthly changes a great deal.

That is the same constraint thinking as chapter 2: improvement anywhere
other than the bottleneck is an illusion. Bimodal IT, by deliberately
keeping systems of record slow, builds a permanent bottleneck under every
system of engagement that depends on them. That is why the book insists
that the practices apply to both, and why a system of record can be a
strong starting stream when it is the constraint for many others.

]} caption="One feature that needs three systems. Read top to bottom: the feature is live only when the slowest system releases, so the quarterly cycle decides when customers see it." />

### Worked example

**Simple.** My blog is a system of engagement: I change it often and a
mistake is cheap. My backup of my notes is closer to a system of record: it
must be correct, and a mistake could lose data. I used to change the
backup script rarely and nervously. Adding a test that restores a backup
and checks every file made changing it safe, so I changed it more often and
it got better.

**Realistic.** A retailer's mobile team can ship weekly, but every feature
that touches orders waits for the order system's quarterly release.
Leadership had accepted this as "bimodal". A team applies the book's
practices to the order system: a pipeline, automated regression tests and
smaller monthly releases. Its change failure rate goes down, not up, and
the mobile team's features stop waiting a quarter.

### Step by step

How to reason about a system of record as a candidate:

1. **Ask what failure costs** in this system: money, legal exposure,
   customer trust.
2. **Ask how changes fail today**: big releases, manual steps, surprises
   in production.
3. **Note that big batches make each of those worse**, by chapter 1's
   argument.
4. **Plan the first steps around safety**: tests and a repeatable pipeline
   before any speed-up.
5. **Measure stability first** (change failure rate, time to restore),
   then speed.
6. **Use the results to answer the bimodal objection** with local evidence.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to accept bimodal thinking: "DevOps is for the
website, not the core systems". The core systems then stay slow and
fragile, and cap the speed of everything connected to them. The fix is to
treat stability as the reason to adopt small batches and automation in
systems of record, not as the reason to avoid them.

### In real systems

Banks, insurers and governments have applied continuous delivery to core
systems, typically starting with automated testing and deployment of
existing code and then reducing batch size. The pattern is usually slower
than for customer-facing apps, but the stability gains are often the most
valuable outcome of the whole programme.

## Start with the most sympathetic and innovative groups

### In one sentence

The single most important factor in choosing where to start is the people:
pick a team that believes change is needed and is willing to try new
practices, because their success will persuade everyone else.

### The idea

The book borrows from Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*, which
describes how new technology spreads through a market. People adopt in
groups. **Innovators** try new things because they are new. **Early
adopters** try them because they see the benefit and are willing to take a
risk. The **early majority** adopt once they see proof from people like
them. The **late majority** adopt once it is the norm. **Laggards** resist
until there is no alternative. Between early adopters and the early
majority there is a gap, the chasm: the evidence that convinces an early
adopter does not convince a pragmatist.

The same pattern holds inside an organisation. Some teams are eager to try
new practices; some will follow once they see proof; some will resist for a
long time. The book's advice is to start with the innovators and early
adopters, not the sceptics. A sympathetic team works through the inevitable
problems instead of using them as proof that the idea is bad. It gives
honest feedback, it improves the practices, and its success is credible to
the early majority because it is local.

**Sympathetic** here means two things: the team agrees that the current way
of working is a problem, and it is willing to change how it works. Ideally
it also has a leader who will protect it while it learns, because every
transformation has a dip: things get slower before they get faster, while
people learn the new practices.

This does not mean ignoring the sceptics forever. It means not starting
with them. Their objections are useful later, as a checklist of what the
evidence needs to answer.

#### Managing the learning dip

Almost every change in practice goes through a dip. When a team starts
writing automated tests, it ships features more slowly for a while,
because it is learning and because it is paying down the absence of tests.
When it moves to small batches, the first few weeks feel clumsy, because the
tools and habits were built for big ones. Only after the dip does the
improvement show: fewer failures, faster delivery, less firefighting.

The dip is dangerous because it arrives before the benefits. If nobody
expected it, a manager sees slower delivery and tells the team to go back
to the old way to hit a deadline, and the effort dies. So the book's advice
to start with sympathetic teams includes the people around the team: its
manager, its stakeholders, its product owner. They need to agree, in
advance, that a temporary slowdown is the price of a lasting improvement,
and to protect the team from pressure to abandon the change.

Practical ways to manage it: agree on a timebox for the dip before
starting; choose a period without a major deadline; measure the leading
signs of improvement, such as test coverage on changed code or the size
of each batch, so progress is visible even before the outcome measures
improve; and keep stakeholders updated on those signs.

#### Questions to ask a candidate team

Sympathy is easier to judge with a few concrete questions, asked in a
conversation rather than a survey:

- **What is painful about how you ship today?** A sympathetic team answers
  with specifics; a sceptical one says everything is fine or blames others.
- **What have you already tried to fix it?** Teams that have tried small
  improvements on their own are often early adopters.
- **What would you need to try something new?** The answer reveals whether
  they need time, tools, skills or permission.
- **How would your manager react to a slower month while you learn?** This
  tests whether the team has protection.
- **Who outside the team depends on your releases?** This shows how visible
  a win would be, and which dependencies might cap it.

The answers turn the sympathy score from a guess into an informed
judgement. They also start the relationship: a team that has been asked
what it needs is more likely to feel the effort is done with it rather than
to it.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I want my study group to adopt a shared practice: everyone
writes a short weekly log. Rather than asking all ten people at once, I
start with the two who already keep notes and like the idea. After a month
their logs are useful to the group, and four more people ask to join
because they can see the benefit from people they know.

**Realistic.** A transformation lead interviews six team leads. Two say
their releases are painful and they want help; one of them has a manager
who will accept a slower quarter while the team learns. Two are neutral.
Two are openly sceptical: "we tried agile, it was a waste of time". The lead
starts with the team that is both frustrated and protected. Six months later
the neutral teams are asking to be next, and one of the sceptics has quietly
started copying the pipeline.

### Step by step

Choosing between candidates is easier with explicit criteria. Here is a
small scoring script, with sympathy weighted double because the book treats
it as the deciding factor. The numbers are invented for illustration; the
point is to make the trade-offs visible and discussable.

```js
// Score candidate value streams for a first DevOps effort (1 = low, 5 = high).
const candidates = [
  { name: "checkout web app", sympathy: 5, visibility: 4, pain: 4, risk: 2 },
  { name: "billing mainframe", sympathy: 2, visibility: 5, pain: 5, risk: 5 },
  { name: "internal admin tool", sympathy: 4, visibility: 1, pain: 2, risk: 1 },
  { name: "mobile API", sympathy: 4, visibility: 4, pain: 3, risk: 3 },
];
const score = (c) => c.sympathy * 2 + c.visibility + c.pain - c.risk;
for (const c of [...candidates].sort((a, b) => score(b) - score(a))) {
  console.log(`${c.name.padEnd(20)} score ${score(c)}`);
}
```

Running it with node prints:

```text
checkout web app     score 16
mobile API           score 12
internal admin tool  score 10
billing mainframe    score 9
```

Reading the result:

1. **The checkout app wins** because its team is eager, its results will
   be seen, and it has real pain to remove, at moderate risk.
2. **The billing mainframe scores last** despite the most pain and
   visibility, because the team is sceptical and the risk is high. It is a
   later wave, not a never.
3. **The internal tool is safe but invisible**: success there would
   persuade nobody.
4. **The weights are a conversation, not a formula.** If leadership
   insists the mainframe is the priority, the score shows exactly which
   factor, sympathy, must be fixed first, for example by finding an
   enthusiastic person inside that team.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to pick the team leadership most wants to change,
often the most resistant one, in the hope that success there will convince
everyone. In practice the resistant team uses every early problem as
evidence that the approach fails. The fix is to start with sympathy, and
let the resistant teams see the results from a distance.

A second mistake is choosing a sympathetic team with no protection. If the
team's manager expects the usual output during the learning dip, the team
will drop the new practices under pressure. The fix is to agree the dip in
advance.

### In real systems

Successful programmes often begin with an open invitation: "we will help
two teams who want to improve their releases". The teams who volunteer are,
by definition, sympathetic. The programme then measures and publicises
their progress internally, in demos and short write-ups, which is what
moves the early majority.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>1. What does "sympathetic" mean when choosing a starting team?</summary>

The team agrees the current way of working is a problem and is willing to
change, ideally with a leader who will protect it through the learning dip.

</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What is the chasm, and how does it apply inside a company?</summary>

The gap between early adopters and the pragmatic majority. The majority
needs proof from people like them, so the first team's success must be
local and visible.

</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why weight sympathy more than pain in the scoring script?</summary>

Because a team in pain but unwilling to change is likely to fail, while a
willing team will work through problems. Pain without willingness does not
produce a win.

</details>

## Expanding DevOps across the organisation

### In one sentence

After the first win, practices spread in waves: first to the other
innovators and early adopters, then to the early majority through local
proof, and finally to the holdouts, by which time the new way is the norm.

### The idea

The book describes expansion as a sequence of stages, each with a
different goal.

**Find the innovators and early adopters.** In the first stage, the aim is
to find the people across the organisation who already want this. They
may be scattered: one engineer in operations, a team lead in development, a
product manager who is tired of quarterly releases. Bringing them together
creates a coalition with skills and energy.

**Build critical mass and a silent majority.** In the second stage, the
coalition grows through more successful value streams. Each success
creates more people who have seen it work. At some point the people who
have adopted the practices, plus those who quietly support them, outnumber
those who resist. The book calls this building a silent majority: a group
that may not campaign, but will not resist either. Success is accumulated
team by team, and each project should be chosen to expand the coalition,
not to take on the hardest problem.

**Identify the holdouts.** In the final stage, the remaining resistant
groups are addressed. By now the practices have local proof, experienced
people and visible support, so the case for change is much stronger. The
book suggests that the holdouts are often the groups with the most power
or the most to lose, and that by now the organisation has the evidence and
allies to persuade them, or to make the change without them.

Throughout, the book advises avoiding unnecessary political battles early.
Each fight costs credibility the young effort cannot afford. Picking battles
that can be won builds the capital for the ones that matter later.

#### Packaging the first success

Packaging is what turns one team's success into an organisation's
capability. It has three parts. The **story** is a short, honest account of
what the team changed, what got better, what was hard, and what they would
do differently, with before-and-after measures. The **assets** are the
reusable pieces: the pipeline definition, the test strategy, deployment
scripts, a checklist for onboarding a new service. The **people** are the
team members who now know how to do it and are willing to help others,
for a while, as coaches.

Each part addresses a different barrier. The story addresses doubt: it is
local proof. The assets address effort: the next team does not start from
zero. The people address skill: learning from someone who has done it in
this organisation is faster than learning from a book. Leaving out any one
of them slows the next wave noticeably.

It is worth being careful with the story. An honest account, including what
went wrong, is more credible than a polished success story, and it helps
the next team avoid the same problems. Overselling the first win creates
expectations the next teams cannot meet, and a backlash when they do not.

### Worked example

**Simple.** After my blog's pipeline worked, I applied it to my two most
active projects next (my own "early adopters", the ones I touch weekly).
Then the rest followed in an afternoon each. My oldest project, which I
barely touch, got it last, and only because by then I had a template that
made it trivial.

**Realistic.** After two successful pilot teams, a company runs a monthly
internal demo where teams show their deploy pipelines and lead time
charts. Teams who want to join get a coach drawn from the pilot teams. A
platform group turns the pilots' pipeline into a reusable template.
Within a year, most product teams use it. Two teams, which own a critical
legacy system and report to a powerful director, still resist. By then the
evidence, the template and a coach who once worked on that system make the
conversation much easier than it would have been on day one.

### Step by step

Running the expansion:

1. **Publish the first results** internally: before and after, in the
   organisation's own numbers.
2. **Turn the first team's practices into reusable assets**: a pipeline
   template, a test strategy, a runbook.
3. **Turn the first team's people into coaches** for the next wave.
4. **Open the next wave by invitation**, again favouring sympathetic teams.
5. **Track the coalition's size**: how many teams use the practices, how
   many support them.
6. **Approach the holdouts last**, with evidence and allies.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat the first success as the end. The pilot team
moves on, its knowledge is not packaged, and the next teams start from
zero. The fix is to plan the expansion as part of the first effort:
templates, coaches and internal demos.

A second mistake is fighting the holdouts too early. A public battle with a
powerful group in the first months can end the programme. The fix is
patience: build evidence and allies first.

### In real systems

Internal platforms and "paved roads" are the usual vehicle for expansion:
a supported pipeline template and a set of shared tools that make the
new way the easiest way. Combined with internal communities of practice,
demos and rotating coaches, they let practices spread without a mandate.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Selecting Which Value Stream to Start With">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the lesson is simply: do not try to change
everything at once. Pick one value stream, one product or service, and make
it better in a way people can see. A value stream is the whole path from an
idea to something running for users, so improving it means more than
adding a tool; it means the work flows faster and more safely.

For my own projects, this is the same rule. One project gets the pipeline,
tests and automated deploys first. When it works, the rest follow. The
first one is chosen because I care about it and change it often, not
because it is the most broken.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I can reason about the trade-offs. Greenfield
is easy to start but may persuade nobody; brownfield is harder but often
matters more. Systems of engagement are used to change; systems of record
need the practices just as much for stability. And the people matter most:
a sympathetic team with a protective leader will work through problems that
a sceptical team would use as evidence of failure.

I can also make the choice explicit: list candidates, score them on
sympathy, visibility, pain and risk, and discuss the weights rather than
argue from gut feeling. The score is not the decision, but it makes the
decision discussable.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I see the first value stream as the start of a
campaign, not a project. Its success has to be measured, published and
packaged, so it becomes evidence, templates and coaches for the next wave.
I think about the adoption curve: who are the innovators and early adopters
here, and what proof will the early majority need? I avoid political
battles early, because credibility is the scarce resource. I also plan for
the learning dip: performance often gets worse before it gets better, and
the team's leader must agree to that in advance.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases include: an organisation where no team is sympathetic, which
means the first step is finding individuals rather than teams and building
a small cross-team group; a candidate stream that is about to be retired,
which wastes the effort; a sympathetic team on a system nobody cares about,
whose success persuades nobody; and a stream whose release depends on
another team's slow system, so the pilot's gains are capped unless that
dependency is part of the plan.

Another edge case is a company with only one large product. There is no
choice of value stream, so the choice becomes which slice of it, which
component or team, starts first.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases are rarer: a pilot so successful that it becomes a
political threat to another group, which then resists harder; a sympathetic
team whose enthusiasm is mainly for new tools rather than for changing how
work flows, so it adopts the tools and skips the practices; a mandated
transformation where teams are not allowed to volunteer, so sympathy must be
built through early, small wins inside each team; and a regulated system
where the first changes must be approved by an auditor, which makes
chapter 22's integration of compliance part of the starting plan.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

At production level, the first value stream is chosen with data and
agreed with stakeholders. Before starting, the team records its baseline
lead time, deploy frequency, change failure rate and time to restore, and
agrees target improvements and a timeline. It gets dedicated support:
platform help, a coach, and protected capacity. Progress is reviewed
monthly with leadership and shown to other teams in demos.

Success is defined so it cannot be gamed: the measures are outcomes, not
tool adoption. "Uses the new CI tool" is not success; "lead time down from
weeks to days, with no rise in change failures" is.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is that change spreads through people and proof, not through
mandates. I would rather have one team that has truly changed how it works
than forty teams that have installed a tool. I respect sceptics, because
their objections tell me what evidence I need, but I do not start with them.
And I treat credibility as a budget: every early win adds to it, every
lost battle spends it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design terms, the choice of starting point is shaped by
dependencies. A value stream that can release independently is a better
first candidate, because its improvements show directly. One that is tightly
coupled to slow systems will have its gains capped. When the most valuable
stream is coupled, part of the first effort is to loosen the coupling: an
API or event boundary that lets the new practices apply on one side without
waiting for the other. That foreshadows chapters 7 and 13 on architecture.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategy is a sequence of deliberate bets. First wave: sympathetic
teams, measurable results, local publicity. Second wave: package the
practices into templates and coaches, and expand by invitation until the
silent majority supports the new way. Third wave: the holdouts and the
hardest systems, approached with evidence and allies. At each stage, the
choice of next stream is made to expand the coalition and the evidence,
not to pick the biggest fight.

Done when the starting value stream has a measured baseline, agreed
targets, a sympathetic team with protection, and a plan for how its
success will be packaged for the next wave.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>Should the first value stream be customer-facing?</summary>

It helps, because visibility matters and customer-facing results are easy
for everyone to understand. But a high-value internal system with a
sympathetic team can also work, as long as its improvement is noticed by
people who matter to the next wave.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if leadership has already chosen a starting point?</summary>

Work with it, but make the risks explicit. If the chosen team is
sceptical, the first job is to find willing individuals inside it and give
them small, early wins. If the system is very coupled, include the
decoupling work in the plan.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How long should the first effort take before results are visible?</summary>

The book does not give a fixed number, but the aim is months, not years.
Choose a scope where meaningful, measurable improvement is possible within
one or two planning cycles, so momentum is not lost.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is it unfair to the other teams to help only one or two at first?</summary>

It can feel that way, but spreading help thin usually helps nobody. A
focused start creates the templates and coaches that make helping everyone
else faster. Being open about the plan for later waves helps.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does "greenfield" really mean in a company with lots of shared infrastructure?</summary>

Even a new service inherits shared networks, databases and processes, so
true greenfield is rare. The useful question is how much existing code,
process and habit the team has to work around.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can I use this chapter for personal projects?</summary>

Yes. The same logic applies: start with the project I care about and change
often, make it visibly better, then reuse what I learned. My own
willingness plays the role of the sympathetic team.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if the first effort fails?</summary>

Run a blameless review, as chapter 4 describes. Was the stream a poor
choice, was the team unprotected, were the goals unclear? The lessons
improve the next choice. A failure in a small, chosen pilot is much cheaper
than a failed company-wide programme.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How is sympathy different from enthusiasm for tools?</summary>

Sympathy is agreement that how the work flows is a problem and willingness
to change practices. Enthusiasm for a tool can coexist with unchanged
practices, which produces a new tool and the same results.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Start with one or two value streams, not the whole organisation; the first effort must succeed visibly.
- Greenfield is easier to start; brownfield often matters more and gains more. Do not rule out brownfield, and do not start it with a rewrite.
- Systems of record need the same practices as systems of engagement, for stability; the slowest connected system caps the speed of the rest.
- The most important factor is people: start with sympathetic, innovative teams with a protective leader.
- Use the adoption curve: the early majority needs local proof from people like them.
- Expand in waves: find the early adopters, build critical mass and a silent majority, then address the holdouts.
- Package the first success as templates, coaches and published results so the next wave is cheaper.
- Treat credibility as a budget and avoid early political battles.

## Glossary

- **Value stream**: the sequence of work from a business idea to a running service delivering value to customers.
- **Greenfield**: a new service built from scratch, with no legacy code or process.
- **Brownfield**: an existing service with established code, process and habits.
- **System of record**: a system where correctness and stability come first, such as a ledger.
- **System of engagement**: a system users interact with directly, which must change often.
- **Bimodal IT**: the idea of running systems of record and engagement in two different modes; the book argues against its conclusion.
- **Technology adoption curve**: the spread of a new idea through innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards.
- **Chasm**: the gap between early adopters and the pragmatic majority, crossed only with credible local proof.
- **Sympathetic team**: a team that agrees change is needed and is willing to try new practices.
- **Silent majority**: the people who support or accept the new practices even if they do not campaign for them.
- **Holdouts**: groups that still resist late in the transformation.
- **Learning dip**: the temporary drop in performance while people learn new practices.
- **Characterisation test**: a test that records what existing code currently does, so it can be changed safely.
