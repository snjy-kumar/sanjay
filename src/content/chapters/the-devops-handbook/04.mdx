## Organisational learning and a safety culture

### In one sentence

An organisation learns only if people can report problems, mistakes and
near misses without fear, so the first job of the Third Way is a culture in
which bad news travels fast.

### The idea

Every organisation has failures. What differs is what happens to the
information about them. In some places, people hide mistakes because
reporting one gets them punished. In others, the news is reported but
dies in a process. In the best, the news travels quickly to the people who
can use it, and the response is curiosity rather than blame.

The sociologist Ron Westrum described this as three types of
organisational culture, which the book uses:

- **Pathological** (power-oriented): information is hoarded or used as a
  weapon, messengers are punished, failure is covered up, and new ideas
  are crushed.
- **Bureaucratic** (rule-oriented): information flows through official
  channels, messengers are tolerated, responsibilities are narrow,
  failures lead to looking for whose rule was broken, and new ideas are
  seen as problems.
- **Generative** (performance-oriented): information is actively sought,
  messengers are trained, responsibilities are shared, failure leads to
  inquiry, and new ideas are welcomed.

Westrum's insight was that the way information flows predicts how safe and
effective an organisation is. Research that followed, including the work
summarised in *Accelerate*, found generative culture associated with better
delivery and organisational performance.

The book links this to the idea of a **just culture**, from safety
research by people such as Sidney Dekker. In a just culture, when something
goes wrong, the question is "what about the system made this action
reasonable at the time?" rather than "who do we blame?". The reason is
practical. If people are blamed for honest mistakes, they stop reporting
them, and the organisation loses the information it needs to prevent the
next one. Blame does not remove the cause; it removes the evidence.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I run a command that deletes the wrong folder in my study
repository. I could just restore it and move on, embarrassed. Instead I
write a short entry in my bug log: what I ran, why it seemed right, what
made it easy to get wrong (two folders with nearly the same name, no
confirmation), and the rule I will follow (rename one folder; use a dry
run first). The mistake is now information.

**Realistic.** An engineer pushes a configuration change that takes down
the login service for twenty minutes. In a pathological culture she is
blamed, and the team learns to hide mistakes. In a bureaucratic one, a new
rule says all configuration changes need a manager's approval, which
slows every change and does not address the cause. In a generative one, the
review finds that the configuration file had no validation, the staging
environment used a different format, and the deploy tool did not show a
diff. The team adds validation, aligns staging, and adds a diff to the
tool. Nobody is punished, and three conditions that made the mistake easy
are gone.

### Step by step

How a just-culture review handles an incident, step by step:

1. **Gather the facts first**, from logs, timelines and the people
   involved, without judgment.
2. **Ask each person what they saw, what they expected and why their
   action made sense** at the time, with the information they had.
3. **List the conditions** that made the outcome possible: missing checks,
   confusing interfaces, time pressure, unclear ownership.
4. **Separate honest mistakes from reckless choices.** A just culture is
   not "no consequences"; it distinguishes error from deliberate
   disregard. Almost all incidents are the first kind.
5. **Choose actions that change conditions**, not ones that only exhort
   people to be careful.
6. **Publish the review** so others can learn from it.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to say "we are blameless" while still asking "who
did this?" in every review, or quietly counting incidents against people.
People notice, and stop reporting. The fix is to change the questions in
the review template and the way leaders react in the first minutes after
an incident, which is when people decide whether it is safe to talk.

The second mistake is to read just culture as "nobody is accountable". It
means accountability for learning and for fixing conditions, and for
explaining what happened honestly. It still deals with genuine recklessness,
which is rare.

### In real systems

Many engineering organisations run blameless post-incident reviews with a
fixed template: timeline, contributing factors, what went well, what was
hard, and actions. Some publish them company-wide. A few measure culture
directly with short surveys based on Westrum's questions, asking whether
information is sought, whether messengers are punished and whether failure
leads to inquiry.

## Institutionalise the improvement of daily work

### In one sentence

Improving how the work is done is itself work, and it has to be scheduled
and protected, or the urgent always crowds it out.

### The idea

Every team has a list of small annoyances: a flaky test, a slow build, a
manual step in the deploy, a confusing error message. Each one costs a
little every day. Each one could be fixed in an hour or a day. And almost
none get fixed, because there is always a feature or an incident that seems
more urgent.

Left alone, those annoyances accumulate. The book calls the result
**technical debt** in the broad sense: the growing cost of doing work in a
system that has not been improved. Over time, teams spend more and more of
their capacity on workarounds and firefighting, and less on new work,
until they can barely move. The book's companion novel *The Phoenix
Project* dramatises a team in exactly that state.

The Third Way's answer is to make improvement a normal, scheduled part of
the work, not a favour done in spare time. Teams reserve explicit capacity
for it: a share of every iteration, regular improvement blitzes, or a rule
that any work making daily work better takes priority. The book puts it
strongly: improving daily work matters even more than doing daily work,
because it compounds.

The image from Lean is that the work has two parts: the work itself, and
the improvement of the work. Toyota's people were expected to do both. A
team that only does the first is standing still at best.

#### Why improvement compounds

A toy model makes the argument concrete. Two teams each have forty hours a
week. Both start with ten hours of toil, meaning repetitive manual work,
workarounds and firefighting, and in both the toil grows a little each
week as the system ages. Team A spends everything that is left on new
work. Team B reserves four hours a week for improvement, and each reserved
hour removes a small slice of weekly toil for good.

```js
// Two teams, 40 hours a week each. Toil grows 2% a week unless fixed.
// Team B reserves 4 hours a week; each hour removes 0.15 hours of weekly toil.
let toilA = 10, toilB = 10;
for (let week = 1; week <= 26; week++) {
  toilA *= 1.02;
  toilB = Math.max(0, toilB * 1.02 - 4 * 0.15);
  if (week % 6 === 0 || week === 1) {
    const freeA = 40 - toilA, freeB = 40 - toilB - 4;
    console.log(`week ${String(week).padStart(2)}: A toil ${toilA.toFixed(1)} h, new work ${freeA.toFixed(1)} h | B toil ${toilB.toFixed(1)} h, new work ${freeB.toFixed(1)} h`);
  }
}
```

Output:

```text
week  1: A toil 10.2 h, new work 29.8 h | B toil 9.6 h, new work 26.4 h
week  6: A toil 11.3 h, new work 28.7 h | B toil 7.5 h, new work 28.5 h
week 12: A toil 12.7 h, new work 27.3 h | B toil 4.6 h, new work 31.4 h
week 18: A toil 14.3 h, new work 25.7 h | B toil 1.4 h, new work 34.6 h
week 24: A toil 16.1 h, new work 23.9 h | B toil 0.0 h, new work 36.0 h
```

The numbers are invented; the shape is the point. In the first weeks team
B looks worse, because it gives up four hours of new work. Around week six
the lines cross. By the end, team A spends a growing share on toil and
team B has more time for new work than A ever had. Improvement is an
investment with a delay before the return, which is exactly why it gets
cut when people only look at this week.

The model also shows the trap in the other direction. If team B's reserved
hours removed nothing, because they were spent on the wrong things, it
would simply be four hours down every week. That is why choosing by daily
cost matters as much as reserving the time.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I keep a short list called "papercuts" in my study repository:
things that slowed me down this week. Every Friday I spend the first hour
fixing the top one. In the first month I fixed a slow test, a confusing
script name, a missing shortcut and a dependency that took ages to
install. Each saved minutes a day; together they made the next month
noticeably smoother.

**Realistic.** A team reserves a fixed share of each two-week cycle for
improvement work chosen by the team itself. They track how much time the
on-call engineer spends on repetitive, manual operations work. In the first
cycles they automate the three most frequent manual tasks. A few cycles
later, on-call is quiet enough that the on-call engineer has time to
improve the tooling further. The improvement time pays for itself, and
then funds more improvement.

### Step by step

Making improvement part of the work:

1. **Collect the annoyances** where people can add them in seconds: a
   board column, a file, a label.
2. **Reserve capacity explicitly**: a fixed share of each cycle, or a
   regular day. Write it into the plan so it is visible.
3. **Let the people doing the work choose** what to improve; they know
   where the pain is.
4. **Rank by daily cost**: how often it hurts times how much each time.
5. **Fix the top items in the reserved time**, and record what changed.
6. **Protect the time.** If it is cancelled for features three cycles
   running, it is not reserved; raise that explicitly.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat improvement time as a buffer, the first
thing cut when a deadline looms. It is then cut every time, because there
is always a deadline. The fix is to treat it like any other commitment:
planned, visible and only cancelled by an explicit decision that is
recorded.

The second mistake is to spend improvement time on whatever is interesting
rather than what hurts most. Rewriting a module for fun is not
institutionalised improvement. The fix is to rank by daily cost.

### In real systems

Teams implement this as a fixed share of capacity for engineering-driven
work, hack days devoted to internal tooling, rotations where one person per
cycle works only on developer experience, or limits on operational toil
that trigger automation work when they are exceeded, as site reliability
engineering practice describes.

## Turn local discoveries into global improvements

### In one sentence

When one person or team learns something, encode it so that the whole
organisation benefits, rather than leaving it in one person's head or one
team's wiki page.

### The idea

Suppose a team spends a day discovering that a certain library has a
memory leak under a particular load pattern. They fix it in their service.
Six months later another team hits the same leak, spends another day
finding it, and fixes it in theirs. Then a third team. The organisation has
paid for the same lesson three times, and will keep paying.

The Third Way says that a local discovery is only half the value. The
other half comes from making it global: turning the lesson into something
that protects every team automatically. That is the difference between an
organisation that learns and one where individuals learn.

Knowledge spreads in roughly three forms, from weakest to strongest:

- **Written down**: a postmortem, a wiki page, a chat message. Better than
  nothing, but it relies on people finding and reading it at the right
  moment.
- **Built into shared tools**: a template, a library, a pipeline step, a
  default configuration. Now people get the lesson without having to know
  it exists.
- **Enforced by the system**: a check that fails the build, a platform
  that does not allow the dangerous configuration. The lesson cannot be
  forgotten.

The book's preference is clear: wherever possible, move knowledge from the
first form to the second or third. A lesson encoded in code protects people
who never read the postmortem, including people who join years later.
Chapter 20 turns this principle into concrete practices.

<Layers caption="Three strengths of shared knowledge. Read top to bottom: each layer protects more people with less effort from them, so move lessons down the stack whenever you can." layers={[
  { name: "Written down", note: "postmortem, wiki, chat: people must find and read it" },
  { name: "Built into shared tools", note: "templates, libraries, defaults: people get it by using the tool" },
  { name: "Enforced by the system", note: "checks and platform rules: the mistake cannot be made" },
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I discover that my site's build silently passes when a note has
a broken internal link. I fix the link. Then I add a check to the build that
fails on any broken internal link. The lesson is now enforced: future me
and anyone else editing the site cannot repeat the mistake, even if we
forget it ever happened.

**Realistic.** One team's incident review finds that their service fell
over because an HTTP client had no timeout, so a slow dependency tied up
every thread. Option one: they add a timeout in their own code and write a
wiki page. Option two: the platform team changes the shared HTTP client
library so it has a sensible default timeout, and adds a lint rule that
warns on clients created without one. Every service that upgrades the
library gets the fix, and the lint rule catches new code. The first option
fixed one service. The second fixed the organisation.

### Step by step

<Trace columns={["Step", "What happens", "Who is protected afterwards"]} rows={[
  ["1. Local fix", "The team adds a timeout to its own HTTP client", "One service"],
  ["2. Write it down", "The incident review names missing timeouts as a cause and is published", "Teams that read it"],
  ["3. Search for the pattern", "A quick search finds clients without timeouts in other services", "Teams told directly"],
  ["4. Change the shared default", "The shared library gets a default timeout", "Every service that upgrades"],
  ["5. Enforce it", "A lint rule flags clients created without an explicit timeout", "All new code, by everyone"],
]} caption="One lesson moved from local to global. Read the last column top to bottom: the protected group grows at each step, and only the last two need nobody to remember anything." />

1. **Fix the local problem** first; the incident has to end.
2. **In the review, ask: where else could this happen?** That is the
   question that turns local into global.
3. **Search for the same pattern** elsewhere: other services, other
   repositories, other teams' configurations.
4. **Find the shared place** where a change protects everyone: a library,
   a template, a base image, a pipeline.
5. **Make the safe way the default**, and add a check for the unsafe way if
   it can still be written.
6. **Write it down last**, with a link to the code change, so readers can
   see what was done, not just what was learned.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to stop at "write it down". The postmortem is
published, everyone agrees it was a good lesson, and a year later a team
that never read it makes the same mistake. The fix is to ask, for every
action item, "could this be a default or a check instead of a document?".

The second mistake is the opposite: encoding every lesson as a hard rule
immediately, so the shared platform fills with restrictions nobody
understands. The fix is to prefer good defaults over prohibitions, and to
explain each check in its error message, linking to the lesson behind it.

### In real systems

This is why organisations build internal platforms, shared libraries and
"golden path" templates for new services. A new service started from the
template gets logging, metrics, timeouts, security headers and a pipeline
with the standard checks, because the template encodes years of local
discoveries. Large monorepos make this easier still, because a fix to a
shared library reaches every user in one change.

## Inject resilience patterns into daily work

### In one sentence

Make the system stronger by exposing it to failure and stress on purpose,
in controlled ways, so that real failures find it already practised.

### The idea

A system that has never failed in a controlled way has untested failure
paths. Its failover has never actually failed over. Its backups have never
actually been restored. Its on-call engineers have never actually handled
an outage of that kind. When the real failure comes, all of those are
tested at once, under pressure, for the first time.

The Third Way says to do the testing deliberately, before reality does.
There are several patterns, at different scales:

- **Shorten the cycle** to expose problems: deploying more often, rebuilding
  environments from scratch regularly, rotating credentials frequently.
  Anything done often becomes practised and automated.
- **Game days**: scheduled exercises where a team simulates a failure, such
  as a lost data centre or a failed database, and practises responding.
- **Fault injection**: deliberately causing failures in a running system,
  such as killing processes, adding latency or dropping network traffic, to
  check that it copes. Netflix's Chaos Monkey, which randomly terminates
  production instances, is the well-known example the book discusses.

The underlying idea is sometimes put in terms of antifragility, a word
from Nassim Nicholas Taleb: some systems get stronger when stressed, the
way muscles do. A team that practises failure gets better at it, and a
system that is regularly broken on purpose is designed to survive being
broken.

A related Lean idea is to deliberately make problems visible by lowering
tolerances. Toyota would reduce inventory buffers on purpose to expose the
problems the buffers were hiding. In software, lowering a timeout or
reducing a retry budget in a test environment can expose hidden
dependencies in the same way.

  "Choose a failure to practise",
  "Write what we expect to happen",
  "Agree how to stop the exercise safely",
  "Inject the failure",
  "Observe what really happens",
  "Review the gaps and fix them",
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I have a backup script for my notes and have never restored
from it. I spend twenty minutes on a game day for one: I delete a copy of
my notes folder and restore it from the backup into a fresh directory. It
turns out the backup skipped hidden files, which is where my configuration
lives. Without the exercise, I would have found out the day I lost the
real data.

**Realistic.** A team runs a quarterly game day. This quarter: "the primary
database becomes unavailable". They write down what they expect: automatic
failover within a minute, a brief error spike, and an alert to on-call.
They run it in a staging environment that mirrors production. Failover
works, but takes several minutes, not one, because a health check has a
long interval; the alert goes to an old rotation; and the application holds
stale connections until it is restarted. Three fixes come out of one
afternoon, each of which would have made a real outage longer.

### Step by step

Running a first game day safely:

1. **Pick one failure** that would hurt and that you have never practised:
   a dependency down, a disk full, a credential expired.
2. **Write down your hypothesis**: what should happen, how fast, and what
   people should see.
3. **Choose the environment.** Start in staging. Move to production only
   when staging exercises are routine and you have a way to stop.
4. **Set a stop condition**: how you will end the exercise if it goes
   wrong, and who decides.
5. **Inject the failure** and watch. Write down what actually happens,
   minute by minute.
6. **Compare with the hypothesis**, list every gap, and turn each into a
   ticket on the board.
7. **Repeat the same exercise later** to check the fixes worked.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to start with chaos in production before the basics
are in place: no monitoring, no rollback, no runbook. That is not
resilience engineering; it is an outage. The fix is to start small, in a
safe environment, with a written hypothesis and a stop condition, and grow
the scope as confidence grows.

The second mistake is to run exercises and not fix what they find. A game
day whose findings sit in a document is a performance. The fix is to put
every finding on the board with an owner and a date.

### In real systems

Practices include regular disaster-recovery drills, restore tests for
backups, scheduled failover tests, chaos engineering tools that inject
faults into Kubernetes clusters or service meshes, and "wheel of
misfortune" exercises where on-call engineers practise diagnosing past
incidents. Some teams also rebuild their environments from scratch on a
schedule, so the automation that builds them is always tested.

</details>

## Leaders reinforce a learning culture

### In one sentence

Leaders create a learning organisation not by having the answers but by
setting direction and conditions, and coaching people to run small
experiments toward a goal, the pattern the book calls the improvement kata.

### The idea

In a traditional view, leaders make decisions and workers carry them out.
The Third Way gives leaders a different job: create the conditions in which
people at every level can discover and implement improvements themselves.
That includes making it safe to report problems, reserving time for
improvement and modelling curiosity about failure.

The book draws on Mike Rother's study of how Toyota develops people, which
he described as the **improvement kata**. A kata is a routine practised
until it becomes habit, like a martial-arts form. The improvement kata is a
routine for making progress toward a goal under uncertainty:

1. **Understand the direction**: a long-term goal or challenge.
2. **Grasp the current condition**: measure where you are now, honestly.
3. **Establish the next target condition**: a specific state you want to
   reach by a date, a step toward the direction.
4. **Experiment toward the target**: run small experiments, learning from
   each, until you get there. Then set the next target.

Alongside it is the **coaching kata**, which leaders use. Instead of telling
people what to do, the leader asks a short set of questions: What is the
target condition? What is the actual condition now? What obstacles are in
the way, and which are you addressing now? What is your next step, and what
do you expect? When can we see what you learned?

The value of the routine is that it makes improvement a skill people
practise daily, rather than a project that happens once a year. And
leaders who coach this way spread the capability to everyone they work
with.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I apply the kata to my own study. Direction: be able to build a
small database engine by the end of the year. Current condition: I can
write a key-value store in memory, with no persistence. Next target
condition, in two weeks: values survive a restart. My first experiment:
append every write to a log file and replay it on start. I predict it works
but is slow to start with many writes. I run it, and it is slow. Next
experiment: a periodic snapshot plus a short log. Each experiment is small,
predicted, run and reviewed.

**Realistic.** An engineering manager wants to shorten her team's lead time.
Instead of announcing a new process, she coaches. Target condition for the
month: median time from merge to production under one day. Current
condition, measured: four days, mostly waiting for a weekly release. The
team names obstacles: the manual release checklist, a slow end-to-end
test, a required approval. They pick the slow test first. Each week the
manager asks the five coaching questions, and the team reports what they
tried, what they expected and what they learned. By the end of the month
the median is just over a day; next month's target builds on it.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is for leaders to treat learning culture as a slogan,
while still punishing failed experiments and rewarding only hitting
targets. People then run only safe experiments, or none, and report only
good news. The fix is for leaders to ask about what was learned, including
from experiments that did not work, and to visibly value that.

The second mistake is to set a target condition that is too large, such as
"zero incidents", which cannot be reached by small experiments. The fix is
a target that is a stretch but reachable in weeks, so the loop turns
quickly.

### In real systems

Leaders reinforce learning by attending postmortems and asking curious
questions, by publicly thanking people who report problems, by protecting
improvement time against deadline pressure, and by holding regular
check-ins structured like the coaching kata. Some organisations run
internal conferences or demo days where teams share experiments,
successful or not, which chapter 21 covers.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 4, The Third Way: The Principles of Continual Learning and Experimentation">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest version of the Third Way is: after something goes wrong, ask
what happened and what you will change, not who is to blame. Then write the
answer down where you will see it again.

A worked example for a beginner: I accidentally commit a file with a
password in it. I remove it and rotate the password. Then I write in my bug
log: "Committed a secret. Cause: config file not in .gitignore, and no
check for secrets. Rule: add the file to .gitignore and run a secret
scanner before every commit." The next week I add the scanner. The mistake
has turned into a check.

The beginner mistake is to feel bad, fix it quietly and move on. The fix is
to treat every mistake as data: what made it easy, and what would make it
hard next time.

I also start the habit of a weekly improvement hour, even alone: one hour
every Friday to fix the most annoying thing in my setup. That is the
smallest form of institutionalised improvement.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level the habits combine for a team. Incidents get a
blameless review with a fixed template. Each review asks "where else could
this happen?" and produces at least one action that changes a shared tool,
default or check. Improvement work has reserved capacity in every cycle.
The team runs a small game day each quarter.

A worked example: a team's review of a disk-full outage asks where else
disks could fill. A search finds four other services with logs that never
rotate. Instead of fixing four services, they add log rotation to the
shared base image and a disk-usage alert to the standard monitoring
template. Next quarter's game day fills a disk on purpose in staging to
check the alert fires and the runbook works.

The intermediate mistake is to produce actions that are all documents:
"update the wiki", "remind people". The fix is a rule that at least one
action per review is a change to code, configuration or a default.

Another intermediate skill is writing the review itself well: a timeline
from the evidence, the contributing factors in plain language, and what
each person knew at each point. The quality of the review decides whether
anyone else learns from it.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath the chapter is a theory of how safety and learning work in
complex systems. Safety researchers distinguish a view where failures come
from people deviating from correct procedures, so the fix is more rules and
more discipline, from a view where people constantly adapt to keep a
complex system working, and failures are the rare cases where normal
adaptation did not work out. The second view, associated with researchers
such as Dekker and with resilience engineering, is the one the book
adopts.

Under that view, hindsight bias is the enemy of learning. After an
incident, the cause looks obvious, so it is easy to say the engineer should
have seen it. But at the time, with the information they had, their action
made sense. A review that does not reconstruct that view learns nothing,
because it only describes what the reviewer can see now.

The second advanced idea is that experimentation is the method, not just a
mood. The improvement kata is the scientific method applied to daily work:
a hypothesis, a prediction, an experiment, an observation, a conclusion.
Its power comes from making the prediction explicit before the experiment,
which is what makes learning possible when the experiment fails.

The advanced mistake is to run experiments without predictions. Then every
result looks like success, because you adjust your story to it. The fix is
to write down what you expect before each step, and compare.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

**An incident with no visible cause.** The system recovered on its own and
nobody knows why it failed. That is still worth a review: the action is
usually to add telemetry so the next occurrence explains itself.

**A near miss.** Nothing broke, but it almost did: a deploy was rolled back
seconds before customers noticed. Near misses are the cheapest lessons and
the easiest to ignore. Review them too, briefly.

**A one-person team.** There is nobody to be blamed or to blame. Even so,
self-blame has the same effect, making me avoid looking closely at my
mistakes. Writing the bug log entry in a neutral, factual voice helps.

**A repeat incident.** The same failure happens twice. That is a signal
that the first review's actions did not change conditions, or were not
done. Review the review: why did the fix not hold?

**A failed game day.** The exercise itself caused a real outage. That is a
finding: the stop condition or the environment choice was wrong. Review it
like any other incident.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

**Blameless reviews plus a reckless act.** Rarely, someone knowingly
bypasses a safety control for no good reason. A just culture handles this
separately and openly, without turning every review into a hunt for
recklessness. The corner case is treating an honest mistake as reckless
because the outcome was bad.

**A global fix plus a team with good reasons to differ.** A shared default
timeout of five seconds is right for most services but wrong for a batch
job that legitimately waits a minute. Enforcing the default without an
override breaks that team. The fix is a default plus an explicit, reviewed
way to opt out.

**Reserved improvement time plus an incident week.** A major incident eats
the whole cycle, including the improvement time. Skipping it once is
reasonable. Skipping it every time there is an incident means teams that
have the most incidents never get time to fix their causes. Protect it
especially there.

**Fault injection plus missing observability.** A chaos tool kills a
process, and the system seems fine. But without metrics for error rates,
nobody can tell whether customers saw errors. The experiment produced no
information. Observability must come before injection.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production organisations, learning has infrastructure. A review
template and a searchable archive of reviews. A tracker for review action
items with owners and dates, and a regular check on how many are overdue.
A schedule for game days and disaster-recovery tests. A shared library and
template owners who turn lessons into defaults.

Some numbers teams track: the share of review actions completed on time,
the number of repeat incidents (same contributing factor twice), how often
backups are restored in tests, and how much on-call time goes to manual,
repetitive work. A growing count of overdue actions is a sign that learning
is being written down but not acted on.

What breaks at scale: review archives become too big to read, so the same
lessons are rediscovered. The fix is periodic synthesis: someone reads the
last quarter's reviews and writes up the themes, then turns the top themes
into platform work.

The production mistake is to make reviews so heavy that teams avoid them
for small incidents. The fix is a lightweight template for small incidents
and near misses, with the full process only for big ones.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset of the Third Way is that every failure is a chance to learn
something the organisation did not know, and that learning is worth more
than avoiding embarrassment. It replaces "who did this?" with "what made
this possible?".

It also treats the way work is done as something to experiment on, not
something fixed. The process I use today is a hypothesis about the best
way to work. Each small change is an experiment, with a prediction.

This mindset works outside software: a cook adjusting a recipe one change
at a time and writing down the result, or a team in a hospital reviewing
a near miss without blame.

The mindset mistake is to confuse learning culture with comfort. Generative
cultures ask hard questions and expect high standards; they just aim those
questions at the system rather than at individuals.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

The Third Way shapes systems in concrete ways. A system built for learning
has observability designed in, so incidents can be reconstructed. It has
shared libraries and templates, so a lesson can be encoded once. It has
safe ways to inject failure, such as feature flags, traffic shaping and
isolated environments, so experiments are cheap. And it has fast, reliable
rollback, so experiments that go wrong are recoverable.

A concrete example: an organisation runs many services from a common
service template. After a series of incidents caused by unbounded retries,
the template's HTTP client gains a retry budget and a circuit breaker by
default. Every new service is protected from day one, and existing ones
get it when they update. A fault-injection tool in the staging cluster adds
latency to dependencies weekly, so the circuit breakers are tested
continuously rather than discovered during an outage.

When I design something, I ask learning questions: if this fails, will we
be able to tell why? Is there one place where a fix would reach every
user? Can we break it on purpose safely?

The design mistake is to build every service as a one-off, so no lesson
can be shared except by copying. The fix is a small, maintained set of
shared building blocks.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

How I learn this chapter: I keep a bug log from day one of Phase 0, where
every entry has the same fields: what happened, what I expected, what made
it easy, and the rule or check I added. I reread it monthly and look for
repeats, which tell me which lessons did not hold.

I reserve an improvement hour every week and protect it. I run a small
game day once a phase: restoring from backup, rebuilding my environment
from scratch, or running my tests on a clean machine. Each one gets a short
written review.

I apply the improvement kata to one goal per phase, with a written target
condition and a prediction before each experiment.

Done when my bug log has entries with rules and checks, at least one lesson
has become an automated check in my repository, I have run and reviewed a
game day, and I can explain Westrum's three cultures with an example of
how bad news travels in each.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

**Isn't blameless just a nice word for avoiding responsibility?** No. It
means holding people responsible for honesty and for improving the system,
while recognising that honest mistakes come from conditions. Reckless
disregard is still dealt with; it is just rare.

**How much time should be reserved for improvement?** The book does not set
a single number, and teams vary. What matters is that it is explicit,
visible and protected. A small, reliable share beats a large one that is
always cancelled.

**Is chaos engineering only for huge companies?** The tools often come from
them, but the idea scales down. Restoring a backup, killing a local process
or turning off the network on a laptop to see how an app behaves are all
fault injection.

**What if leadership does not support any of this?** Start where you have
control: your own reviews, your own improvement time, your own team's
defaults. Show results. Westrum's typology is about how information flows,
and you can improve that in your own team even if the wider organisation is
slower.

**How do you stop postmortems from becoming paperwork?** Keep them short,
require at least one action that changes code or configuration, track the
actions to completion, and occasionally read old ones to check the actions
held.

**What is the difference between the improvement kata and a plan?** A plan
assumes you know the steps. The kata assumes you do not, and finds them by
small experiments toward a target, learning as you go.

## Key takeaways

- An organisation can only learn from failures it hears about, so bad news has to travel fast and safely.
- Westrum's three cultures, pathological, bureaucratic and generative, differ in how information flows.
- A just culture asks what made an action reasonable at the time, instead of who to blame.
- Improving daily work is work; it needs reserved, protected time.
- A lesson is only half learned until it is encoded where everyone benefits, ideally as a default or a check.
- Practising failure on purpose, through game days and fault injection, finds gaps before reality does.
- The improvement kata turns progress under uncertainty into a daily routine of small, predicted experiments.
- Leaders build learning culture by coaching and by how they react in the first minutes after a failure.

## Glossary

- **Westrum typology**: Ron Westrum's three culture types, pathological, bureaucratic and generative.
- **Generative culture**: a performance-oriented culture where information is sought and failure leads to inquiry.
- **Just culture**: a culture that treats honest mistakes as system problems and distinguishes them from recklessness.
- **Hindsight bias**: the tendency, after an outcome is known, to see it as having been obvious.
- **Blameless post-incident review**: a review focused on conditions and learning rather than on fault.
- **Near miss**: an event that could have caused harm but did not.
- **Technical debt**: the accumulated cost of working in a system that has not been improved.
- **Improvement blitz**: a short, focused period devoted to fixing accumulated problems.
- **Game day**: a scheduled exercise that simulates a failure to practise the response.
- **Fault injection**: deliberately causing failures in a system to test its resilience.
- **Chaos engineering**: disciplined experimentation with failures in production-like systems.
- **Antifragility**: the property of getting stronger under stress.
- **Improvement kata**: a routine of direction, current condition, target condition and experiments.
- **Coaching kata**: the questions a leader asks to help others practise the improvement kata.
- **Target condition**: a specific, dated state to reach on the way to a long-term direction.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up.**

1. Think of the last mistake you made at work or in a project. Write a
   blameless entry for it: what happened, what you expected, what made it
   easy, what would make it hard.
2. For a team you know, answer three Westrum-style questions: is
   information sought, are messengers punished, does failure lead to
   inquiry? Which culture type is it?
3. List five annoyances in your setup and estimate how much each costs per
   week.

**Build.**

4. Create a bug log template with the fields on this page and fill in your
   last three bugs.
5. Take one lesson from your log and turn it into an automated check in
   your repository.
6. Run a backup-restore game day on your own machine. Write a short review.

**Stretch.**

7. Apply the improvement kata to one goal for a month. Write the target
   condition, and a prediction before each experiment. Compare predictions
   with results at the end.
8. Pick a past incident you know about and rewrite its review without
   hindsight: what did each person know at each moment?
