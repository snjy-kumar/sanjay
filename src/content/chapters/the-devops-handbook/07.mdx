The value stream map from chapter 6 usually shows that the longest waits
are at hand-offs between teams. This chapter asks why those hand-offs exist,
and answers with Conway's Law: systems end up shaped like the
organisations that build them. So if we want a system where changes flow
quickly and safely, we have to design the teams and the architecture
together. The chapter covers how organisations are typically structured,
what each structure optimises for, and the practices that let small teams
own services from end to end.

## Conway's Law

### In one sentence

Organisations design systems whose structure copies the organisation's own
communication structure, so team boundaries become system boundaries
whether anyone intends it or not.

### The idea

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that any organisation that designs a system
will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organisation's
communication structure. The book paraphrases the consequence: the way we
organise teams has a powerful effect on the software we produce and on our
architectural and production outcomes.

The reason is practical. To build two parts of a system that work closely
together, the people building them must communicate closely. If they are
in different teams with a formal interface between them, the parts they
build will also have a formal interface between them. If one team builds
everything, the parts will tend to be tightly entangled, because nothing
forces them apart. The system grows along the lines of who talks to whom.

That cuts both ways. If an organisation has separate front-end, back-end
and database teams, it tends to produce a three-layer system where every
feature needs all three teams to change something and coordinate a release.
If it has teams organised around customer-facing capabilities, each owning
everything needed for that capability, it tends to produce services that
can change independently.

The book uses this to argue for deliberate design. Because the
organisation will shape the system anyway, we should choose an
organisation that shapes it in the way we want: teams aligned to parts of
the system that can be built, tested and deployed independently. This is
sometimes called the inverse Conway manoeuvre: design the teams to get the
architecture you want.

#### The communication arithmetic

Part of why team structure matters so much is that communication paths
grow much faster than team size. In a group of n people, the number of
possible one-to-one paths is n times n minus one, divided by two. A small
script, run with node, shows how quickly that grows:

```js
// Communication paths grow quadratically with team size: n * (n - 1) / 2.
for (const n of [3, 5, 8, 12, 20, 50]) {
  console.log(`${String(n).padStart(3)} people -> ${String((n * (n - 1)) / 2).padStart(5)} paths`);
}
// Splitting 20 people into four teams of five that talk through one interface each:
const inside = 4 * ((5 * 4) / 2);
const between = (4 * 3) / 2;
console.log(`four teams of five: ${inside} paths inside teams + ${between} team-to-team interfaces`);
```

It prints:

```text
  3 people ->     3 paths
  5 people ->    10 paths
  8 people ->    28 paths
 12 people ->    66 paths
 20 people ->   190 paths
 50 people ->  1225 paths
four teams of five: 40 paths inside teams + 6 team-to-team interfaces
```

Twenty people working as one group have nearly two hundred possible paths,
far too many to keep everyone informed. Split into four teams of five with
clear interfaces between them, the same people have forty paths inside
teams and a handful of team-to-team interfaces. That is Conway's Law in
arithmetic: the teams communicate along a small number of interfaces, and
the system they build will have a small number of interfaces too, which is
what makes independent change possible.

### Worked example

**Simple.** When I built a project with a friend, I wrote the front end
and they wrote the API. We agreed a JSON format early and rarely talked
after that. The result was a clean boundary between the two halves, exactly
where our conversation boundary was. The parts we both touched, the
authentication flow, were the messiest code in the project, because neither
of us owned it.

**Realistic.** A company has front-end, back-end and database teams. Every
feature needs work from all three, and a coordinated release. The value
stream map shows most of the lead time is waiting between teams. The
company reorganises a few product areas into cross-functional teams that
each own a capability end to end. Over the following year, those areas'
systems are split along the same lines, and their features stop waiting
for other teams.

### Step by step

Using Conway's Law deliberately:

1. **Draw the current team structure** and who communicates with whom.
2. **Draw the current system architecture** and its main boundaries.
3. **Notice where they match**: team boundaries usually show up as system
   boundaries.
4. **Decide the architecture you want**: parts that can change and deploy
   independently.
5. **Design teams to match it**: one team per independently deployable
   part.
6. **Expect the system to follow** the new structure over time, and help it
   with the architecture work later in this chapter.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to design a target architecture, for example
microservices, while keeping the old team structure. The old structure
pulls the system back: services that need three teams to change end up as
a distributed version of the old layered system. The fix is to change team
boundaries and architecture together.

### In real systems

Organisations that move to services often reorganise into teams that each
own a few services end to end. The Team Topologies approach, which builds
on Conway's Law, names stream-aligned teams, platform teams, enabling teams
and complicated-subsystem teams, and designs their interactions on purpose.

## Organisational archetypes

### In one sentence

Organisations are usually structured in one of three ways, functional,
matrix or market-oriented, and each optimises for something different:
cost and expertise, a compromise, or speed and responsiveness.

### The idea

The book describes three common archetypes.

**Functional organisations** group people by speciality: all developers in
one department, all testers in another, all operations staff in a third,
each with its own manager. This optimises for expertise, career paths and
cost efficiency: specialists learn from each other, and resources can be
shared across many projects. But any piece of work that needs several
specialities has to pass between departments, which means queues and
hand-offs.

**Matrix organisations** combine the two: people report to a functional
manager for their speciality and work on a product or project team under
another manager. The aim is to get both expertise and focus. In practice,
matrix organisations often add complexity: people have two bosses with
different priorities, and decisions need both to agree.

**Market-oriented organisations** group people by customer, product or
service. Each team has all the skills it needs to deliver its product:
development, testing, operations, security. This optimises for speed and
responsiveness: work rarely leaves the team, so it rarely waits. The cost
is some duplication of skills across teams, and specialists who may feel
isolated from others in their field.

The book's position is that many technology organisations are heavily
functional, which is why their value streams are full of hand-offs, and
that DevOps outcomes come more easily from market orientation. But it does
not say functional organisations are doomed; a later section describes how
they can work well too.

### Worked example

**Simple.** In a study group, we tried splitting by skill: one person
researched, one wrote, one made diagrams. Every piece of work passed between
three people and waited each time. When we switched to each person owning a
whole topic, including research, writing and diagrams, topics finished much
faster, even though each person was less expert in some parts.

**Realistic.** A company's operations department serves thirty
development teams. Every environment, firewall change and deploy goes
through its ticket queue, and operations prioritises by whoever shouts
loudest. The company embeds an operations engineer in each of its largest
product teams and builds self-service tooling for the rest. Work that used
to wait days in the queue now happens within the team.

### Step by step

Identifying the archetype in my organisation:

1. **Ask who a developer reports to**: a development manager (functional)
   or a product lead (market)?
2. **Trace one feature**: how many departments does it cross?
3. **Ask how operations and testing are staffed**: shared central teams or
   embedded?
4. **Note the trade-off**: where is efficiency gained, and where is speed
   lost?
5. **Compare with the value stream map**: are the longest waits at the
   departmental boundaries?

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to treat archetypes as good or bad in themselves. A
functional organisation can be fast if hand-offs are made cheap through
self-service and automation; a market-oriented one can be slow if teams
still depend on shared services with long queues. The fix is to judge by
the flow of work, not by the org chart.

### In real systems

Most large organisations are mixtures: market-oriented product teams
supported by functional platform, security and data teams. The question is
whether the functional parts offer self-service and quick help, or long
queues.

## Optimise for speed with market-oriented teams, or make functional orientation work

### In one sentence

Overly functional organisations create long queues and lost context, so
the book recommends market-oriented teams that own their work end to end,
while showing that functional organisations can also work when shared
groups serve teams quickly.

### The idea

#### Problems caused by overly functional orientation

When work has to pass through many specialist departments, three problems
appear. First, **queues**: each department has its own backlog and
priorities, so work waits at each hand-off, and the waits add up. Second,
**lost context**: the person doing each step knows only their part, not
why the work matters or how it will be used, so they cannot make good
local decisions or notice when something is wrong. Third, **escalation**:
when everyone's work is urgent and departments set their own priorities,
the only way to get something done quickly is to escalate to a manager,
which makes managers the real scheduler and turns every week into a series
of fights over priority.

The book also notes the human cost: specialists at the end of a long chain,
often operations, receive work they did not shape and are blamed when it
fails. That is chapter 1's downward spiral in organisational form.

#### Market-oriented teams

The book's main recommendation is market orientation: teams responsible
not only for building features but for testing, securing, deploying and
running them in production. Such teams can take an idea from request to
customer without waiting in other teams' queues. To make this possible, the
book suggests embedding the needed skills in each team, such as an
operations engineer or a tester, and making shared functions available as
self-service tools.

This is not about eliminating specialists. It is about putting enough of
each skill inside the team that ordinary work never leaves it, and
reserving specialist groups for the unusual.

#### Making functional orientation work

The book is careful to say that functional organisations can achieve good
outcomes too. The key is how the functional groups behave. If a central
operations group responds quickly, offers self-service platforms instead of
tickets, prioritises the organisation's goals over its own local measures,
and has a culture of high trust, development teams can move quickly while
relying on it. The book describes organisations with centralised
operations that still deploy often, because their operations groups act as
service providers with fast, automated interfaces rather than as gatekeepers.

The real lesson is that the structure matters less than the flow of work
across it. Long queues at boundaries are the problem; whether those
boundaries are departments or team edges matters less if crossing them is
fast.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I depended on a friend to review my deploy scripts before I
could run them, and every review waited for them to be free. We changed it:
they wrote a checklist and a linter rule that caught the issues they
usually found, and reviewed only unusual changes. My deploys stopped
waiting, and they spent less time reviewing.

**Realistic.** A company keeps a central database team but changes how it
works. Instead of handling schema changes by ticket, it provides a
migration tool with automated checks, documentation and office hours.
Product teams run their own routine migrations; the database team reviews
only changes the tool flags as risky. The database team's queue shrinks,
and product teams stop waiting days for routine changes.

### Step by step

1. **Find the queues at boundaries** on the value stream map.
2. **For each, ask why work leaves the team**: missing skill, missing
   access, or a required approval?
3. **Missing skill**: embed it in the team, or teach it.
4. **Missing access**: provide self-service with guardrails.
5. **Required approval**: automate the check, and keep human review for
   the unusual.
6. **Measure the shared group on the outcomes** of the teams it serves.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to reorganise the chart and leave the queues: a team
is labelled "cross-functional" but still files tickets to central groups
for every deploy. The fix is to measure and remove the waits, whatever the
chart looks like.

### In real systems

Platform teams are the modern form of functional groups as service
providers: they build internal platforms that product teams use on their
own. Good ones treat product teams as customers and measure success by how
quickly those teams can ship.

## Testing, operations and security as everyone's job, every day

### In one sentence

Quality, availability and security are not the job of separate
departments at the end of the line; they are part of every team's daily
work.

### The idea

In a traditional organisation, developers write code, testers find bugs
in it, operations keeps it running, and security reviews it before
release. Each concern belongs to a department, and each department sees the
work only after the developers are done. That arrangement has two effects.
Problems are found late, when they are expensive to fix. And developers do
not feel the consequences of quality, operability or security problems, so
they do not learn to prevent them.

The book argues that these concerns must be shared. Developers write
automated tests as part of writing code. Teams own the operability of
their services and share in on-call. Security is built into daily work
through automated checks, shared libraries and early involvement. The
specialists do not disappear; they become coaches and toolmakers who help
every team do this well, rather than gatekeepers who inspect work at the
end.

This is the Second Way from chapter 3 in organisational form: feedback
reaches the people who can act on it, quickly, and quality is built in at
the source rather than inspected at the end.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I used to "test later". Bugs I found weeks after writing code
took far longer to fix, because I had forgotten the details. Writing a test
as I write each function means I find most problems while the code is
still in my head.

**Realistic.** A team that used to hand releases to a QA department now
writes its own automated tests, with a test specialist who pairs with
developers and maintains the test framework. The team also joins the
on-call rotation for its service. Within a few months, the number of
production problems falls, because developers who are paged at night
for a fragile feature make it robust.

### Step by step

1. **Make automated tests part of done**, not a later phase.
2. **Put the team on call** for what it builds, with support from
   operations.
3. **Add security checks to the pipeline**: dependency scanning, static
   analysis, secret detection.
4. **Turn specialists into coaches and toolmakers.**
5. **Share the measures**: the team sees its defects, incidents and
   vulnerabilities.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to hand quality, operations or security to
developers without support: "you are on call now" without runbooks,
telemetry or training. That produces burnout, not ownership. The fix is to
pair the responsibility with specialist help and tooling.

### In real systems

"You build it, you run it" is a common phrase for this model. Teams carry
pagers for their own services, while platform and security teams supply
the tools and guardrails that make that responsibility manageable.

## Enable every team member to be a generalist

### In one sentence

Teams flow better when their members have broad skills across the
stream as well as depth in one area, so the book encourages T-shaped and
E-shaped people over narrow specialists.

### The idea

The book uses a vocabulary of skill shapes.

- **I-shaped** people have deep expertise in one area and little in others. They are valuable, but they create bottlenecks: work that needs their skill waits for them, and they cannot help elsewhere when they are free.
- **T-shaped** people have deep expertise in one area and broad, working knowledge across many others. They can help with adjacent work, understand the whole stream, and communicate well with other specialists.
- **E-shaped** people have deep expertise in a few areas, experience across many, proven execution, and a habit of continual learning.

The argument connects to flow. In a team of I-shaped specialists, work
queues for the one person who can do each step, exactly the queue problem
of functional organisations, just inside a team. In a team of T-shaped
people, more people can pick up any given piece of work, so it waits less,
and people understand enough of each other's work to avoid surprises.

The book also links generalism to growth. Encouraging people to learn
across the stream, through pairing, rotation and training, makes the team
more resilient and the work more interesting. It recommends valuing
learning over existing skills when hiring and developing people, which
echoes the Third Way.

<Figure caption="Skill shapes. Read each shape as breadth across the top and depth downward: I is deep and narrow, T adds breadth, E has several deep areas plus breadth." art={`
     I-shaped            T-shaped                E-shaped
                    ________________        ________________
        |                  |                  |     |     |
        |                  |                  |     |     |
        |                  |                  |     |     |
        |                  |                        |
   deep, narrow      broad + one deep     broad + several deep,
                                          execution, learning
`} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I am strongest at front-end work. Learning enough about
servers, CI and databases to set up and debug my own deploys made me
T-shaped. Now a broken deploy is an hour's problem, not a week waiting for
help.

**Realistic.** A team has one person who understands its infrastructure.
Every infrastructure change waits for them, and when they are on holiday,
nothing ships. The team starts pairing: every infrastructure change is done
by that person with someone else. Within a few months, three people can
make routine infrastructure changes, and the expert spends more time on the
hard problems.

### Step by step

1. **Map who can do each kind of work** on the team.
2. **Find the single points**: work only one person can do.
3. **Pair on that work** so knowledge spreads.
4. **Rotate responsibilities**, such as on-call or releases.
5. **Value learning** in reviews and hiring, not only existing expertise.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to read "generalist" as "no specialists". Teams still
need depth. The aim is breadth on top of depth, so that ordinary work does
not wait for one person.

### In real systems

Pairing, mob programming, rotation of on-call and release duties, and
internal training are common ways teams spread skills. Many teams keep a
simple skills matrix to spot single points of knowledge.

## Fund services and products, not projects

### In one sentence

Funding temporary projects breaks up teams and leaves nobody responsible
for the result afterwards, so the book recommends funding long-lived
teams that own a product or service and are measured on its outcomes.

### The idea

In project funding, money is allocated to a project with a fixed scope,
budget and end date. A team is assembled, builds the thing, and disbands
when the project ends. The software is handed to someone else to run and
maintain. Success is measured by whether the project finished on time and
on budget.

The book argues this undermines good outcomes. When the team disbands, the
knowledge leaves with it. The people who run the software did not build it,
and the people who built it never feel its operational problems. And
"on time and on budget" says nothing about whether customers got value.

Instead, the book recommends funding **products and services**: a stable
team owns a product over its life, with an ongoing budget. Success is
measured by outcomes: customer value, revenue, reliability, the
organisation's goals. The team improves the product continuously and keeps
the knowledge. This aligns with market orientation and with "you build it,
you run it".

  ["Temporary team, disbanded at the end", "Stable team over the product's life"],
  ["Fixed scope and end date", "Ongoing budget and evolving priorities"],
  ["Measured on time and budget", "Measured on customer and business outcomes"],
  ["Software handed to someone else to run", "Builders also run it"],
  ["Knowledge leaves with the team", "Knowledge stays with the product"],
]} />

### Worked example

**Simple.** I treated each feature of my site as a "project": build it,
call it done, never look again. Bugs accumulated in features I had stopped
thinking about. When I started treating the whole site as one product I
own, with regular time for its health, the old features stopped decaying.

**Realistic.** A company funds a team for a twelve-month project to build
a new customer portal. At the end, the team moves to other projects and the
portal is handed to a support group. Within months, its defects pile up,
and the support group cannot change it confidently. Next time, the company
funds a permanent portal team with a yearly budget and measures it on
customer satisfaction and adoption.

### Step by step

1. **List the products and services** customers use.
2. **Assign a stable team** to each, or a few small ones to a team.
3. **Fund the team**, not a list of features.
4. **Agree outcome measures** for each product.
5. **Let the team set priorities** within those outcomes, with its product owner.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to call projects "products" while keeping project
behaviour: teams still disband, and measures are still time and budget.
The fix is stable teams and outcome measures.

### In real systems

Many organisations have moved to persistent product teams with yearly or
quarterly funding, sometimes grouped into product lines. The shift often
requires changes in finance and budgeting processes, not only in
engineering.

On customer and business outcomes rather than on finishing on time and on

## Design team boundaries and loosely coupled architecture

### In one sentence

Team boundaries should follow the boundaries of a loosely coupled
architecture, so each team can build, test and deploy its part safely
without coordinating with every other team.

### The idea

Conway's Law says the system will follow the teams. The book adds the
other half: the teams can only work independently if the system lets them.
If the architecture is **tightly coupled**, where any change can break
something in another part, every team must coordinate with every other
team, test everything together and release together. Developers become
afraid to change anything, because they cannot know what will break, and
productivity falls as the organisation grows.

A **loosely coupled** architecture is one where components communicate
through well-defined interfaces and can be changed, tested and deployed
independently. The book describes the qualities it wants: a team can make
large changes to its own part without permission from anyone outside the
team, and without depending on other teams to change their parts; it can
test its part in isolation; and it can deploy it on its own schedule.

The book highlights **service-oriented architectures** as one way to get
there: the system is made of services, each with its own data and a clear
API, and services interact only through those APIs. Another tool is the
**bounded context** from domain-driven design: a part of the domain, such
as ordering, billing or shipping, with its own model and language, and a
clear boundary. Aligning services and teams with bounded contexts means
each team owns a coherent piece of the business.

The book stresses that loose coupling is about the *properties*, not about
any particular technology. A well-structured single application with clear
internal modules can have many of the benefits; a set of microservices
that all share one database and must be deployed together has none of
them.

#### Testing the coupling

A practical test of coupling is to ask, for a recent change: how many
teams had to be involved, how many components had to be redeployed, and
did any tests outside the changed component have to run? If the answers are
"one, one, no", the architecture is loosely coupled for that kind of
change. If the answers are "several, several, the whole suite", it is not,
whatever the diagram says.

#### How teams should interact

Loose coupling is also a question of how teams work together, not only
how their code does. The Team Topologies approach by Matthew Skelton and
Manuel Pais, which builds directly on Conway's Law, describes three ways
teams can interact, each suited to a different situation.

- **Collaboration**: two teams work closely together for a period, for example to discover a new boundary or build something neither could alone. It is high-bandwidth but expensive, so it should be temporary.
- **X-as-a-service**: one team provides something, such as a platform or an API, that another uses with little need to talk. It is cheap to sustain and suits stable, well-understood boundaries.
- **Facilitating**: one team helps another learn a skill or adopt a practice, then steps back.

The useful lesson is that interactions should be chosen on purpose and
changed as the situation changes. Two teams that collaborate permanently
are, in Conway's terms, one team with a boundary drawn through the middle,
and their code will be entangled to match. Two teams that have settled
their boundary should move to an as-a-service interaction, so each can
change its own side without meetings.

### Worked example

**Simple.** My site's build script and content shared a lot of global
state, so changing the build broke posts in surprising ways. I separated
them: content became plain files with a defined frontmatter format, and
the build read only that format. After that I could change the build
without touching content, and the other way round.

**Realistic.** An e-commerce company's checkout, inventory and shipping
code all read and write the same database tables. Every schema change
needs all three teams to agree and release together. The company defines
bounded contexts for each, gives each service its own data store, and
replaces shared tables with APIs and events. It moves one context at a
time. Once complete, each team deploys on its own schedule.

### Step by step

1. **Identify the bounded contexts** of the business domain.
2. **Assign each to a team**, so team and context boundaries match.
3. **Give each context its own data**, accessed by others only through an
   API or events.
4. **Make the interfaces explicit and versioned**, with contract tests.
5. **Remove shared deploys**: each service deploys on its own.
6. **Run the coupling check** on real changes to see progress.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to split a system into services without splitting
the data or the release. The result is a distributed monolith: all the
complexity of services, none of the independence. The fix is to judge
coupling by whether teams can change and deploy alone.

### In real systems

Domain-driven design's bounded contexts are a common guide for service
and team boundaries. Contract tests, API versioning and event-driven
integration are the usual tools for keeping services independent.

## Keep team sizes small

### In one sentence

Small teams, often described as teams that two pizzas can feed, keep
communication manageable, preserve a shared understanding of the system,
and give people clear ownership.

### The idea

The book describes Amazon's well-known two-pizza team rule: a team should
be small enough to be fed by two pizzas, which usually means somewhere
between five and ten people. The rule is about more than food. The book
lists several benefits of small teams.

- **Clear understanding**: a small team can understand the whole system it owns, which is only possible if that system is small enough.
- **Limited growth**: the team's part of the system cannot grow without limit, because the team would not be able to manage it, which pushes toward small, well-bounded services.
- **Autonomy**: a small team owning its service can make decisions without coordinating with many others.
- **Leadership development**: small teams give more people the chance to lead, which develops leaders across the organisation.

The communication arithmetic from the start of the chapter shows why
size matters. Doubling a team from five to ten people more than quadruples
its communication paths. Beyond a certain size, a team stops being a team
and becomes a group with sub-teams and coordination overhead.

Small teams depend on everything else in the chapter. A small team can only
own a service end to end if the service is loosely coupled, the team has
the needed skills, and it is funded as a lasting product team.

### Worked example

**Simple.** In a group project of eleven people, meetings took most of
our time and decisions took days. When we split into two groups of five
and six with one agreed interface, each group moved faster and the only
cross-group meeting was a short weekly sync.

**Realistic.** A product team grows to eighteen people. Stand-ups take half
an hour, nobody understands the whole codebase, and decisions stall. The
team splits into three teams of six, each owning one bounded context of the
product, with clear APIs between them. Each team's stand-up is short again,
and each can deploy its part independently.

### Step by step

1. **Count the team**: if it is above about ten, look for a split.
2. **Split along a boundary in the system**, ideally a bounded context.
3. **Give each new team end-to-end ownership** of its part.
4. **Define interfaces** between the teams' parts.
5. **Watch for re-coupling**: shared components that pull teams back
   together.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is to split a team without splitting the system: two
small teams that both change the same code and must release together. They
have the same coordination cost as before, plus a team boundary. The fix is
to split along a real boundary in the architecture.

### In real systems

Many organisations cap team size at around eight to ten people and treat
growth beyond that as a signal to split. Splits are planned with the
architecture: the team boundary and the service boundary move together.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 7, How to Design Our Organization and Architecture with Conway's Law in Mind">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the lesson is Conway's Law itself: systems end up
shaped like the teams that build them. If three teams must cooperate on
every feature, the system will have three layers that change together. If
one team owns a whole capability, the system will have a part that can
change on its own. So when I see a messy boundary in code, I can often
find a messy boundary between people behind it.

I also learn the arithmetic: communication paths grow with the square of
team size, which is why big teams slow down.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I can compare organisational structures.
Functional organisations optimise for expertise and cost but create queues
at every hand-off. Market-oriented teams optimise for speed by owning work
end to end. Matrix organisations try both and often add confusion. I know
that structure matters less than flow: a functional group that offers fast
self-service can work well, and a "cross-functional" team that still files
tickets for everything does not.

I can also explain why quality, operations and security should be part of
every team's daily work, and why T-shaped people reduce queues inside a
team.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I design teams and architecture together. I align
teams with bounded contexts, give each its own data and deploy, and judge
coupling by real changes: how many teams, how many deploys, how many test
suites. I argue for product funding so teams are stable and keep their
knowledge, and for small teams so each can understand what it owns. I see
these as one system: small teams need loose coupling, generalist skills and
stable funding, and each of those supports the others.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases include: a system that genuinely needs deep specialists, such
as a cryptography library or a complex search engine, where a
specialist team owning a complicated subsystem makes sense; regulatory
rules that require separation of duties, which chapter 22 shows can be met
with automated controls rather than separate departments; very small
organisations where one team owns everything, so Conway's Law applies to
modules inside one codebase; and shared platforms that many teams depend
on, which must be designed as self-service products to avoid becoming a new
queue.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases include: a reorganisation that changes teams faster than the
architecture can follow, leaving teams owning code they cannot change
independently; a bounded context that turns out to be wrong after a year,
so the team boundary must move with it; a two-pizza team whose service
grows until it no longer fits, signalling a split; and teams that become
too autonomous, choosing incompatible tools so that nobody can help anyone
else, which a light set of shared standards or a paved road addresses.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

At production level, each stream-aligned team owns a few services end to
end: code, tests, pipeline, telemetry, on-call. Services have their own
data and versioned APIs with contract tests. Teams deploy independently,
many times a week. Platform teams provide self-service infrastructure and
measure themselves on product teams' lead time. Funding is by product, with
outcome measures. The organisation regularly checks coupling by looking at
which changes needed more than one team.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is that org charts are architecture decisions. Every time I
draw a team boundary, I am drawing a likely system boundary, and every time
I draw a system boundary, I am deciding who will need to talk to whom. I
look at the flow of work across boundaries rather than at labels, and I
value breadth of skill and lasting ownership over efficiency on paper.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

In system design terms, this chapter is the bridge between people and
architecture. I design services around bounded contexts, give each its
own data and deploy, keep interfaces explicit and versioned, and make sure
each service has one owning team small enough to understand it. When a
service needs two teams to change it routinely, I treat that as a design
problem: either the boundary is wrong or the team structure is.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

The strategy is the inverse Conway manoeuvre, applied gradually. Decide
the architecture that would let teams work independently; shape teams to
match it; give them the skills, funding and ownership to run their part;
and move the architecture toward loose coupling one bounded context at a
time. Use the value stream map to choose where to start: the boundaries
with the longest waits. Keep teams small and stable, and keep shared groups
as fast self-service providers.

Done when each of my value stream's main parts has one small team that can
change, test, deploy and run it without waiting for any other team.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>Does Conway's Law mean microservices are always the answer?</summary>

No. It means team and system boundaries should match. A small organisation
with one team may be best served by one well-modularised application. What
matters is that teams can change and deploy their part independently.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the right team size?</summary>

The book uses the two-pizza rule, roughly five to ten people. The real test
is whether the team can understand what it owns and coordinate without
heavy process.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Do specialists have a place in market-oriented organisations?</summary>

Yes. Some are embedded in teams, some run platforms or complicated
subsystems, and some act as coaches. The aim is that ordinary work does
not wait for them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I change the architecture without stopping feature work?</summary>

Incrementally: one bounded context at a time, using patterns such as the
strangler fig from chapter 13, and the reserved capacity for technical debt
from chapter 6.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is a matrix organisation always bad?</summary>

Not always, but it often creates conflicting priorities. If people have two
managers, the organisation needs clear rules for which priorities win,
or work stalls.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does product funding work with annual budgets?</summary>

The budget funds teams rather than lists of features. Each team gets an
ongoing allocation and outcome goals, and priorities within it are set by
the team and its product owner, revisited regularly.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does this chapter mean for someone working alone?</summary>

Conway's Law applies to modules: I can keep parts of my code loosely
coupled with clear interfaces, so I can change one without touching the
others. And being T-shaped lets me run my whole stream myself.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Conway's Law: systems mirror the communication structure of the organisations that build them; design teams and architecture together.
- Communication paths grow roughly with the square of team size, so large groups need splitting along real boundaries.
- Functional organisations optimise for cost and expertise, market-oriented ones for speed; the flow of work across boundaries matters more than the chart.
- Functional groups work when they act as fast, self-service providers rather than ticket-queue gatekeepers.
- Quality, operations and security are every team's daily work, with specialists as coaches and toolmakers.
- T-shaped and E-shaped people reduce queues inside teams.
- Fund long-lived products and services, measured on outcomes, rather than temporary projects.
- Loose coupling means teams can change, test and deploy their part alone; bounded contexts help define the parts.
- Keep teams small, and split them along architectural boundaries.

## Glossary

- **Conway's Law**: the observation that system designs mirror the communication structures of the organisations that produce them.
- **Inverse Conway manoeuvre**: designing team structure deliberately to produce a desired architecture.
- **Functional organisation**: one grouped by speciality, such as development, testing and operations departments.
- **Matrix organisation**: one where people report both to a functional manager and to a product or project lead.
- **Market-oriented organisation**: one grouped by product, customer or service, with each team holding the skills to deliver.
- **I-shaped, T-shaped, E-shaped**: skill profiles with one deep area only; one deep area plus breadth; several deep areas plus breadth, execution and learning.
- **Project funding**: funding a temporary team for fixed scope and end date.
- **Product funding**: funding a stable team that owns a product over its life, measured on outcomes.
- **Loose coupling**: components that interact through clear interfaces and can change and deploy independently.
- **Bounded context**: a part of a business domain with its own model and language and a clear boundary.
- **Distributed monolith**: services that still share data or deploys, so they cannot change independently.
- **Two-pizza team**: a team small enough to be fed by two pizzas, roughly five to ten people.
- **Platform team**: a team that provides self-service tools and infrastructure for other teams.
