Many projects fail before a line of code is written, because nobody
really knew what was needed, the problem looked impossible, or people
worked in isolation. This chapter is about the work at the very start, and
it keeps coming back as the project goes on: discovering requirements,
untangling hard problems, working closely with others, and staying agile
in practice rather than in name.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- treat requirements as something learned through feedback, not handed over;
- separate a requirement from the policy behind it, and keep policy in data;
- keep a project glossary and handle scope growth openly;
- find the real constraints of a hard problem, and drop the imagined ones;
- work with others through pairing, mobbing and close contact with users;
- apply the book's simple loop for agility to any process.

What I should already know: nothing technical beyond basic TypeScript for
the two short examples. It helps to have read the ideas on prototypes and
tracer bullets in
<a href={withBase('/books/the-pragmatic-programmer/02-a-pragmatic-approach/')}>chapter 2</a>,
because requirements are discovered with them. The
<a href={withBase('/books/the-pragmatic-programmer/07-while-you-are-coding/')}>previous chapter</a>
was about habits while coding; this one steps back to before coding
starts.

Reading time: about 70 minutes for this page, plus two hours for the
exercises.

## Topic 45. The Requirements Pit

### In one sentence

Nobody knows exactly what they want at the start, so requirements are
discovered together, through questions, examples and feedback, rather than
collected from a document.

### The idea

#### No one knows exactly what they want

The book starts with an observation I recognise from every project:
requirements rarely arrive clear and complete. People describe a problem
in terms of a solution they imagine, leave out cases they have not thought
of, and change their minds when they see something working. That is not
a failing of the client; it is how people think. The book's tip:
programmers help people understand what they want.

A request is better treated as an invitation to explore. Its literal
words are a starting point. Behind them is a need, and often several, that
the request only partly describes.

#### Requirements are a process

So gathering requirements is a loop. I hear the request, ask questions,
reflect back what I understood, often with an example, mock-up or
prototype, and listen to the response. Each round, both sides understand a
little more. The book's tip: requirements are learned in a feedback loop.

That links to chapter 2. Prototypes and tracer bullets are requirement
tools: showing someone something real, however rough, gets better feedback
than any amount of description.

#### Walk in the client's shoes

The book suggests a simple technique that is often skipped: spend time
doing the user's job. Sit with them for a day, use their current tools,
watch where they struggle. The tip: work with a user to think like a user.
It builds trust, and shows needs that nobody would think to mention,
because to them it is just how the work is done.

#### Requirements versus policy

The book draws an important line. A statement like "only HR staff may view
salary records" mixes a requirement, that access to salary records is
restricted, with a policy, which roles qualify. Policies change far more
often than requirements. If the policy is coded as an `if` statement, every
policy change is a code change.

The tip: policy is metadata. Implement the general requirement, access
control by role and action, and keep the specific policy in data that the
business can change. This is chapter 5's configuration idea applied to
business rules.

#### Requirements versus reality

The book also warns about requirements that are technically met but miss
the point. A system can do exactly what was written and still be rejected
because it does not fit how people actually work. The fix is the same
feedback loop: show real software to real users early.

#### Documenting requirements

The book is sceptical of large requirements documents. Clients rarely read
them, and they freeze an understanding that is still changing. It
suggests short descriptions, such as index cards or user stories, that
capture a need and serve as a promise of a conversation. Detailed
documents are, at best, for developers, as a record of what was
understood.

It warns against overspecification too: describing the solution in such
detail that design choices are made too early, by people without the
information to make them.

#### Scope growth and the glossary

Requirements grow. The book's image is the one extra small thing, accepted
again and again, until the project is overwhelmed. The answer is not to
refuse change, but to make each addition visible: what it adds, what it
costs, what it delays. With the feedback loop and short iterations, the
client sees the trade-off and decides.

Finally, the tip: maintain a project glossary. Every term with a specific
meaning in the project, such as "customer", "order" or "active", is
defined in one place and used consistently by users and developers. It
prevents the costly confusion of one word meaning two things, which
chapter 7's naming topic also warned about.

#### Requirements and the developer's day

All of this can sound like work for meetings, but most of it happens in
small moments while coding. I reach a branch in the logic the story did
not mention, such as what to show when a user has no orders yet.

That is
a requirement question. The pragmatic move is to ask it right away, in a
message or a quick call, rather than guess and move on. Each answer goes
into the examples and, if it introduces a term, the glossary.

Over a project, hundreds of these small questions shape the product more
than the original specification did. Developers who ask them, and record
the answers, are where much of a product's quality comes from.

### Worked example

**Simple.** Separating policy from requirement in access control. The
code implements "an action is allowed for some roles"; which roles is data.

```ts
// Policy is metadata: the requirement is "only some staff may view salaries";
// which roles qualify is policy, so it lives in data the business can change.
type Role = 'hr' | 'manager' | 'engineer' | 'finance';
const policy: Record<string, Role[]> = { 'salary:view': ['hr', 'finance'] }; // loaded from config
const can = (role: Role, action: string) => (policy[action] ?? []).includes(role);

console.log('before:', (['hr', 'manager', 'finance'] as Role[]).map(r => `${r}=${can(r, 'salary:view')}`).join(' '));
policy['salary:view'].push('manager'); // the policy changes; no code change
console.log('after: ', (['hr', 'manager', 'finance'] as Role[]).map(r => `${r}=${can(r, 'salary:view')}`).join(' '));
console.log('unknown action:', can('hr', 'salary:edit'));
```

The output:

```text
before: hr=true manager=false finance=true
after:  hr=true manager=true finance=true
unknown action: false
```

When the company decides managers may see their team's salaries, the
change is in the policy data. The last line shows a secure default from
chapter 7: an action with no policy is denied.

**Second example.** A request in the spirit of the book's own: "orders of
500 rupees or more get free shipping." It sounds complete. Writing it as
code with test cases immediately raises questions:

```ts
// Turning a vague requirement into questions by enumerating cases.
type Order = { items: number; subtotal: number; digitalOnly: boolean; country: string };
const freeShipping = (o: Order) => o.subtotal >= 500 && !o.digitalOnly && o.country === 'IN';
const cases: [string, Order][] = [
  ['exactly 500', { items: 2, subtotal: 500, digitalOnly: false, country: 'IN' }],
  ['499.99', { items: 1, subtotal: 499.99, digitalOnly: false, country: 'IN' }],
  ['digital only', { items: 1, subtotal: 900, digitalOnly: true, country: 'IN' }],
  ['abroad', { items: 3, subtotal: 2000, digitalOnly: false, country: 'NP' }],
];
for (const [name, o] of cases) console.log(`${name.padEnd(13)} free shipping: ${freeShipping(o)}`);
```

The output:

```text
exactly 500   free shipping: true
499.99        free shipping: false
digital only  free shipping: false
abroad        free shipping: false
```

Every one of those lines is a guess I made, and each is a question for the
client. Is 500 before or after discounts?

Including tax? What about an
order of only digital items, which have no shipping at all? Orders to
other countries? The table becomes the agenda for a fifteen-minute
conversation that saves a week of rework.

**Realistic.** A hospital asks for "a dashboard showing bed availability".
Spending a shift with the ward coordinator shows the real need: not a
count of empty beds, but which beds will be free in the next four hours,
because discharge planning is where the delays are. The first prototype,
shown on day three, is a list of expected discharges, and the dashboard
that ships looks nothing like the original request.

#### A requirements conversation, worked through

Here is how the free-shipping example might go as a conversation. I start
by restating: "So any order of 500 rupees or more ships free?" The
product owner agrees. I bring the table and ask about the first guess:
"Is that before or after a discount code?" She thinks, then says after,
because the goal is to encourage larger baskets, and a discount that drops
an order below 500 should lose free shipping.

That answer reveals the real need, which is basket size, and it changes the
next answers. Digital-only orders are excluded, since there is nothing to
ship.

Orders abroad are excluded for now, because the courier contract
only covers India. Each answer becomes a row in the table and then a test.
The whole conversation takes fifteen minutes, and the glossary gains one
entry: "subtotal" means the order value after discounts and before tax.

Without the conversation, I would have built something plausible and
wrong in at least two of the four cases, and found out from a customer
complaint.

#### User stories and acceptance criteria

The book's preference for short descriptions fits the common practice of
user stories. A story names a user, a want and a reason: as a shopper, I
want free shipping on larger orders, so that I am encouraged to buy more.
It is deliberately incomplete. Its acceptance criteria, a short list of
concrete examples like the table above, are added through conversation and
say when the story is done.

The value is that the story stays small enough to read in seconds, while
the examples carry the precision. When the business changes its mind, a
row changes, not a forty-page document.

1. **Restate the request in my own words**, and confirm it.
2. **Ask why**, until I reach the underlying need.
3. **Turn it into concrete examples and edge cases**, and ask about each.
4. **Separate policy from requirement**, and plan for policy as data.
5. **Show something real soon**, and repeat the loop.
6. **Add new terms to the glossary**, and make scope changes visible.

#### Questions that uncover the need

Some questions work on almost any request. What happens today, without
this feature? Who will use it, and how often?

What would make it a
success, and how would we know? What is the cost of getting it wrong?
What is the simplest version that would help? Each one moves the
conversation from the solution the person imagined toward the problem they
actually have.

It also helps to ask for a real example rather than a general rule.
"Show me the last report you made by hand" reveals the columns, the
sorting, the odd manual adjustments and the person who reads it, none of
which appear in "we need a monthly report". General descriptions hide
detail; specific examples reveal it.

#### Reading between the lines

The book makes the point that a request often contains an unstated
solution. "Add a button to export to Excel" may really mean "I need to
share this data with finance every Friday". The better answer might be a
scheduled email, or giving finance access to the dashboard. I do not
ignore the literal request, but I check whether it is the best way to meet
the need, and I offer alternatives when it is not.

Doing this well is a large part of what makes a developer valuable beyond
writing code. Two developers can build the same feature equally well;
the one who found the real need builds something people actually use.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is treating the first description as the
specification and building it exactly, then being surprised when the
client is unhappy. The fix is the feedback loop: examples, questions and
early demos.

Another mistake is the opposite: asking the client to specify every detail
up front in a long document. The fix is short stories plus conversation,
and deferring detail until it is needed.

A third mistake is treating developers as order-takers who should not
question the request. The book's view is the reverse: a developer who
asks good questions is doing the job, not avoiding it. The fix is to make
questions normal, for example by ending every planning discussion with
"what are we not sure about yet?"

### In real systems

User stories with acceptance criteria, example mapping sessions where
business and developers list rules and examples on cards, and behaviour
specifications written as examples are common practices. Role-based access
control systems with policies in data, and feature flags for business
rules, keep policy out of code.

## Topic 46. Solving Impossible Puzzles

### In one sentence

When a problem seems impossible, the way through is usually to list its
constraints and find that some of them are not real.

### The idea

#### Degrees of freedom

The book opens with puzzles such as the Gordian knot of legend, which
nobody could untie until Alexander cut it. The point is that the
constraints people believed in, untie it by hand, were not actually
stated. Many hard problems in software are like that. They feel impossible
because I am working inside assumptions I have not noticed.

The book's tip: don't think outside the box, find the box. The box is the
set of real constraints.

Before trying to escape it, I should know exactly
where its walls are. Some walls are real, set by physics, law, money or a
firm business need. Others are imagined, carried over from habit or
someone's casual remark.

#### Enumerate and challenge the constraints

The method is to list every constraint I believe applies, then test each
one. Who said so?

Is it written down? What happens if it is broken? Often
a constraint turns out to be a preference, or a solution someone once
proposed that hardened into a rule.

The book offers questions to ask when stuck, which I paraphrase:

- Is there an easier way?
- Am I solving the right problem, or have I been distracted by a
  technicality?
- Why is this a problem at all?
- What makes it so hard?
- Does it have to be done this way?
- Does it have to be done at all?

#### Get out of your own way

Sometimes the answer comes only when I stop pushing. The book suggests
stepping away: do something else, sleep, go for a walk. The brain keeps
working on problems in the background, and solutions often arrive when
attention is elsewhere. Explaining the problem to someone else also helps,
the same rubber-duck effect as in debugging.

#### Fortune favours the prepared mind

The book's last point is that insight is not luck. It comes more often to
people who have practised, collected experience and noted what worked. It
suggests recording, in the engineering daybook from chapter 3, the
puzzles I solve and how I solved them. Over time that becomes a personal
library of approaches.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A team must import a nightly file of ten million rows into the
database, and the import takes nine hours, but the window is four hours.
Listed constraints: the file arrives at midnight; the import must finish by
4 am; every row must be validated; the database is the same size; the job
runs on one machine. Challenging each: the file can be produced in chunks
through the day, so the work need not start at midnight; validation can
happen as chunks arrive; and the job can run on several machines. Only the
4 am deadline is real. The redesigned import runs in parts throughout the
evening and finishes at 1 am.

**Realistic.** A mobile app must work offline, but the team believes all
data must be validated by the server before it is accepted, which seems
impossible offline. Challenging the constraint: what the business needs is
that invalid data never reaches the official records, not that every tap
waits for the server. The app accepts entries locally, marks them pending,
and the server validates on sync, flagging the rare rejected entry for the
user to fix. The impossible requirement dissolves once the real one is
found.

A second worked puzzle is about performance. A search page must return
results in under 200 milliseconds, but the query over the full product
catalogue takes two seconds.

The team assumes the constraint is "search the
whole catalogue on every keystroke". Challenging it: users only see the
first ten results, most searches are for a few hundred popular terms, and
results do not need to reflect stock changes within the last minute. Once
those facts are on the table, a precomputed index refreshed every minute,
plus a cache for popular terms, meets the 200 millisecond limit easily.
The real constraint was response time; the imagined one was freshness to
the second.

#### Keeping a puzzle log

The book's advice to record solved puzzles is easy to skip and pays off
slowly. A puzzle log entry needs only a few lines: the problem, the
constraints I first believed, the one that turned out to be false, and the
solution. After a year, patterns appear. Many of my own entries share the
same false constraint, "it has to happen synchronously", which tells me
where to look first next time.

1. **Write the problem down** in one or two sentences.
2. **List every constraint** I believe applies.
3. **For each, find its source**, and ask whether it is real.
4. **Ask the book's questions**: easier way, right problem, need it at all.
5. **Step away if stuck**, then come back to the list.
6. **Record the solution and the approach** in the daybook.

#### Kinds of constraint

It helps to sort constraints into kinds, because each kind is challenged
differently. **Absolute** constraints come from physics, mathematics or
law: data cannot travel faster than light, a contract specifies a
deadline, a regulation requires an audit trail.

These are walls of the
box. **Resource** constraints, such as budget, people and time, are real
but negotiable, since they can be traded against each other. **Habitual**
constraints come from how things have always been done, and are the most
common source of false walls. **Preference** constraints are someone's
taste stated as a rule, and are worth a polite question.

Most "impossible" problems I have seen became possible once one habitual
or preference constraint was named and questioned. The skill is not
cleverness but the patience to list everything and ask where each item
came from.

#### When the puzzle is people

Sometimes the box is organisational: two teams each believe the other
must change, or a decision is stuck because nobody is sure who owns it.
The same method applies. List the constraints each side believes in,
trace their sources, and look for the ones that are assumptions. Often the
deadlock dissolves when someone asks whether a requirement really comes
from the customer or from an old internal agreement.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is jumping to "think outside the box" brainstorming
without first knowing the box. Ideas then break real constraints and get
rejected. The fix is to list and test constraints first, so creativity is
spent where it can succeed.

The other mistake is accepting a constraint because someone senior said
it. The fix is to ask, politely, where it comes from; often they will be
glad it was questioned.

A last mistake is solving the puzzle and not telling anyone how. The
insight that a constraint was false is valuable to the whole team, since
others are probably working inside the same imagined box. The fix is a
short note in the team channel or the decision record: which constraint
turned out not to be real, and how we know.

### In real systems

Architecture decision records capture constraints and why they hold, so
later teams can see whether they still do. Design reviews often turn on a
single challenged assumption, such as "must this be real time?" or "does
this data have to live in one region?".

## Topic 47. Working Together

### In one sentence

Software is built best by people working closely together, with users
and teammates in the conversation and other eyes on the code as it is
written.

### The idea

#### Conway's law

The book recalls Conway's law: the structure of a system tends to mirror
the communication structure of the organisation that builds it. Three
teams that rarely talk will build three loosely joined components with
awkward interfaces. That cuts both ways. If I want a system with certain
boundaries, the team should be organised to match, and if the teams do not
talk, their systems will not fit.

#### Don't go into the code alone

The tip is: don't go into the code alone. The book describes two
practices that put more than one mind on the code at once.

In **pair programming**, two developers work at one machine. One types,
the driver; the other thinks about the bigger picture, the navigator; they
swap often. The navigator catches mistakes, questions choices and keeps
the direction, while the driver handles detail. It sounds like half the
output, but in practice fewer bugs, better design and shared knowledge
often make up for it.

In **mob programming**, the whole team, sometimes including a user or
tester, works on one task at one screen, with one person typing and
rotating. The book presents it as an extension of pairing: everyone shares
understanding, and decisions are made with all the relevant people
present.

#### Users in the team

The book extends "together" to users. Having a user available to answer
questions while the code is being written, even sitting with the team for
part of the day, shortens the requirements feedback loop from topic 45
from weeks to minutes.

#### How to work together well

The book's advice for pairing and mobbing, paraphrased:

- build the code, not my ego; nobody is the smartest person in the room
  all the time;
- start small, with a pair or a short mob session, and see how it goes;
- criticise the code, not the person;
- listen, and try to understand other viewpoints;
- hold frequent retrospectives to improve how the team works.

#### Why it is faster than it looks

The usual objection to pairing is cost: two people on one task. The book's
answer, which matches my experience, is that typing speed is rarely what
limits progress.

Thinking, misunderstanding, rework and waiting for review
are. A pair spends less time stuck, makes fewer wrong turns, and needs no
separate review afterwards. Knowledge spreads as a side effect, so the
team is less dependent on any one person. Measured over a week rather than
an hour, the difference in output is usually much smaller than it looks,
and sometimes reversed.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I am stuck on a tricky date-handling bug. Instead of another
hour alone, I ask a teammate to pair for thirty minutes. As I explain the
code, she asks why one function receives a date string and another a
`Date` object. That question is the bug: one path parses in local time, the
other in UTC.

**Realistic.** A team building a claims system for an insurer invites a
claims handler to join their mob sessions two afternoons a week. During one
session she sees the developers modelling a claim as having one assessor,
and explains that complex claims are passed between three. The model is
changed before any code depends on it, a fix that would have taken weeks
if found after release.

#### Pairing styles

There are a few common ways to pair, and each suits different work. In
**ping-pong pairing**, one person writes a failing test, the other makes
it pass and writes the next failing test, and so on.

It fits
test-driven work and keeps both people engaged. In **strong-style
pairing**, the rule is that an idea must go through someone else's hands
to reach the computer: the navigator decides, the driver types. It is
especially good for teaching, because the less experienced person drives
and learns by doing.

For exploring an unfamiliar codebase, a looser style works: one person
reads and narrates while the other takes notes and asks questions, then
they swap. Whatever the style, the book's advice holds: talk constantly,
swap often, and stop when either person is tired, since a tired pair is
worse than two rested individuals.

#### Onboarding through pairing

Pairing is one of the fastest ways to bring a new person into a team. In a
week of pairing on real tasks, a newcomer learns the codebase, the
tools, the conventions and the unwritten rules that documentation misses.
The experienced partner also benefits, because explaining the code shows
which parts are hard to explain, which is often a sign they need
refactoring.

#### Working together across distance

Many teams no longer share a room. The book's advice still applies, with
some adjustments.

Pairing works over a screen-sharing tool with both
people able to type; short, scheduled sessions keep it from becoming
tiring. Mob sessions need a clear rotation timer and someone keeping notes.
Users can join a video call for part of a session rather than sitting with
the team.

The bigger risk with distance is that people slip back into working alone
for long stretches and meeting only to report status. The fix is to keep
some time each week for working on code together, not just talking about
it, so the shared understanding the book values is built while the code is
written.

#### Code review as collaboration

Code review is the most common form of working together on code, and it
can be done in the book's spirit or against it. In its spirit, review is a
conversation about the code: questions, suggestions and explanations, with
the shared aim of better code and shared knowledge. Against it, review
becomes a gate where one person judges another. The book's advice to
criticise the code, not the person, and to build the code rather than an
ego, applies directly.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is pairing where one person types and the other
watches silently or checks their phone. The fix is real roles, frequent
swaps, and the navigator speaking up.

Another mistake is forcing pairing on everything. The fix is to use it
where it helps, such as hard problems, onboarding and risky changes, and to
let people work alone on routine tasks when that suits them.

Working together also changes how problems are owned. When code is
written by pairs and mobs and reviewed by others, it belongs to the team
rather than to one author. Nobody is the only person who can fix a module,
holidays do not stop work, and design choices are discussed rather than
discovered later. The book sees this shared ownership as one of the main
benefits, alongside fewer bugs.

### In real systems

Many teams pair on complex or risky work and for onboarding, and use code
review for the rest. Remote pairing tools make it work across locations.
Some organisations deliberately shape team boundaries to match the
architecture they want, an approach sometimes called the inverse Conway
manoeuvre.

### Check yourself

**What does Conway's law predict?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That a system's structure will mirror the communication structure of the
organisation that builds it.

</details>

**What are the driver and navigator roles in pair programming?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The driver types and handles details; the navigator watches the bigger
picture, questions choices and catches mistakes. They swap often.

</details>

## Topic 48. The Essence of Agility

### In one sentence

Agility is not a process or a product but a way of working: find out
where I am, take the smallest meaningful step toward where I want to be,
check the result, and repeat.

### The idea

#### Agile is an adjective

The book reminds readers that the Agile Manifesto, written in 2001, is a
short statement of values: individuals and interactions over processes and
tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer
collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over
following a plan. The items on the right still matter; the ones on the
left matter more.

Since then, "agile" has often become a noun: a branded process with
certifications, roles and ceremonies. The book's tip: agile is not a noun;
agile is how you do things. A team following an agile process to the
letter, without responding to feedback, is not agile.

#### There can never be an agile process

The book makes a sharper claim: there can never be a single agile
process, because agility means responding to the unknowns of a particular
situation. A process fixed in advance cannot anticipate them. What can be
written down is a way to evolve a process.

#### The recipe

The book gives a three-step loop:

1. Work out where you are.
2. Make the smallest meaningful step towards where you want to be.
3. Evaluate where you end up, and fix anything you broke.

Repeat until done. This works at every level: a single function, a
feature, a team's way of working, a company's strategy. It is the same as
the headlights idea from chapter 4 and the feedback loop for requirements
in topic 45.

#### And this drives design

The loop only works if each step can be changed cheaply. If the code
resists change, small steps become expensive and teams fall back on big
plans. That is why the book ties agility back to its central design tip
from chapter 2: good design is easier to change than bad design. Agile
process depends on changeable code.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A team's sprint reviews have become a ritual: slides, no
users. Applying the loop: where are we? Reviews produce no feedback. The
smallest meaningful step: invite two real users to the next one and show
working software only. Evaluate: the users give more useful feedback in
twenty minutes than the last three reviews combined. Keep the change and
look for the next one.

**Realistic.** A company adopts a well-known agile framework, with all its
roles and meetings. A year later, releases still take three months,
because deployment is manual and fragile. The ceremonies are agile in
name; the delivery is not. Applying the loop to the real bottleneck: the
smallest step is automating one deployment step. Over a quarter of such
steps, releases become weekly. The meetings did not change; the ability to
respond did.

#### Reading the values carefully

Each value in the manifesto is a preference, not a rejection. Individuals
and interactions over processes and tools does not mean no process; it
means that when the process gets in the way of people working together,
people win.

Working software over comprehensive documentation does not
mean no documentation; it means a running demo is better evidence of
progress than a status report. Customer collaboration over contract
negotiation means treating the client as a partner in discovery, which is
topic 45 again. Responding to change over following a plan means a plan is
a tool for the next step, not a promise about the last one.

Read that way, the manifesto and this book agree closely. Both care about
feedback, small steps, close work with users and code that is easy to
change. The book's contribution is to strip the idea back to the loop, so
it cannot be mistaken for a set of meetings.

A second realistic example shows the loop on a single feature. A team
wants to improve onboarding for new users. Where are we: analytics show
that 40 percent drop out at the "connect your bank" step.

Smallest step:
add a "skip for now" option. Evaluate: drop-off at that step falls by half,
but fewer users connect a bank later. Fix: add a reminder after the first
week. Each step is small, measured and reversible, and the team learns
more in a month than a redesign would have taught them in a quarter.

### Step by step

  "Work out where I am",
  "Take the smallest meaningful step",

  "Fix anything I broke",
  "Repeat",

<Timeline caption="The loop applied to a team's delivery over a quarter. Look at each entry: one small change, then a check before the next." items={[
  { label: "Week 1", text: "Where are we: releases take three months; deploys are manual." },
  { label: "Week 2", text: "Step: script the database migration; evaluate: one deploy step automated." },
  { label: "Week 4", text: "Step: automated tests gate the build; evaluate: fewer broken releases." },

  { label: "Week 12", text: "Where are we now: weekly releases; next bottleneck is review time." },

1. **Describe where I am honestly**, with evidence, not the plan.
2. **Choose the smallest step** that would produce a meaningful change.
3. **Take it.**
4. **Measure or observe the result**, and repair any damage.
5. **Decide the next step** from what I learned.

#### The loop at different scales

The same three steps look different at each scale. For a single function,
"where am I" is the failing test, the step is a few lines of code, and
evaluation is running the tests. For a feature, "where am I" is user
feedback, the step is a small release behind a flag, and evaluation is
usage data. For a team, "where am I" is how long work takes and where it
waits, the step is one change to how the team works, and evaluation is
whether that time went down.

Seeing it this way removes much of the mystery around agile methods. The
ceremonies of a framework are one team's choices about how to run the
loop. They are worth trying, and worth changing when the loop shows they
are not helping.

#### Measuring where I am

The first step, working out where I am, is the one most often skipped,
because it requires honesty. A team may believe it releases often when the
data show releases take six weeks from first commit. A useful practice is
to pick two or three simple measures, such as time from commit to
production, number of changes that caused incidents, and time to recover,
and look at them regularly. They turn "where am I" from an opinion into a
fact, and make each small step's effect visible.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is adopting the ceremonies of an agile framework and
believing that makes the team agile. The fix is to judge agility by how
quickly the team responds to feedback, not by which meetings it holds.

The other mistake is using "agile" as a reason not to plan. The loop needs
a direction, "where I want to be"; it just holds the plan loosely.

The loop also gives a simple test for any proposed process change: what
step does it let us take, and how will we know whether it helped? A change
that cannot answer both is ceremony. A change that can is worth trying for
a few weeks, measuring, and then keeping or dropping. Teams that run their
own process this way stay agile even as they grow, because the process
itself keeps being learned.

### In real systems

Continuous delivery, short iterations, retrospectives that actually change
something, and metrics such as lead time from commit to production are
the practical forms of agility. Teams that measure and improve these
usually outperform teams that only follow a framework's rules.

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Before the Project">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the habit is asking questions before coding. When
given a task, I restate it in my own words, ask what problem it solves,
and list two or three edge cases to check with whoever asked.

Example: asked to "add an export button", I ask what format, which
columns, for how many rows, and who will open the file. The answer, a
spreadsheet for the finance team, changes the format from CSV to Excel.

Mistake: starting to code the moment the task is assigned, then rebuilding
when the first demo misses the point. Fix: ten minutes of questions first.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I run the requirements feedback loop
deliberately, with examples and quick mock-ups, and keep a project
glossary. I separate policy from requirement, and I pair with teammates on
hard problems.

Example: before building a discount feature, I write a table of ten
example orders and what each should cost, and review it with the product
owner. Three rows change.

Mistake: keeping the glossary in my head. Fix: a shared document in the
repository, linked from the README, updated whenever a new term appears.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I find the box on hard problems: list constraints,
trace each to its source, and challenge the imagined ones. I bring users
into the team's working sessions and design policies as data from the
start.

Example: a "must be real time" requirement turns out to mean "within five
minutes", which allows a simple batch design instead of a streaming
platform.

Mistake: accepting a constraint because it appeared in a slide. Fix: ask
for the source and the consequence of breaking it.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases in requirements are the boundaries nobody mentions: exactly at
a threshold, zero items, the first and last day of a period, users in
other countries, accounts with no history.

Example: "free shipping at 500 rupees" leaves open whether 500 itself
qualifies, and whether discounts count toward it.

Mistake: guessing the answer at a boundary and coding it silently. Fix:
write the guess as a test case and ask about it; the test then documents
the answer.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases combine rules: a discount and a free-shipping threshold
together, a role that is both manager and HR, an offline entry that
becomes invalid because the rules changed before it synced.

Example: a manager who is also in HR sees salaries through HR access, even
though the manager policy excludes them; is that intended?

Mistake: rules that each make sense alone but conflict together, with no
defined priority. Fix: an explicit order of precedence for rules, agreed
with the business and written in the glossary.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, requirements keep being learned. Usage analytics, support
tickets and user interviews feed the loop. Policies in data are changed
through an audited admin interface, not by editing configuration on a
server.

Example: analytics show nobody uses an elaborate report filter; the team
removes it, simplifying code and the interface.

Mistake: changing a policy directly in the production database with no
record. Fix: policy changes go through a reviewed change, with who, when
and why.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is curiosity about the need behind every request, humility
about my own assumptions, and comfort with not knowing everything at the
start. The book's view is that discovering requirements is part of the
developer's job, not something done before the job starts.

Example: I treat "the client changed their mind" as new information to
welcome, not a failure to resent.

Mistake: defensiveness when a demo reveals I misunderstood. Fix: thank
them, note what was learned, and adjust; the demo did its job.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

At system design level, Conway's law matters. Team boundaries shape
system boundaries, so architecture and team structure are designed
together. Policies are a first-class part of the design, with a rules or
permissions service that the business can configure.

Example: a company that wants an independent payments platform first
creates a payments team with its own on-call and roadmap.

Mistake: designing services that cut across team boundaries, so every
change needs three teams. Fix: align services with teams, or change the
teams.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

Strategically, agility is the ability to learn and respond faster than the
environment changes. The book's loop applies to the organisation's own
way of working: measure where it is, change one thing, check, repeat.

Example: a leadership team reviews lead time and customer feedback
monthly and picks one improvement to try, instead of a yearly process
overhaul.

Mistake: buying an agile transformation programme as a product. Fix:
start from the current bottleneck and take the smallest step that would
improve it.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>Isn't gathering requirements the product manager's job?</summary>

Product managers lead it, but the book says developers must take part.
Developers see edge cases and technical trade-offs that others miss, and
questions asked while coding are part of discovering requirements.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How detailed should user stories be?</summary>

Detailed enough to start a conversation and to know when it is done,
usually a sentence and a few acceptance criteria. Detail is added as it is
learned, close to when the work happens.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I push back on scope creep without being difficult?</summary>

Make the trade-off visible. "We can add this; it will take about three
days, so the release moves, or we drop something else. Which do you
prefer?" The client decides with full information.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is pair programming worth it for experienced developers?</summary>

Often, for hard or risky work, because two experienced people catch more
and share context. For routine work, solo coding plus review is usually
enough. The book recommends starting small and judging by results.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if my company's agile process is just rituals?</summary>

Apply the loop to my own work and team first. Find one place where
feedback is slow, make one small change, show the result. Changes that
work tend to spread.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I know whether a constraint is real?</summary>

Find where it came from and what breaks if it is violated. Legal,
contractual, physical and budget limits are usually real. "We have always
done it this way" usually is not.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Nobody knows exactly what they want at the start; developers help them find out.
- Requirements are learned in a feedback loop of questions, examples and working software.
- Work alongside users to see needs they would never think to mention.
- Policy is metadata: implement the general requirement and keep the specific rules in data.
- Keep a project glossary and make every scope change visible.
- Find the box: list constraints, trace their sources, drop the imagined ones.
- Don't go into the code alone; pair, mob and involve users.
- Agile is how you work, not a product: where am I, smallest step, evaluate, repeat.

## Glossary

- **Requirement**: a statement of a need the system must meet.
- **Policy**: a specific, changeable rule that applies a requirement, such as which roles may act.
- **User story**: a short description of a need from a user's view, a promise of a conversation.
- **Overspecification**: describing the solution in such detail that design decisions are made too early.
- **Scope creep**: requirements growing a little at a time without the trade-offs being visible.
- **Project glossary**: one shared list of project terms and their exact meanings.
- **Constraint**: a limit a solution must respect; real or merely assumed.
- **Conway's law**: systems mirror the communication structures of the organisations that build them.
- **Pair programming**: two developers working together on one task at one machine.
- **Mob programming**: a whole team working on one task at one screen, rotating the typist.
- **Agile Manifesto**: a 2001 statement of values for software development.
- **Retrospective**: a regular team discussion of what to keep and what to change.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up.** Take a recent task request. Write down its literal words,
then the need I think is behind it, then five questions I should have
asked. Ask at least two of them, even if late.

**Build.** For a feature in a project I know, write an example table:
inputs, expected result, and which cells are guesses. Review it with
someone who knows the business, and turn every confirmed row into a test.

**Stretch.** Pick a problem my team has called impossible or too
expensive. List every constraint and its source in a table like the import
example. Find at least one that is assumed, and sketch the solution that
becomes possible without it.
