The last chapter scales the book's ideas from one developer to a whole
team and project. It covers how pragmatic teams work, why copying another
company's process rarely works, the three tools every project needs,
what it means to delight users, and the pride of signing my own work.
Almost every earlier chapter reappears here, applied at a larger scale.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- apply the book's individual habits, such as no broken windows and DRY, to a whole team;
- tell a practice that works for us from one copied because it is fashionable;
- set up the starter kit: version control driving everything, thorough automated testing, and full automation;
- test the tests themselves with saboteurs, and think in state coverage rather than line coverage;
- define project success by what users need, not by features shipped;
- take ownership of my work and sign it.

What I should already know: the habits from earlier chapters, especially
broken windows and the knowledge portfolio from

## Topic 49. Pragmatic Teams

### In one sentence

A pragmatic team applies the same habits as a pragmatic individual, kept
small and stable, with time scheduled for the work that keeps it healthy.

### The idea

#### Small, stable teams

The book's starting point is that teams work best when they are small,
around ten to twelve people at most, and stable, with members who stay
long enough to know and trust each other. Members come and go
occasionally, but a team that is constantly reshuffled never builds the
shared understanding that makes it effective. The tip: maintain small,
stable teams.

#### Individual habits, at team scale

The book then revisits earlier ideas with a team lens.

**No broken windows.** Quality is everyone's responsibility, not a separate
quality team's. A team that tolerates small problems, such as a flaky test
or a hack nobody fixes, sends the message that nobody cares, and the
entropy from chapter 1 sets in.

**Boiled frogs.** A team is even more likely than an individual to miss
slow changes, because each member assumes someone else is watching. Scope
creep, rising build times and growing on-call load creep up unnoticed. The
book suggests someone actively watches for these, and that the team checks
its environment regularly.

**Schedule your knowledge portfolio.** Improvement work does not happen in
spare time, because there is none. Maintenance, refactoring, process
reflection, experiments with new technology and learning must be on the
schedule. The tip: schedule it to make it happen.

**Communicate team presence.** The team itself should be seen to
communicate well, with clear meetings, good documentation and a
consistent voice, so that the rest of the organisation knows what it
does. The book even suggests a team name or identity to help.

**Don't repeat yourselves.** Duplication between team members, such as two
people writing the same utility, is DRY at team scale. Good communication
prevents it.

**Team tracer bullets.** Teams should be organised so that they can
deliver end-to-end features, not split by layer into a front-end team, a
database team and a testing team. The tip: organise fully functional
teams. Each team builds tracer bullets through the whole system, as in
chapter 2.

**Automation.** A team should automate everything it can, from builds to
deployments, which topic 51 covers.

**Know when to stop adding paint.** Teams are made of individuals, and the
book says to give them room to shine, but also to know when a piece is
good enough, as chapter 1 argued for good-enough software.

#### Why size and stability matter

The number of possible conversations in a team grows much faster than the
team. Five people have ten pairs who might need to talk; ten people have
forty-five; twenty people have one hundred and ninety. Past a certain
size, people stop knowing what others are doing, and coordination moves
into meetings and documents. Keeping teams small keeps most coordination
in direct conversation, which is faster and loses less.

Stability matters for a different reason. Trust and shared understanding
take months to build: knowing who is good at what, how each person
communicates, which corners of the code each knows. Reshuffling resets all
of it. The book does not say teams should never change, only that change
should be occasional and deliberate.

#### Watching for boiled frogs, concretely

It is easy to agree that a team should watch for slow changes and hard to
do it without something to watch. A few numbers, looked at weekly, make it
concrete: build time, test suite duration, number of open bugs, on-call
pages per week, and time from merge to production.

None needs to be
perfect. What matters is the trend. A build that grew from four minutes to
twelve over a year is a frog that has been boiling, and the team only sees
it when someone puts the numbers side by side.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A team notices that their test suite has three flaky tests
that everyone re-runs until they pass. Applying no broken windows: they
agree that any flaky test is fixed or quarantined within a day, with an
owner. Within two weeks, the suite is trusted again, and real failures stop
being dismissed.

**Realistic.** A company has a front-end team, a back-end team and a QA
team. Every feature needs all three, so each waits on the others, and
interfaces between them are a constant source of bugs. They reorganise into
three smaller cross-functional teams, each with front-end, back-end and
testing skills, each owning a product area. Features now flow end to end
within one team, and the coordination meetings shrink from daily to weekly.

A third example is about team presence. A platform team builds shared
tools, but other teams keep building their own versions because they do
not know what exists.

The platform team starts a short monthly update,
keeps a single page listing what they offer and how to ask for help, and
names a rotating contact for questions each week. Within a quarter,
duplicate tools stop appearing. Nothing about their code changed; what
changed was that the team became visible, which is the book's point about
communicating team presence and DRY at team scale.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is leaving improvement for "when things calm down",
which never happens. The fix is to schedule it, as the book says, and
protect the time like any other commitment.

Another mistake is growing one team past fifteen or twenty people, where
communication paths multiply and meetings swell. The fix is to split into
smaller teams along product boundaries.

### In real systems

Many organisations use small cross-functional teams that own a product
area end to end, with a platform team providing shared tools. The "two
pizza team" rule of thumb, a team small enough to feed with two pizzas,
expresses the same idea as the book's size limit.

## Topic 50. Coconuts Don't Cut It

### In one sentence

Copying the visible rituals of successful teams does not bring their
results, so I use what actually works in my context, and keep checking.

### The idea

#### Cargo cults

The book tells the story behind the phrase **cargo cult**. During the
Second World War, islanders in the Pacific saw aircraft land with goods.
After the war, some groups built imitation runways and control towers,
with coconut-shell headphones, hoping the planes would return. They had
copied the visible form, not the cause.

Software teams do the same. They adopt a famous company's process, stand-up
format, tool or architecture, because it is associated with success,
without the conditions that made it work there. Microservices for a
three-person team, a heavy process framework for a small product, or a tool
chosen because a large company uses it, are common examples.

#### Context matters

The book's tip: do what works, not what's fashionable. The way to know
what works is to try something, measure the effect in my own context,
and keep or drop it. That is the agility loop from chapter 8 applied to
practices themselves.

The book adds that one size fits no one. Methods are a set of ideas, and
each team should take the ones that help it, adapt them, and leave the
rest. Every published method grew from one team's experience, and is a
starting point, not a prescription.

#### The real goal

The book reminds me what all methods are for: delivering software users
can use, when they need it. Its tip: deliver when users need it. Not on a
fixed release train if users need it sooner, and not as fast as possible if
they cannot absorb change that quickly. The ideal is a team able to
deliver continuously, so that delivery is a business decision rather than
a technical one.

#### How cargo cults start

Cargo cults usually start with good intentions. Someone reads about a
practice that helped a respected team, and it sounds like a solution to a
vague frustration. The practice is adopted as a whole, including the parts
that only made sense in the original setting. Because nobody stated the
problem it was meant to solve, nobody can tell whether it is working, and
it continues on habit.

The defence is simple to state. Before adopting a practice, write one
sentence about the problem it should solve and one about how we will know.
After a few weeks, look at the evidence. This is the same loop the book
uses everywhere: small step, feedback, adjust.

#### Continuous delivery as the goal behind the goals

The book's emphasis on delivering when users need it leads to a practical
target. If a team can deploy any change, safely, at any time, then the
question of how often to release becomes a business choice. Methods,
meetings and tools are all means toward that capability. When a practice
does not move the team closer to it, or to better outcomes for users, it
is a candidate for dropping, however fashionable it is.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A four-person team reads that a large company runs hundreds
of microservices and decides to split their app into twelve services.
Six months later they spend most of their time on deployment, networking
and debugging across services. Asking "what works for us" leads them back
to a well-structured single application with clear modules, which they can
split later if a real need appears.

**Realistic.** A team adopts daily stand-ups because the framework says
so. They become thirty-minute status reports to the manager. Applying the
book's idea, they ask what the stand-up is for, coordinating today's work,
and change it: ten minutes, focused on blockers, held at the task board.
When that still feels heavy, they try asynchronous updates three days a
week, and measure whether blockers get resolved as fast. They do, so they
keep it.

A third example comes from architecture. A team adopts an event-driven
design because conference talks praise it. Their product is a simple
internal form tool with one database and a few hundred users.

Events make
every bug harder to trace, as chapter 6 warned for blackboards. Asking the
book's question, what problem did events solve for the teams in those
talks, gives the answer: many independent consumers at large scale. Their
product has neither. They move back to direct calls inside one service and
keep events only for the one integration with another system that needs
them.

### Step by step

<Flow caption="Adopting a practice without a cargo cult. Look at the third step: the purpose comes before the copying." steps={[
  "Notice a practice that works elsewhere",
  "Ask what problem it solved there",
  "Check whether we have that problem",
  "Try a small version, measure",
  "Keep, adapt or drop",
]} />

1. **Name the problem** I want to solve, before choosing a practice.
2. **Find out why the practice worked** where it came from.
3. **Check whether my context is similar**: size, product, risk.
4. **Try a small, reversible version**, with a measure.
5. **Keep what helps, adapt or drop the rest.**

### Common mistake

The common mistake is "best practice" used as an argument by itself.
Every practice is best for some context. The fix is to ask "best for whom,
and for what problem?".

### In real systems

Successful teams often describe their process as their own blend: a few
ideas from one framework, a few from another, refined over years by
retrospectives. Delivery capability, such as deploying on demand, tends to
matter more than which named method a team follows.

### Check yourself

**What is a cargo cult in software?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Copying the visible rituals, tools or structures of successful teams
without the conditions or understanding that made them work.

</details>

**What does "deliver when users need it" mean in practice?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Being able to release continuously, so the timing of a release is decided
by users' needs and the business, not by technical limits.

</details>

## Topic 51. Pragmatic Starter Kit

### In one sentence

Every project needs three things from the start: version control driving
everything, thorough automated testing, and full automation of builds and
deployments.

### The idea

#### Drive with version control

The book's first leg is version control, from chapter 3, but used more
broadly: as the driver of the whole project. Every change, to code,
configuration, infrastructure or documentation, goes through the
repository, and the repository triggers everything else. A commit starts a
build; a merge to the main branch deploys to a test environment; a tag
releases to production. The tip: use version control to drive builds,
tests and releases.

The benefit is that the state of the system is always described by the
repository. Any release can be rebuilt, any change traced, and any machine
replaced by running the pipeline again.

#### Ruthless and continuous testing

The second leg is testing. The book's tips: test early, test often, test
automatically. And: coding ain't done till all the tests run. A feature is
not finished when it works on my machine; it is finished when the full
automated suite passes in the pipeline.

The book lists kinds of tests a project needs:

- **unit tests** for individual modules, as in chapter 7;
- **integration tests** that check modules work together, including with
  real databases and services where possible;
- **validation and verification**, checking that the system does what
  users need, not just what the specification says;
- **performance tests** under realistic load, to find limits before users
  do.

#### Test the tests

How do I know the tests are good? The book suggests a **saboteur**:
deliberately introduce bugs into a copy of the code and check that the
tests catch them.

If a bug survives, the tests are missing something. The
tip: use saboteurs to test your testing. Automated **mutation testing**
tools do this systematically, changing operators and constants and
reporting which changes no test notices.

#### State coverage, not code coverage

The book warns that code coverage, the percentage of lines run by the
tests, can mislead. Every line can run while important states are never
tested.

The tip: test state coverage, not code coverage. What matters is
whether the tests explore the meaningful states and inputs, such as empty,
one, many, boundary values and errors. Property-based testing from chapter
7 helps here.

#### Find bugs once

When a bug is found, by a user or a tester, the book says to write a test
that catches it before fixing it. The tip: find bugs once. The same bug
should never need to be found by a human twice.

#### Full automation

The third leg is automation. Builds, tests, deployments, database
migrations and environment setup should all be scripted and run by
machines. Manual steps are slow, error-prone and depend on the one person
who remembers them. The tip: don't use manual procedures.

#### Testing at every layer

The book's list of tests maps onto a familiar shape. At the bottom are many
fast unit tests, run on every save or commit.

Above them are fewer
integration tests, which use real databases and services and take longer.
Above those are a small number of end-to-end tests through the real user
interface, which are slow and more fragile but check the whole system.
Performance and validation tests sit alongside, run on a schedule or
before releases.

Each layer catches different bugs. Unit tests catch logic errors.

Integration tests catch mismatches between modules, such as a query that
works in a mock and fails in a real database. End-to-end tests catch
wiring mistakes. A project with only one layer has blind spots in the
others.

#### Testing the environment too

The book also mentions testing things around the code: that the
deployment works, that the configuration is valid, that the system
recovers from failures. Some teams go further and deliberately inject
failures in production-like environments, killing servers or slowing the
network, to check that the system behaves as designed. That is the
saboteur idea applied to infrastructure instead of code.

#### Automation as documentation

A script that builds and deploys the project is also the most accurate
documentation of how to do it, because it is the procedure actually run.
A wiki page drifts; a script that runs on every commit cannot. When a new
team member asks how releases work, the answer is the pipeline file, which
is always current.

### Worked example

**Simple.** Line coverage versus state coverage. This test runs every line
of `average`, yet misses the empty-list state:

```ts
// Test state coverage, not code coverage: every line runs, yet a state is untested.
function average(xs: number[]): number {
  let sum = 0;
  for (const x of xs) sum += x;
  return sum / xs.length;
}
console.log('test [2, 4] ->', average([2, 4]), '(every line executed: 100% line coverage)');
console.log('state [] ->', average([]), '(never tested)');
```

The output:

```text
test [2, 4] -> 3 (every line executed: 100% line coverage)
state [] -> NaN (never tested)
```

A coverage tool would report 100 percent, and the function returns NaN for
an empty list, which then spreads through any report that uses it. The
missing test is a state, not a line.

**Second example.** A tiny saboteur. Two mutants of an age check, each a
plausible typo, are run against a weak and a strong test suite:

```ts
// A tiny saboteur: mutate the code on purpose and see whether the tests notice.
const impls: Record<string, (age: number) => boolean> = {
  original: a => a >= 18,
  'mutant >= to >': a => a > 18,
  'mutant 18 to 17': a => a >= 17,
};
const weakTests: [number, boolean][] = [[30, true], [5, false]];
const strongTests: [number, boolean][] = [...weakTests, [18, true], [17, false]];
for (const [label, tests] of [['weak', weakTests], ['strong', strongTests]] as const) {
  const survivors = Object.entries(impls).slice(1)
    .filter(([, f]) => tests.every(([a, want]) => f(a) === want)).map(([n]) => n);
  console.log(`${label} suite: ${survivors.length} of 2 mutants survive ${JSON.stringify(survivors)}`);
}
```

The output:

```text
weak suite: 2 of 2 mutants survive ["mutant >= to >","mutant 18 to 17"]
strong suite: 0 of 2 mutants survive []
```

The weak suite tests ages far from the boundary, so both bugs pass. Adding
the boundary cases, 18 and 17, kills both mutants. The saboteur showed
exactly which tests were missing.

**Realistic.** A team's release process is a wiki page of twenty manual
steps that only two people run. One is on holiday when a security patch
must go out, and the other skips step fourteen, which clears a cache. The
site serves stale pages for an hour. The fix is a pipeline: merge to main
builds, tests and deploys to staging; a tag deploys to production, cache
clear included. Anyone on the team can now release, and step fourteen can
never be skipped.

6. **Replace every manual step with a script.**

#### Find bugs once, in practice

The find-bugs-once rule changes how a bug fix is done. First, reproduce
the bug with an automated test, at the lowest level that shows it, often a
unit test.

Watch it fail. Then fix the code and watch the test pass. The
test is committed with the fix, so the reviewer can see both the problem
and the proof that it is solved.

Over time, the regression tests become a record of everything that has
gone wrong in the project. They are often the most valuable tests in the
suite, because each one guards against a mistake that someone has already
made once and that the code's shape makes easy to make again.

#### Automation beyond deployment

Full automation also covers chores that are easy to forget: renewing
certificates, rotating secrets, cleaning up old data, updating
dependencies, and checking backups by actually restoring them. Each manual
chore is a future incident waiting for the week someone is on holiday.
Scheduled jobs, with alerts when they fail, turn them into things nobody
has to remember.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is chasing a coverage number. Teams write tests that
execute lines without checking meaningful results, and the number goes up
while bugs still slip through. The fix is to think about states and
boundaries, and use mutation testing to check whether the tests actually
detect changes.

Another mistake is a pipeline with one manual step "just for now". The fix
is to automate it; manual steps become the ones that fail at the worst
moment.

### In real systems

Continuous integration services, infrastructure as code, database
migration tools and deployment pipelines are the starter kit in practice.
Mutation testing tools exist for most languages, including Stryker for
JavaScript and TypeScript. Many teams require a regression test with every
bug-fix pull request.

### Check yourself

**Why can 100 percent line coverage still miss bugs?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Running a line is not the same as testing every meaningful state through
it; empty inputs, boundaries and errors can all go untested.

</details>

**What is a saboteur in the book's sense?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A deliberately introduced bug, used to check whether the test suite
detects it. A surviving bug shows a gap in the tests.

</details>

## Topic 52. Delight Your Users

### In one sentence

Users do not want code; they want their problem solved, so I find out how
they will judge success and aim to exceed that.

### The idea

#### Expectations, not features

The book points out that users rarely care about the code itself. They
have a business problem and expectations about how it will be solved. A
project that ships every requested feature can still disappoint if it does
not meet those expectations, and one that ships fewer features can delight
if it solves the real problem well.

The tip: delight users, don't just deliver code.

#### The success question

The book offers a simple question to ask at the start, which I paraphrase:
how will you know that we have all been successful a month or so after this
project is done? The answers are often surprising. They are rarely about
features. They are about outcomes: fewer support calls, faster month-end
close, staff who can work from the field, customers who stop complaining
about a process.

Those answers become the real goals of the project. They guide decisions
about what to build first, what to cut, and what "done" means. They also
give the team something to measure after release.

#### Problem solvers

The book's view of the developer's role widens here. A pragmatic
developer is a problem solver, not a feature factory. That means
understanding the business, suggesting simpler solutions when they exist,
and sometimes finding that the best answer is not software at all, such as
a process change.

It also means continuing to care after delivery: checking whether the
expected outcomes happened, and adjusting if they did not.

#### Expectations change during the project

Expectations are not fixed at the start. As users see working software,
their ideas of what is possible grow, sometimes beyond the budget. The book
suggests managing this through the same feedback loop as requirements:
show progress often, talk about outcomes rather than features, and make
trade-offs visible. A user who has seen the product evolve and been part of
the decisions is far more likely to be delighted than one who sees it only
at the end.

There is also a difference between meeting and exceeding expectations.
Meeting them means the problem is solved. Exceeding them often comes from
small things nobody asked for, such as a sensible default, a helpful error
message, or a report that arrives before anyone remembers to ask for it.
These come from developers who understand the user's work well enough to
anticipate what would help.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A sales team asks for a report of last month's orders. I ask
the success question. The answer: "we want to stop missing follow-ups with
customers who ordered once and never came back." The report becomes a
weekly list of one-time customers from sixty to ninety days ago, sent
automatically, which is what they actually needed.

**Realistic.** A warehouse asks for a new inventory system. The success
question reveals that the real pain is the hour each morning spent
reconciling counts between two systems. The team prioritises a nightly
automatic reconciliation in the first release, before most of the new
system's features. The morning hour disappears in week three, and the
warehouse staff become the project's strongest supporters.

#### When the best solution is not software

The book's view of developers as problem solvers includes accepting that
sometimes the right answer is not to build anything. A team asked for a
tool to track who has approved a document may find that the real problem is
that nobody knows who is supposed to approve it. A one-page agreement on
approvers solves it in a day. Suggesting that is not avoiding work; it is
the most direct way to delight the user, and it builds the trust that
makes the next project easier.

  ["Done at release", "Done when the outcome is seen"],

1. **Ask the success question** early, of the people who will judge it.
2. **Write the answers as measurable outcomes.**
3. **Order work by its effect on those outcomes.**
4. **Suggest simpler or different solutions** when they serve the outcome
   better.
5. **Measure after release**, and adjust.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is treating the feature list as the definition of
success. The team ships it all, on time, and the users are still unhappy.
The fix is outcomes agreed at the start and checked at the end.

### In real systems

Product teams use outcome goals, such as reducing a support metric or
increasing a conversion rate, rather than feature lists. Post-launch
reviews check whether the outcomes happened. Developers who sit in on
user research or support calls build better intuition for what delights.

### Check yourself

**What is the book's success question?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Roughly: how will you know that we have all been successful a month or so
after the project is done?

</details>

**Why might a project that delivers every feature still fail?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because users judge it by whether their problem and expectations were
met, not by the features shipped.

</details>

## Topic 53. Pride and Prejudice

### In one sentence

Pragmatic programmers take responsibility for their work and are proud to
put their name on it, while respecting the work of others.

### The idea

#### Sign your work

The book's final tip is: sign your work. Craftspeople have long signed
what they made, as a mark of responsibility and pride. Software is
usually collaborative, but each part still has people who made it, and
they should be willing to stand behind it.

Signing is not about ego or ownership in a territorial sense. It is a
promise: this is code I am willing to be judged by. It should be
well-written, tested and documented, and when there are problems I will
fix them.

#### Anonymity and sloppiness

The book warns that anonymity can breed sloppiness. When nobody's name is
attached, it is easier to leave the shortcut, skip the test or ignore the
broken window. A sense of personal ownership pushes the other way.

#### Prejudice: respect others' code

The title's other half is a warning about **prejudice**. Owning my code
must not turn into defending it against all change, or into dismissing
other people's code. Code belongs to the team; others should feel free to
improve it, and I should approach their code with respect, assuming good
reasons for choices I do not understand until I learn otherwise.

The book's hope is that a programmer's name on a piece of code becomes a
sign of quality: people see it and expect the code to be solid,
well-tested and well-documented.

#### The book's close

In the postface, the authors widen the view once more. Software now runs
much of the world, so developers have a moral responsibility for what they
build: to protect users from harm, and to consider whether they would want
to be a user of the system themselves. The final message is that each of
us is building the future, one piece at a time, and should build it well.

#### What makes work worth signing

The book gives a sense of what a signature should guarantee. The code is
readable by the next person.

It is tested, including the awkward cases.
Its behaviour and any surprising decisions are documented, in code
comments, commit messages or the README as appropriate. It handles errors
honestly, following chapter 4. It leaves the area around it at least as
clean as it was found, following chapter 1's broken windows.

That is a summary of the whole book. Signing my work is the point at which
all the earlier habits come together: caring about craft, thinking about
the user, communicating clearly, testing thoroughly and taking
responsibility.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I am about to merge a change with a known edge case
unhandled, planning to "fix it later". Asking myself whether I would sign
it, the answer is no. I spend twenty more minutes adding the test and the
fix, and the commit message explains the case.

**Realistic.** A team adopts a simple practice: every module's README
lists its current maintainers, and every pull request description states
what was tested and what was not. Nobody is blamed for problems, but
everyone knows who to ask. Over a few months, the "what was not tested"
section gets shorter, because people prefer not to write it.

### Step by step

<Figure caption="What signing a piece of work implies. Look at the bottom line: ownership includes fixing it and welcoming improvements from others." art={`
   +------------------------------------------------+
   |  signed: my name on this change                |
   |                                                |
   |  [x] I understand why it works                 |
   |  [x] it is tested, including the edge cases    |
   |  [x] it is documented where it needs to be     |
   |  [x] I will fix it if it breaks                |
   |  [x] others are free to improve it             |
   +------------------------------------------------+
`} />

1. **Before submitting, ask: would I sign this?**
2. **If not, fix what stops me**, or state it openly.
3. **Write commit messages and pull request notes** that explain the work.
4. **Own problems in my code** and fix them promptly.
5. **Treat others' code with respect**, and welcome changes to mine.

The other side of the mistake is anonymity by habit: commits with messages
like "fix" or "update", pull requests with no description, code with no
indication of why it is the way it is. Nobody has to sign their name
literally for the work to be anonymous in effect. The fix is to write as if
the next reader will know it was me, because in version control they will.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is confusing pride with possessiveness: refusing
changes to "my" module, or taking review comments personally. The fix is
the book's balance: proud of the work, open to it being improved.

Signing also has a public form for many developers: open-source
contributions, technical writing and talks. Each puts a name on work that
others can inspect. The habits that make that work good, clarity, testing
and care for the reader, are the same habits that make private work good,
and practising them in public tends to raise the standard everywhere.

### In real systems

Commit authorship, code owners files, maintainers lists and blameless
post-incident reviews all express the idea. Ownership is visible, but
mistakes are treated as learning, not as grounds for blame.

#### Putting the chapter together

Read as a whole, the chapter moves from people to tools to purpose. Small
stable teams create the conditions for good work.

Choosing practices by
results keeps the team honest about what helps. The starter kit makes
delivery reliable and repeatable. Delighting users points everything at
the right goal. Signing the work ties it back to individual pride and
responsibility, the theme the book opened with in chapter 1.

That circle is deliberate. The book starts with a single developer taking
responsibility and ends with the same developer, now part of a team,
putting their name on what they build. Every habit in between, from
tracer bullets to property tests to refactoring, is a way of making that
signature mean something.

#### A checklist for any project

When I join a project or start a new one, the chapter gives a short list
of questions. Is the team small enough for everyone to know what the
others are doing? Can anyone build, test and deploy from a clean checkout
with one command?

Do tests run on every change and block merges when they
fail? Is there a test for each fixed bug? Do we know how users will judge
success, and do we check it after release? Would each of us sign the last
thing we merged?

Any "no" is a place to start. I do not need to fix them all at once; one
improvement each week compounds, which is the knowledge-portfolio idea
from chapter 1 applied to the project instead of to myself.

### Check yourself

**What does "sign your work" promise?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That I stand behind the work: it is well-made, tested and documented, and
I will take responsibility for fixing it.

</details>

**What is the "prejudice" the book warns against?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Defending my own code against change, or dismissing others' code, instead
of treating code as the team's and approaching others' work with respect.

</details>

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Pragmatic Projects">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

At the beginner level, the project habits start with my own changes:
every change goes through version control with a clear message, every
change runs the tests, and every bug I fix gets a test first. I ask
myself whether I would sign each pull request.

Example: I find a bug where a date shows in the wrong format. I write a
test that fails, fix the bug, and the test stays in the suite.

Mistake: fixing the bug and moving on without a test, so the same bug
returns three months later after a refactor. Fix: find bugs once; the test
comes before the fix.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level, I help the team keep its windows unbroken:
fixing flaky tests, automating a manual step, writing integration tests
for the parts that break most. I think about test states, not just
coverage.

Example: I replace a five-step manual deploy checklist with a script and
add it to the pipeline, then delete the checklist.

Mistake: adding tests to raise the coverage number without checking
meaningful outcomes. Fix: write tests for boundaries and error states, and
run a mutation tool on one module to see what the tests miss.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

At the advanced level, I shape how the team works: proposing practices
that solve our real problems, organising work around user outcomes, and
making the pipeline the single path to production.

Example: I introduce a quarterly mutation-testing check on the most
critical modules, and the team adds boundary tests where mutants survive.

Mistake: introducing a practice because another company uses it. Fix:
state the problem it solves for us and the measure that will show whether
it helps.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Edge cases at project level are the rare events the pipeline and tests
must still handle: the first deploy of a new service, a rollback, a
release on a holiday, a migration on a very large table.

Example: a rollback script that was never tested fails during the first
real incident.

Mistake: automating only the happy path of deployment. Fix: test
rollbacks and migrations in staging regularly, as part of the pipeline.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Corner cases combine failures: a deploy and a database migration that
succeed separately but fail together; a test that passes in isolation and
fails when run after another test; a flaky test that hides a real
intermittent bug.

Example: a test passes alone but fails in the full suite because an
earlier test leaves data in a shared database.

Mistake: marking such a test as flaky and ignoring it. Fix: find the
shared state, isolate tests from each other, and treat flakiness as a bug
in either the test or the code.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

In production, the starter kit is visible as reliability: every release is
built from a tag, deployable by anyone, and reversible. Outcomes agreed
with users are measured after each release, and incidents produce tests
and pipeline checks so they do not recur.

Example: after an outage caused by a missing environment variable, the
team adds a startup validation and a pipeline check that compares
required variables with the deployed configuration.

Mistake: post-incident reviews that end with "be more careful". Fix: every
review ends with an automated check, test or guard.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The mindset is ownership with openness. I care about the outcome for
users, take responsibility for my part, and welcome others improving it.
I judge practices by results, not by fashion.

Example: when a user reports a problem in code I wrote, my first reaction
is curiosity about what I missed, not defence.

Mistake: measuring my contribution by lines written or tickets closed.
Fix: measure by user outcomes and the health of the codebase I leave
behind.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

At system design level, the starter kit becomes infrastructure: every
service built and deployed the same way from its repository, environments
created from code, and a shared testing strategy across services, from
unit to contract to end-to-end tests.

Example: a template repository that gives every new service a pipeline,
test layout, health checks and deployment configuration on day one.

Mistake: each team building its own pipeline differently, so knowledge does
not transfer. Fix: a paved road of shared templates, with teams free to
deviate when they have a reason.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

Strategically, a pragmatic organisation invests in small, stable,
cross-functional teams, delivery capability and user outcomes, and resists
cargo cults. It measures what matters, such as lead time, incident rate
and user satisfaction, and improves in small steps.

Example: leadership tracks four delivery measures and one user outcome per
team, and funds improvements to whichever is weakest.

Mistake: reorganising teams every year, which destroys the stability the
book values. Fix: change team boundaries rarely and deliberately, when
the product boundaries change.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>How small is a small team?</summary>

The book suggests up to about ten to twelve people. Below that, everyone
can know what everyone else is doing. Above it, communication overhead
grows quickly and splitting usually helps.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What code coverage should we aim for?</summary>

The book discourages targeting a number. Aim to cover meaningful states:
empty, one, many, boundaries and errors. Coverage tools are useful for
finding untested code, and mutation testing for checking whether tests
detect changes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is mutation testing practical on a large codebase?</summary>

It can be slow, since it runs the tests once per mutant. Teams usually run
it on critical modules, on changed files in a pull request, or on a
schedule rather than on every commit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if users cannot say how they will judge success?</summary>

Ask about their current pain instead: what takes too long, what goes
wrong, what they dread. The success measures are usually the opposite of
those pains.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does signing my work mean owning a module alone?</summary>

No. Code belongs to the team, and others should change it freely. Signing
means standing behind the parts I wrote or changed, and making sure they
are worth putting my name on.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where does the book's postface leave me?</summary>

With responsibility: software affects people's lives, so I should think
about the harm it could do and whether I would be happy to use it myself.
It is a reminder that the habits in the book serve people, not code.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I bring these ideas to a team that does not share them?</summary>

Start with my own work, as the book suggests with stone soup in chapter 1.
Automate one painful step, add tests to the module I touch, and fix the
broken windows near my changes. When others see the time saved, they
usually ask how to do the same, which is a far easier conversation than
proposing a new process.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Are end-to-end tests worth their cost?</summary>

A few are, covering the most important user journeys such as signing up,
paying, or saving work. They catch wiring mistakes no other test sees.
Many of them become slow and fragile, so most checks belong in faster unit
and integration tests, with end-to-end tests kept for the paths that
matter most.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should I automate first?</summary>

The step that is most painful, most error-prone or most often skipped.
Usually that is the build and test run on every commit, then deployment,
then environment setup for new team members. Each automated step pays back
every time it runs, so frequency matters more than difficulty.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do I know whether a team practice is working?</summary>

Decide before adopting it what should change, and look a few weeks later.
If the practice was meant to cut review time, measure review time. If
nothing measurable changed and nobody misses it when skipped, it is a
candidate for dropping.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Keep teams small and stable, and give them every skill needed to deliver end to end.
- Apply individual habits at team scale: no broken windows, watch for boiled frogs, avoid duplicated work.
- Schedule improvement work, or it will never happen.
- Do what works for us, not what is fashionable; one size fits no one.
- The starter kit: version control drives everything, testing is thorough and automatic, nothing is manual.
- Test state coverage, not code coverage; use saboteurs to test the tests; find each bug only once.
- Delight users by solving their real problem; ask how they will judge success.
- Sign your work: take pride and responsibility, and respect others' work.

## Glossary

- **Cross-functional team**: a team with all the skills needed to deliver a feature end to end.
- **Cargo cult**: copying visible rituals in the hope of getting results, without the underlying causes.
- **Pipeline**: an automated sequence that builds, tests and deploys code in response to repository events.
- **Unit test**: a test of one module in isolation.
- **Integration test**: a test that modules work together.
- **Validation**: checking that the system meets users' real needs.
- **Performance test**: a test of speed and capacity under realistic load.
- **Saboteur**: a deliberately introduced bug used to check that tests catch it.
- **Mutation testing**: automatically generating small code changes to see which survive the tests.
- **Code coverage**: the share of code lines or branches run by the tests.
- **State coverage**: how well tests explore the meaningful states and inputs of the code.
- **Regression test**: a test that guards against a previously fixed bug returning.
- **Outcome**: the change in the user's world that defines success.

## Exercises and self-check

**Warm-up.** List every manual step in how my current project gets from a
commit to production. For each, note who knows how to do it and what
breaks if it is skipped.

**Build.** Pick one module with tests. Introduce three deliberate bugs, one
at a time: a changed comparison, an off-by-one, and a removed line. Record
which ones the tests catch, then add tests until all three are caught.
Optionally run a mutation testing tool on the same module.

**Stretch.** For a feature I am working on, ask a real user the success
question. Write the answers as two or three measurable outcomes, and plan
how to check them a month after release.

<details>
<summary>Self-check: what are the three legs of the starter kit?</summary>

Version control driving builds, tests and releases; thorough automated
testing; and full automation with no manual procedures.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Self-check: why did the weak suite let both age mutants survive?</summary>

It only tested ages far from the boundary, 30 and 5, where the original
and the mutants agree. Boundary inputs, 18 and 17, distinguish them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Self-check: what is the difference between delivering code and delighting users?</summary>

Delivering code measures success by features shipped; delighting users
measures it by whether their real problem was solved and their
expectations exceeded.

</details>
