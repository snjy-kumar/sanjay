This chapter is about the person writing the code, not the code. Its seven
short topics cover owning my career and my results, keeping a codebase from
rotting, starting change and noticing slow decline, agreeing how good
software must be, investing in what I know, and communicating it. Every
later chapter assumes these habits, so this is where the book, and my study
of it, begins.

## Before you start

After this chapter I will be able to:

- respond to a failure with options and a recommendation instead of an excuse;
- find the broken windows in a codebase, board them up, and stop new ones;
- start a change without waiting for permission to do all of it, and spot slow decline with a fixed baseline;
- agree a quality bar with the people who use the software, and write it down;
- run a written, reviewed plan for what I learn each quarter;
- plan a design note, email or pull request description for its audience.

What I should already know: nothing technical. It helps to have worked on
one project with other people, because most of the topics are about what
happens between people. In my learning path this is the first reading of
Phase 0 of the
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-0')}>traditional core roadmap</a>.

Reading time: about 50 minutes for this page, plus an hour for the
exercises. The book's own chapter is short; the habits take months.

## Topic 1. It's Your Life

### In one sentence

I am in charge of my own working life, so when something about it is
wrong, my first job is to change what I can.

### The idea

The claim is simple: if I do not like my job, my tools, my team or what I
know, I can change it. Software is one of the few fields where that is
genuinely true. The skills are portable, the demand is broad, and almost
everything I need to learn is available to me today.

A programming book opens with this because the opposite belief undermines
every later habit. A developer who thinks the codebase is somebody else's
problem will not fix broken windows. One who thinks learning is the
employer's job will not keep a knowledge portfolio. Agency, the belief that
my actions change outcomes, is the precondition for all of it.

The obvious alternative is to complain productively: point out every
problem to whoever is in charge. That falls short because it hands the
problem back to someone else. The pragmatic move is to treat each complaint
as the first line of a plan I own.

So "the build is slow" becomes "the build is slow; I measured it at eleven
minutes; here are two fixes; I can do the first one this sprint". Same
observation, but now it has an owner and a next step.

Careers compound, which is why this matters so much. A habit of changing
what I can change produces a slightly better toolset and skill set every
year. A habit of waiting produces drift, and over a decade the two curves
end up far apart.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I join a project where nobody runs the whole test suite before
merging, and twice in my first month a merge breaks the main branch for a
day. The passive response is to be careful myself and complain at the
retrospective. The agency response is to write a ten-line script that runs
the suite, add it to the repository, and offer to wire it into the merge
check.

I did not need permission to write a script. Nobody had to agree to "a new
testing culture". The change started because I started it.

**Realistic.** I am two years into maintaining a legacy billing service and
I want to work on data infrastructure. My options, in order of cost:

| Rung | What I do | What it costs | Evidence it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Volunteer for the billing reporting pipeline, which touches the data warehouse | Some of my current focus | Real data work, with people I know |
| 2 | Study data topics a few fixed hours a week, with notes in a repository | My own time | A visible record of learning |
| 3 | Ask the data team lead for a transfer, showing rungs 1 and 2 | Some awkwardness | A move inside a known organisation |
| 4 | Change jobs, using rungs 1 to 3 as the story | Context and relationships | A new role in the area I want |

The point is not that everyone should switch jobs. Each rung is mine to
take, and the cheap rungs come first.

### Step by step

<Flow caption="The agency ladder. Read left to right: try the cheapest change first, and carry the evidence from each rung to the next." steps={[
  "Name what I want to be different",
  "Change what I do in the current role",
  "Change what I know, on my own time",
  "Change the team",
  "Change the organisation",
]} />

Walking the ladder for any complaint looks like this:

1. **Write the complaint as a fact.** "Deploys take forty minutes and need
   me to watch them", not "deploys are awful".
2. **Split it into mine and not mine.** Mine: I can script the watching.
   Not mine: the shared runner is underpowered.
3. **Do the cheapest thing on the "mine" list this week.** Here, a script
   that posts to chat when the deploy finishes.
4. **Turn the "not mine" list into a proposal with evidence.** Twenty
   deploys timed, the runner's CPU graph, a cost estimate for a bigger one.
5. **Check after a month.** If nothing on either list moved, climb a rung.

### Common mistake

The first mistake is reading this topic as "if you are unhappy, quit".
Quitting is the last rung and the most expensive one: I lose context,
relationships and the deep knowledge of one system that makes me valuable.
The fix is to climb the ladder in order, making each rung concrete enough
to test within a few weeks.

The opposite mistake is reading it as "you alone are responsible for
everything that happens to you". A layoff or a market downturn is not mine
to change. The fix is to separate the two lists quickly and spend my
energy only on the first.

### In real systems

In teams this shows up as the difference between filing a ticket and
filing a ticket with a proposed fix attached. In on-call rotations it is the
person who leaves the runbook better than they found it. Most good internal
tools began as one developer's script for their own annoyance.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>The test suite takes 25 minutes and I hate waiting. What is the agency response, in one line?</summary>

Measure where the 25 minutes go, then do the cheapest fix I control (run
only the affected tests locally, parallelise one slow file) and bring the
rest to the team as a proposal with the measurements.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is "change jobs" the last rung rather than the first?</summary>

It is the most expensive: it throws away context, relationships and deep
knowledge of a system. Cheaper rungs often solve the problem, and if they
do not, they produce the evidence that makes the last rung succeed.

</details>

## Topic 2. The Cat Ate My Source Code

### In one sentence

When I take on a task I own the result, and when it goes wrong I say so
early and bring options, not excuses.

### The idea

Agreeing to do something means taking responsibility for the outcome, not
just for the effort. Things will go wrong, because software is full of
surprises. What builds trust is how I handle it: early, honest, and with a
way forward.

An excuse shifts the problem onto someone else: the network, the library,
the unclear requirements, the cat. An option keeps the problem with me and
turns it into a decision someone can make. The facts can be identical; the
framing is what differs.

Responsibility needs trust to work. If admitting a mistake gets me punished,
I will hide mistakes, and hidden mistakes are the expensive ones. So the
topic has two sides: own my results, and help make it safe for others to
own theirs.

Responsibility is also something I accept, which means I can decline it.
If I believe a task cannot be done safely on that timeline, the
responsible move is to say so before accepting, or to accept with an
explicit condition. Once I have accepted, I own the risks too, including
the ones that depend on other people.

The book suggests a rehearsal before any "it went wrong" conversation.
Imagine what they will ask, and say my explanation out loud, even to a
rubber duck or a plant. Excuses sound lame when spoken; options sound like
a plan.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I promised a code review by the end of the day and missed it
because a production incident ate the afternoon.

<Compare left="Excuse" right="Option" caption="The same facts, told two ways. Look at where the decision ends up: the right column hands it back to the person who needs it." rows={[
  ["Sorry, the incident took all afternoon.", "The incident took the afternoon. I can review the first half tonight so you can keep going, and the rest by ten tomorrow."],
  ["I didn't get to it.", "If it is blocking you, Priya knows that module and could review it now. Shall I ask her?"],
  ["It's not my fault.", "I should have told you at three o'clock when I knew I would miss it. Next time I will."],
]} />

**Realistic.** I am leading a database migration. Nine days before the
announced date, I find a reporting job nobody mentioned that relies on the
old database's date handling. The excuse is "nobody told us about it",
which is true and useless.

The options version is a short message to the people who own the date:

| Option | What happens | Cost | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Keep the date, pause the report one week | Migrate on time, fix the report afterwards | One late report | Low |
| 2. Keep the date, keep the report | Add a compatibility view that mimics the old behaviour | Two extra days, plus a view to remove later | Medium |
| 3. Move the date one week | Fix the report properly first | A changed announcement | Lowest technically |

I recommend option 1 and say why. Then I add the process fix: every future
migration starts by listing every reader of the old database from its
access logs. Owning the result includes owning the gap that let it happen.

### Step by step

<Flow caption="Turning a failure into an options message. Each box is one paragraph of the message, in this order." steps={[
  "What happened, facts only",
  "What it affects",
  "Two or three options with cost and risk",
  "My recommendation and why",
  "What I change so it does not recur",
]} />

1. **Facts first, no adjectives.** "The report job reads dates in the old
   format" rather than "a hidden landmine".
2. **Impact next.** Who notices, when, and how badly.
3. **Options, each with cost and risk.** At least two, or it is an
   ultimatum rather than a choice.
4. **A recommendation.** The person deciding wants my judgment, not just a
   menu.
5. **The process fix.** One concrete change, with an owner.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is confusing responsibility with blame. Taking
responsibility does not mean saying "it was all my fault" when it was not;
it means taking charge of what happens next. The fix is to separate three
things in my head before I speak: what happened, what we can do now, and
what we change.

A second mistake is the late warning. Telling someone on Friday that the
Tuesday deadline slipped is worse than the slip itself. The fix is a rule:
I raise a risk the day I see it, even if I think I can still recover.

### In real systems

Every good incident process encodes this topic. An incident report has a
timeline (facts), a mitigation (the option chosen) and action items (the
process changes), and no culprit. That is what blameless postmortems mean.
A bug-fix pull request description works the same way: what broke, why,
what this change does, and which new test stops it coming back.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>What turns a list of options into an ultimatum?</summary>

Having only one option. A choice needs at least two real alternatives, each
with its cost, so the person deciding can weigh them.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is "I take full responsibility; it was my fault" always the right response?</summary>

No. Responsibility is about owning what happens next, not about claiming
blame for things I did not cause. Say the facts accurately, then lead the
recovery.

</details>

## Topic 3. Software Entropy

### In one sentence

Code rots when small messes are left in place, so I fix or visibly board up
every broken window I find.

### The idea

Entropy is the physicist's word for disorder, which tends to increase on
its own. Codebases behave the same way: each change is made under some
pressure, each shortcut is locally reasonable, and the shortcuts add up.
Developers call the result software rot or technical debt.

The book explains why some codebases rot fast with the broken windows
theory from urban studies. A building with one unrepaired broken window
soon gets more, because the window signals that nobody cares. In code, one
ugly hack invites the next, one skipped test makes the next skip feel
normal, and a build with forty warnings makes the forty-first invisible.

The rule that follows: fix bad designs, wrong decisions and poor code as
soon as I see them. If I cannot fix one properly now, I board it up. That
means making it visible and contained, so the next person can see it is
known and owned.

Boarding up can be a clear "not implemented" error in place of dangerous
code, a dated TODO with a name, a failing case recorded as a known test, or
bad data replaced by a value that fails loudly. Each costs minutes. Each
stops the window from teaching "mess is normal".

The flip side is about clean code. The book's image is a firefighting crew
that rolls out a mat to protect the carpet of a beautiful house, even while
the house is burning. Pristine code gets that respect, which is why the
first broken window matters far more than the hundredth.

### Worked example

**Simple.** A broken-window census for a small TypeScript project. It
counts three signals that say mess is normal: TODO comments without an
owner, disabled lint rules and skipped tests.

```ts
// A broken-window census: count the signals that say "mess is normal here".
const files: Record<string, string> = {
  'src/cart.ts': 'export function total() {}\n// TODO: handle refunds\n// eslint-disable-next-line\n',
  'src/cart.test.ts': "it.skip('applies coupon', () => {})\nit('adds item', () => {})\n",
  'src/user.ts': 'export const user = 1;\n',
};
const signals: [string, RegExp][] = [
  ['TODO without owner', /\/\/ TODO(?!\(\w+\))/g],
  ['disabled lint rule', /eslint-disable/g],
  ['skipped test', /\b(it|test|describe)\.skip\(/g],
];
let total = 0;
for (const [name, re] of signals) {
  const hits = Object.entries(files).flatMap(([path, text]) =>
    [...text.matchAll(re)].map(() => path));
  total += hits.length;
  console.log(`${name.padEnd(20)} ${hits.length}  ${hits.join(', ')}`);
}
console.log(`broken windows: ${total}`);
```

Running it with `node --experimental-strip-types census.ts` prints:

```text
TODO without owner   1  src/cart.ts
disabled lint rule   1  src/cart.ts
skipped test         1  src/cart.test.ts
broken windows: 3
```

The number is not the point; visibility is. A rule like "the census never
goes up in a pull request" stops new windows breaking. Notice that the
pattern accepts `// TODO(sanjay)`: a TODO with a name is boarded up, not
broken.

**Realistic.** A service has an integration test that fails about one run
in twenty. Nobody knows why, so the team re-runs the pipeline when it goes
red. That is the worst kind of broken window, because it teaches everyone
that red does not mean broken.

The fix comes in three moves. Today, move the test to a quarantine list that
runs but does not block, with an owner and an expiry date. This week, run it
a few hundred times in a loop with logging and fix the timing dependency.
Then make the rule: an expired quarantine entry fails the build.

### Step by step

<Flow caption="How one flaky test becomes a culture of ignoring red builds. Every step is reasonable on its own; that is why the slide happens." steps={[
  "One test fails 1 run in 20",
  "People re-run on red",
  "Red stops meaning broken",
  "A real failure is re-run and passes by luck",
  "A bug ships on a green build",
]} />

The repair runs the same chain backwards:

1. **Restore the signal.** Quarantine the flaky test so red means broken
   again. This is the boarding-up, and it is done today.
2. **Make the window visible.** The quarantine list is a file in the
   repository, reviewed like code, with an owner per entry.
3. **Fix the cause.** Loop the test, find the race or shared state, fix it,
   and remove it from quarantine.
4. **Stop the next one.** An expiry date per entry, enforced by the build.

### Common mistake

The mistake is treating every broken window as an emergency and stopping
feature work to refactor everything. That burns goodwill and often leaves a
half-done refactor, which is itself a broken window. The fix is to board up
everything immediately, which is cheap, and then repair in priority order
as part of normal work.

The opposite mistake is deciding the codebase is already so bad that one
more hack cannot matter. That is exactly the slide the topic describes.
Every hack I add teaches the next developer the same lesson.

### In real systems

Production broken windows are often not in the code at all. They are
alerts that fire every night and get ignored, dashboards nobody trusts,
runbooks describing a system that no longer exists, warnings in the build
log, and feature flags left on for years. The same rule applies: every
alert actionable, zero build warnings, every flag with an owner and an
expiry.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a broken window and a boarded-up one?</summary>

A boarded-up window is visible, contained and owned: a named TODO, a
quarantined test with an expiry, a loud "not implemented" error. A broken
one is silent and ownerless, so it teaches that mess is acceptable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does the first broken window matter more than the hundredth?</summary>

Because it changes the norm. While the code is clean, people protect it.
The first unrepaired mess tells everyone that care is optional, and the
slide starts there.

</details>

## Topic 4. Stone Soup and Boiled Frogs

### In one sentence

People join a change that is already working, so I start small and show
it; and slow decline goes unnoticed, so I measure against a fixed
baseline.

### The idea

This topic pairs two old stories. In the stone-soup folktale, hungry
travellers arrive in a village whose people will not share food. They put
a stone in a pot of boiling water and say the soup is nearly ready, just
missing a carrot, an onion, a bit of salt. Each villager adds one thing,
and soon there is real soup that nobody would have contributed to up front.

The software lesson is about starting change. If I ask for permission to
build a whole new system, I get meetings, objections and a budget fight. If
I build a small, useful piece and show it working, people start offering
the carrots: "could it also do this?" Being a catalyst means making the
first, cheap version real.

The boiled-frog story is the warning on the other side. A frog dropped into
hot water jumps out; one in water heated slowly supposedly stays until it
is too late. (Real frogs do jump, but the metaphor is the point.) Projects
decline the same way, one small step at a time, each step too small to
trigger an alarm.

The defence is to keep the big picture in view and to measure. Feelings
compare today with yesterday, and yesterday was almost the same.
Measurements can compare today with a fixed point from months ago, and that
comparison shows the drift.

There is a tension between the two stories. Stone soup quietly grows scope
by small additions; boiled frogs are hurt by exactly that. The difference
is intent and visibility: the catalyst knows where the soup is going and
says so, while the frog never looks at the thermometer.

### Worked example

**Simple: stone soup.** My team argues for months about adopting a design
system. Instead of writing a proposal, I extract the three buttons and the
form field we already use into a small shared package, with a page that
shows them. Within two weeks, two other developers have added a modal and a
date picker. Nobody approved "a design system". It exists because it was
useful on day one.

**Realistic: boiled frog.** A service's 95th-percentile latency (the time
under which 95 out of 100 requests finish) creeps up about three percent a
week. Week-on-week, nothing looks wrong. Against a fixed baseline, it does:

```ts
// Boiled frogs: compare each week's p95 latency with a fixed baseline,
// not with last week, so slow drift still trips the alarm.
type Week = { week: number; p95ms: number };
const weeks: Week[] = [
  { week: 1, p95ms: 200 }, { week: 2, p95ms: 206 }, { week: 3, p95ms: 212 },
  { week: 4, p95ms: 219 }, { week: 5, p95ms: 225 }, { week: 6, p95ms: 232 },
];
const baseline = weeks[0].p95ms;
for (let i = 1; i < weeks.length; i++) {
  const w = weeks[i];
  const vsLast = (w.p95ms - weeks[i - 1].p95ms) / weeks[i - 1].p95ms;
  const vsBase = (w.p95ms - baseline) / baseline;
  const alarm = vsBase > 0.1 ? 'ALARM' : 'ok';
  console.log(`week ${w.week}: +${(vsLast * 100).toFixed(1)}% vs last, +${(vsBase * 100).toFixed(1)}% vs baseline -> ${alarm}`);
}
```

The output:

```text
week 2: +3.0% vs last, +3.0% vs baseline -> ok
week 3: +2.9% vs last, +6.0% vs baseline -> ok
week 4: +3.3% vs last, +9.5% vs baseline -> ok
week 5: +2.7% vs last, +12.5% vs baseline -> ALARM
week 6: +3.1% vs last, +16.0% vs baseline -> ALARM
```

No single week moved more than about three percent, so a week-on-week alarm
set at five percent would never fire. The baseline alarm fires in week 5.
That is the boiled frog, caught by a thermometer that does not move with
the water.

  ["2", "206", "+3.0%", "+3.0%", "ok"],
  ["3", "212", "+2.9%", "+6.0%", "ok"],

Running a stone-soup change follows its own steps:

1. **Pick the smallest piece that is useful on its own.** Three shared
   buttons, not a design system.
2. **Make it real and visible.** A working package with a demo page.
3. **Invite additions.** "If you need a component, add it here."
4. **Say where it is going.** A one-paragraph note on the goal, so the
   additions point the same way and nobody feels tricked.

### Common mistake

The stone-soup mistake is using the technique to sneak in something people
would reject if they saw it whole. That spends trust I will need later. The
fix is to be open about the destination; the small start is about proving
value, not hiding it.

The boiled-frog mistake is alerting on rates of change only. A rate alarm
catches cliffs and misses slopes. The fix is to keep at least one alarm
against a fixed baseline, or a budget, such as "p95 stays under 250 ms",
which does not move with the data.

### In real systems

Stone soup is how most internal platforms start: one team's deploy script
becomes everyone's pipeline. Boiled frogs are why performance budgets,
bundle-size limits and service-level objectives exist. Each is a fixed line
that slow drift eventually crosses, which a week-on-week comparison would
never show.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Why does a week-on-week alert miss slow decline?</summary>

Because each week is compared with the week before, which was almost the
same. Small steps never cross the threshold, even when their sum is large.
A fixed baseline or budget adds the steps up.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What separates being a catalyst from scope creep?</summary>

Intent and visibility. The catalyst starts small to prove value and says
openly where it is going. Scope creep is unplanned growth that nobody is
watching.

</details>

## Topic 5. Good-Enough Software

### In one sentence

Perfect software does not exist, so I agree the quality bar with the people
who will use it and stop when I reach it.

### The idea

Every piece of software has defects, and every extra unit of polish costs
time that could have gone to something else. The question is never "is it
perfect?" but "is it good enough for the people who use it, for what they
use it for?" Good enough is not sloppy. It is a quality level chosen on
purpose.

The key move is to make quality a requirement, discussed with users like
any other. Some systems need near-perfection: a payment ledger or a
medical device. Others gain more from being available next week than from
being flawless next quarter. Users often prefer something useful today over
something perfect later.

The book also warns about the opposite failure: not knowing when to stop.
A painter who keeps adding layers ruins the painting, and a developer who
keeps refining code past what anyone needs is spending other people's time.
Software that ships and gets real feedback improves faster than software
polished in private.

Good enough also has a floor. It never means shipping something that loses
data, leaks secrets or breaks the law. Those are not quality trade-offs;
they are constraints.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I am building an internal script that renames files in a
shared folder each morning. The users are my team, and the script runs
once a day with a person watching. Good enough here is: it works on the
normal cases, it refuses to run if the folder is missing, and it prints
what it did. It does not need a GUI, a config file or retries.

**Realistic.** A product manager wants a new export feature for a
reporting tool. Rather than guessing, I write down the quality questions
and get answers:

| Question | Answer from users | What it means for the build |
|---|---|---|
| How big is the biggest export? | About 50,000 rows | Stream the file; no need for a background job yet |
| How fresh must the data be? | Yesterday's is fine | Export from the nightly copy, not the live database |
| What happens if it fails? | They retry later | A clear error message; no automatic retry |
| Must the format be exact? | It goes into a spreadsheet | CSV with a header row and quoted fields |
| Is it legal or financial data? | Yes, invoices | Audit log entry for every export (floor, not trade-off) |

That table is the quality bar. It took twenty minutes, and it saved me from
building a job queue nobody needed and from missing the audit log, which
was not optional.

### Step by step

<Compare left="Polish forever" right="Good enough, on purpose" caption="Two ways to finish a feature. Look at who decides when it is done: the right column asks the users." rows={[
  ["The developer decides when it is done", "The users and the developer agree the bar first"],
  ["Every edge case gets equal effort", "Effort goes where users said it matters"],
  ["Ships late, feedback arrives late", "Ships when the bar is met, feedback arrives early"],
  ["Quality is a feeling", "Quality is a written list that can be checked"],
  ["Hard limits mixed in with nice-to-haves", "Hard limits (data, security, law) listed separately and never traded"],
]} />

1. **List the users and what they do with it.** Who, how often, how much
   data.
2. **Ask the quality questions.** Size, freshness, failure behaviour,
   format, regulation.
3. **Separate the floor from the trade-offs.** Security, data integrity
   and law go on the floor.
4. **Write the bar down** in the ticket or design note.
5. **Ship at the bar, then measure.** Real use decides the next round.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is using "good enough" as cover for low effort: no
tests, no error handling, "we will fix it later". That is not a chosen
quality bar; it is no bar at all. The fix is that good enough is always
written down and agreed, and the floor is never negotiable.

The other mistake is gold-plating: adding features and polish nobody asked
for. The fix is the same written bar, used in reverse. If a piece of work is
not on it, it waits for evidence that someone needs it.

### In real systems

Service-level objectives, or SLOs, are good-enough software made formal: a
target such as "99.9 percent of requests succeed each month", agreed with
the business. The gap between that and 100 percent is an error budget. The
team spends it on shipping faster and tightens up when it runs out.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Name one thing that is never part of the good-enough trade-off.</summary>

Any hard constraint: not losing data, not leaking secrets, complying with
the law. Those are the floor, not the dial.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Who should set the quality bar for a feature?</summary>

The people who use it, together with the developers, so the bar reflects
real needs and real costs. It should be written down so it can be checked.

</details>

## Topic 6. Your Knowledge Portfolio

### In one sentence

What I know is an asset that loses value over time, so I manage it like an
investment portfolio: invest regularly, diversify, balance risk, and review.

### The idea

Technical knowledge is an expiring asset. The framework I master today may
be a legacy concern in five years. If I stop learning, my value slowly
falls, even if I am working hard every day on the same things.

The book maps learning onto investing, and the analogy holds up well:

| Investing rule | What it means for learning |
|---|---|
| Invest regularly | A fixed time slot every week, not a burst once a year |
| Diversify | Several areas, not only my current stack |
| Manage risk | Mix safe bets (my main language) with speculative ones (an unusual language or field) |
| Buy low, sell high | Learn emerging things before they are popular |
| Review and rebalance | Each quarter, check the mix and adjust it |

The book also gives concrete goals: learn a new language every year, read a
technical book regularly, read beyond technical topics, take courses, join
local groups, try different environments, and keep current. Each is a way of
investing; none is required in a fixed form.

The last part of the topic is about thinking critically. Not everything I
read is true, and the most visible information is not always the best. I
should ask who wrote it, why, what they gain, what the context was, and why
this is a problem worth reading about.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I keep hours per quarter in three buckets: core (the stack I use
daily), adjacent (things one step away, like databases or networking) and
speculative (a different language or field). A tiny script tells me how to
rebalance toward my target mix:

```ts
// A knowledge portfolio as hours per quarter, rebalanced toward a target mix.
const target = { core: 0.5, adjacent: 0.3, speculative: 0.2 };
const actual = { core: 52, adjacent: 6, speculative: 2 }; // hours this quarter
const sum = Object.values(actual).reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);
for (const k of Object.keys(target) as (keyof typeof target)[]) {
  const share = actual[k] / sum;
  const gap = target[k] - share;
  console.log(`${k.padEnd(12)} ${(share * 100).toFixed(0).padStart(3)}% of ${sum}h, target ${target[k] * 100}%, next quarter ${gap > 0 ? 'add' : 'trim'} ${Math.abs(Math.round(gap * sum))}h`);
}
```

It prints:

```text
core          87% of 60h, target 50%, next quarter trim 22h
adjacent      10% of 60h, target 30%, next quarter add 12h
speculative    3% of 60h, target 20%, next quarter add 10h
```

The result is typical. Without a plan, nearly all learning goes to the
urgent, everyday stack, and the portfolio drifts into a single holding.

**Realistic.** My learning path itself is a portfolio. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>
is the safe, long-term holding: C, data structures, operating systems. The
modern and AI roadmaps are the growth holdings. Each quarter I look at the
study log, see where the hours went, and move the next quarter's slots.

### Step by step

<Timeline caption="One quarter of a knowledge portfolio. Look at the rhythm: small weekly deposits, one monthly check, one quarterly rebalance." items={[
  { when: "Every week", what: "Two fixed learning slots, logged with what I did and one thing I can now do" },
  { when: "Every month", what: "One book chapter or course module finished, one small thing built with it" },
  { when: "Mid-quarter", what: "Check the hours by bucket; fix any bucket at zero" },
  { when: "End of quarter", what: "Rebalance: compare with the target mix, pick next quarter's speculative topic" },
]} />

Thinking critically about a source is a short routine I can run in two
minutes:

1. **Who wrote it, and what do they gain?** A vendor's benchmark of its own
   database is a sales document first.
2. **What was the context?** A tip from a team of ten thousand may not fit
   a team of three.
3. **Can I check one claim myself?** Run the benchmark, read the source,
   try the example.
4. **What would change my mind?** Write it down before deciding.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is mistaking consumption for investment: reading
articles and watching videos without building anything. Knowledge I never
apply decays fastest. The fix is a rule that each learning slot ends with
something made: a snippet, a note in my own words, or a small program.

Another mistake is chasing every new thing, which is all speculation and no
core. The fix is the target mix: speculative learning gets a fixed share,
not all of it.

### In real systems

Teams that run internal reading groups, rotate people across services, or
hold regular "learning hours" are running a shared portfolio. When a
technology shift arrives, a move to a new cloud, a new language, or machine
learning features, those teams adapt in months instead of years.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Why is knowledge called an expiring asset?</summary>

Because the specific technologies I know lose value as the field moves on.
Without new investment, what I know becomes less useful each year.

</details>

<details>
<summary>My log shows 90 percent of learning in my daily stack. What should I do?</summary>

Rebalance: keep the core but move fixed slots to adjacent and speculative
topics next quarter, with something built in each.

</details>

## Topic 7. Communicate!

### In one sentence

An idea that does not reach other people has no effect, so I plan
communication as carefully as I plan code.

### The idea

Developers communicate constantly: in meetings, pull requests, design
notes, commit messages, chat and code comments. A brilliant idea explained
badly loses to an ordinary idea explained well. Communication is therefore
part of the job, not a soft extra.

The book breaks it into a few questions I can plan for. Know what I want to
say, and outline it first. Know my audience: what they need, what they
already know, and what they care about. Choose the moment, because the same
message lands differently at the end of a stressful day. Choose a style
that fits the reader, and make it look good, because presentation affects
whether people read it at all.

Communication also runs both ways. Involve the audience early by sharing a
draft, listen to them, and always reply, even if only to say "got it, I
will answer Thursday". Silence reads as indifference.

The book adds two modern points. Documentation should live with the code,
ideally generated from it or kept next to it, so it does not drift.
Messages to a team are also part of communication, and online writing has
the same rules as any other: read it before sending, and check the tone.

### Worked example

**Simple.** I want a teammate to review my pull request. The weak version
is a title like "fixes" and an empty description. The strong version says
what changed, why, how I tested it, and where to look first:

```text
Fix double charge when a payment retry races the webhook

Why: a retry and the provider's webhook could both mark one order paid,
creating two charges (ticket 412).
What: the payment update now uses an idempotency key per order.
Tested: new test runs retry and webhook concurrently 500 times; no
duplicates. Existing payment tests pass.
Review first: src/payments/settle.ts, the key derivation.
```

**Realistic.** I need to propose replacing a homegrown job queue with a
managed one. The same proposal goes to three audiences:

| Audience | What they care about | What I lead with | Format |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engineering lead | Risk, migration effort | The migration plan and rollback | Two-page design note |
| Finance | Cost | Current versus new monthly cost | One table in an email |
| On-call engineers | Their nights | Fewer alerts, new runbook | Ten-minute demo |

Same facts, three shapes. Sending the design note to finance would bury the
one number they need on page two.

### Step by step

<Flow caption="Planning a piece of communication. Each step is a question to answer before writing the first sentence." steps={[
  "What do I want them to know or do?",
  "Who are they, and what do they already know?",
  "When should they get it?",
  "What format and length?",
  "Draft, then read it as them",
  "Send, then listen and reply",
]} />

1. **Write the one sentence I want them to remember.** Everything else
   supports it.
2. **Sketch the audience.** Their role, their concern, their background.
3. **Pick the moment.** Not five minutes before their release.
4. **Pick the shape.** Table for comparisons, diagram for flows, prose for
   reasoning.
5. **Read it as the reader.** Cut what they do not need.
6. **Close the loop.** Ask for questions, and answer every reply.

### Common mistake

The common mistake is writing for myself: the order I discovered things,
my vocabulary, my level of detail. The reader then has to do the work of
finding the point. The fix is to put the conclusion first and the history
after, if at all.

The second mistake is not replying. Ignored emails and unanswered review
comments signal that the other person does not matter. The fix is a quick
acknowledgement with a time for the full answer.

### In real systems

In practice communication is infrastructure. Good commit messages make
`git log` and `git blame` useful years later. A design document reviewed
before coding catches problems that would cost weeks after. An incident
update every thirty minutes, even with "no change", keeps a whole company
calm.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>Why lead with the conclusion?</summary>

The reader may stop early, and they need the point to judge the details.
Putting it first respects their time and makes the rest easier to follow.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What should I do with a message I cannot answer properly today?</summary>

Reply anyway, briefly: acknowledge it and give a time for the full answer.
Silence reads as indifference.

</details>

## From beginner to strategy

<Levels label="Chapter 1, A Pragmatic Philosophy">

<Level name="Beginner" depth={3}>

The smallest working version of this chapter is three habits. When
something I own goes wrong, I tell the person affected the same day and
offer two options. When I see a small mess in code I am touching, I fix it
or label it with my name. And every week I spend one fixed hour learning
something on purpose, and write one line about it.

A worked example: my script that imports customer data crashes on a file
with an empty last line. I tell the person waiting for the data, "the
import failed on a blank line; I can strip blank lines and re-run in ten
minutes, or skip today's import". Then I fix the script, add a test for the
blank line, and note it in my log.

The beginner mistake is waiting until I have fixed the problem before
saying anything. The person waiting would rather know now. Fix: say it
early, fix it after.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate" depth={3}>

At the intermediate level the habits combine in a real codebase. A broken
window, say a skipped test, is both an entropy problem and a communication
problem. Fixing it well means boarding it up, telling the team in the pull
request, and agreeing the quality bar for that test with the people who
rely on it.

A concrete example: I find `it.skip` on the checkout tax test, skipped six
months ago with no note. I un-skip it locally, see it fail on one currency,
and write a pull request that quarantines it with my name and a two-week
expiry, links a ticket, and explains in three lines what is broken. That is
topics 2, 3, 5 and 7 in one change.

The common mistake here is fixing the window silently. The code gets better
but the team's norm does not. Fix: make the repair visible, so others learn
that windows get fixed here.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced" depth={3}>

Underneath, the whole chapter is about feedback loops. Entropy grows when
nobody owns the feedback, the warnings, the flaky tests, the slow pages.
Boiled frogs happen when feedback is relative to yesterday instead of to a
fixed point. Good-enough software is a feedback loop with users, and the
knowledge portfolio is a feedback loop with myself, closed by the quarterly
review.

Seen that way, each habit has an exact form. A broken-window rule is a
ratchet: a count that may go down but never up. A boiled-frog alarm is a
comparison with a fixed baseline or budget. A good-enough bar is a list of
checkable statements. A portfolio is a target allocation plus a review
date.

The mistake at this level is building the loop and never reading it: a
dashboard with the drift on it that nobody opens. Fix: every loop has an
owner and a moment when it is read, such as the weekly team meeting.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases" depth={3}>

Specific single boundaries where the advice changes. **A decision I had no
say in.** I can own my work without owning that decision. I say in writing
that I disagree and why, then do the work well, or decline it if it is
unsafe.

**A legacy codebase that is all broken windows.** Fixing everything is
impossible. The rule becomes "leave every file I touch slightly better, and
never add a new window". The census ratchet still applies.

**Zero-tolerance domains.** In a payment ledger, "good enough" collapses to
"correct", because the cost of a defect is too high. The trade-off moves to
other things: features, speed, polish.

**An audience of one.** Communicating with myself, for example in a
daybook, still benefits from structure. Future me is a different reader
who has forgotten the context.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases" depth={3}>

Two boundaries at once, where the advice conflicts. **Broken windows meets
good-enough software.** A deliberately rough prototype is not a broken
window if it is labelled and has an expiry date. An unlabelled one is,
because the next person cannot tell it was a choice.

**Stone soup meets boiled frogs.** My small shared component library grows
by small additions, which is the stone-soup technique. Without a stated
direction, it is also slow scope creep. The fix is a short README that says
what the library is for and what it will not include.

**Responsibility meets a deadline I cannot hit.** Owning the outcome and
telling the truth both apply. I accept the date only with an explicit
condition, such as "if the vendor API is ready by Wednesday", and I raise
the risk on the day the condition looks shaky, not on the deadline.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level" depth={3}>

When software runs for other people, every topic has an operational form.
Entropy is alert fatigue, flaky tests and stale runbooks. Boiled frogs are
the slow growth in latency, cloud cost or bundle size. Good-enough software
is a service-level objective with an error budget. Responsibility is a
blameless incident process. Communication is status updates during an
outage.

A concrete production rule set I would propose for any service: every alert
must have a runbook and be acted on, or be deleted within a week; build
warnings fail the build; latency, cost and bundle size each have a fixed
budget checked in continuous integration; every incident gets a written
review with action items and owners.

The mistake is adopting the rules without the reasons and enforcing them
blindly. A budget nobody revisits becomes another broken window. Fix:
review every budget each quarter, alongside the portfolio review.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset" depth={3}>

The habit of thought is ownership plus curiosity. Ownership says "this is
mine to improve". Curiosity says "I wonder why this is like this, and what
else I could learn". Together they produce someone who improves things and
keeps getting better at improving them.

It shows up far outside code. When a shared document is confusing, I edit
it rather than complaining. When a meeting has no agenda, I send one. When I
notice I have used the same tool the same way for two years, I spend an
hour learning its features.

The mindset mistake is turning ownership into control, insisting on doing
everything myself. The fix is to own the outcome, not every task: make
sure it happens, which often means helping someone else do it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design" depth={3}>

At system scale the chapter turns into architecture decisions. Good-enough
software becomes the non-functional requirements, the targets for
availability, latency and durability that shape the design. A system that
needs 99.99 percent availability looks very different from one that needs
99 percent.

Take a concrete system: an order service. Its broken windows are missing
runbooks, undocumented ports and dead feature flags. Its boiled frogs are
creeping queue depth and database size. Its good-enough bar is an SLO per
endpoint. Its communication is the design document and the architecture
decision records that explain why it looks the way it does.

The mistake at this scale is setting one quality bar for the whole system.
The checkout path and the recommendations widget deserve different targets.
Fix: set the bar per user journey, and spend effort where it matters.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy" depth={3}>

My plan for this chapter is to turn each topic into one running habit
during Phase 0, then check them monthly. I set up the study log with a
weekly portfolio entry, write a broken-window census for my study
repository, and keep a "mine / not mine" list for anything I complain
about.

I know I have learned it when the habits run without reminders. The
evidence is in the repository and the log, not in my memory: dated
entries, a census that only goes down, and at least one options message I
actually sent.

Done when the study log has eight consecutive weekly entries with one thing
built each, the census in my study repository is at zero, and I can point to
one change I started that someone else then added to.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Questions people ask

<details>
<summary>Is this chapter just motivational advice?</summary>

No. Each topic has a concrete practice behind it: an options message, a
census, a baseline alarm, a written quality bar, a portfolio review, a
communication plan. The page above shows each one as something I can run.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Do I have to learn a new programming language every year?</summary>

The book suggests it as a way to stretch how I think, not as a rule. The
underlying idea is regular, diversified investment. A new paradigm, a new
field or a deep dive into an adjacent area serves the same purpose.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What if my team does not care about broken windows?</summary>

Start with my own code and make the repairs visible in pull requests. Norms
spread from examples more than from arguments. That is stone soup applied
to quality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How is "good enough" different from cutting corners?</summary>

Good enough is agreed with users, written down and checkable, and it never
trades away data integrity, security or legality. Cutting corners is an
unagreed, invisible drop in quality.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does taking responsibility mean working longer hours to hit every promise?</summary>

No. It means being honest about what I can deliver, raising risks early and
offering options. Heroics hide problems; options surface them.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- Treat every complaint as the first line of a plan I own, and try the cheapest change first.
- When something goes wrong, bring facts, options with costs, a recommendation and a process fix.
- Fix or visibly board up every broken window, because one unrepaired mess changes the norm.
- Start change with a small working piece, and say openly where it is heading.
- Measure slow decline against a fixed baseline or budget, never only against last week.
- Make quality a written requirement agreed with users, with a floor that is never traded.
- Manage knowledge like a portfolio: invest weekly, diversify, take some risk, rebalance quarterly.
- Plan communication for its audience, lead with the point, and always reply.

## Glossary

- **Agency**: the belief, and the habit, that my actions can change my situation.
- **Broken window**: a visible, unrepaired mess in code or operations that signals that mess is acceptable.
- **Boarding up**: making a known problem visible, contained and owned until it can be fixed.
- **Software entropy (rot)**: the gradual decline of a codebase's quality through accumulated shortcuts.
- **Technical debt**: the future cost of shortcuts taken now.
- **Catalyst (stone soup)**: someone who starts a change with a small working piece that others then join.
- **Boiled frog**: a decline too gradual for any single step to raise an alarm.
- **Baseline**: a fixed reference measurement that later measurements are compared with.
- **p95 latency**: the time under which 95 percent of requests complete.
- **Good-enough software**: software that meets a quality bar agreed with its users.
- **SLO (service-level objective)**: a measured, agreed target for a service, such as an availability percentage.
- **Error budget**: the amount of failure an SLO allows, which a team can spend on change.
- **Knowledge portfolio**: the whole of what I know, managed like a set of investments.
- **Blameless postmortem**: an incident review that focuses on causes and fixes, not on a culprit.
- **Ratchet**: a measurement that is allowed to improve but never to get worse.

## Exercises and self-check

These are my own exercises. The book has short exercises and challenges at
the end of several of these topics; do those too, in writing, before
reading its possible answers.

**Warm-up (by hand).**

1. Write down three things about my current work I would like to be
   different. Split each into "mine" and "not mine".
2. Take the last thing that went wrong in a project of mine. Rewrite my
   explanation of it as facts, options and a process fix.
3. List five broken windows in a codebase I know, and say how I would board
   up each one in under ten minutes.

**Build (code and test).**

4. Turn the census script into a real command that walks a directory, adds
   two more signals of my own choosing, and exits with a non-zero code if
   the count rose compared with a number stored in a file.
5. Extend the baseline script so the alarm compares against a budget (a
   fixed number of milliseconds) as well as the first week, and test it on
   a flat series, a slow drift and a single spike.
6. Write the portfolio script against my real study log for the last
   month.

**Stretch (change the problem).**

7. The baseline in exercise 5 is the first week. What goes wrong if the
   first week was itself unusually slow or fast? Propose a better baseline
   and justify it.
8. Write the same incident update for three audiences: engineers, a
   product manager, and customers. What changes and why?

**Self-check.**

<details>

Options with their costs and risks, my recommendation, and the process

<details>

Constraints such as data integrity, security and legal compliance. They are

Invest regularly, diversify, manage risk, buy low and sell high, and review
