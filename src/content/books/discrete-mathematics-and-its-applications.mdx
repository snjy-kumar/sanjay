## Why this book

The <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a> lists Rosen as
the optional depth for Phase 2, discrete mathematics. <a href={withBase('/books/mathematics-for-computer-science/')}>MIT's text</a> is the primary book; Rosen is the
encyclopaedia beside it, with many worked examples and exercises, and with two topics the phase needs
that the MIT text leaves out: Boolean algebra and logic gates, and matrices. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-2')}>Phase 2 strategy</a> uses it
that way.

The chapter list follows the publisher's contents for the eighth edition. Every chapter now has its
own page, written as a full chapter: each section of the book's chapter taught from first principles,
with definitions in plain words, proofs walked line by line, worked problems, TypeScript I ran in Node,
diagrams, and the nine levels from beginner to strategy. I describe everything in my own words; no
text, figures or exercises are copied from the book.

## How I would learn it

I do not read Rosen front to back on the first pass. I read the matching Rosen chapter when the MIT
chapter is hard, to see the same idea explained more slowly, and I take its exercises when I need more
practice than the proof notebook gives me. Chapters 2 (matrices) and 12 (Boolean algebra) I read in
full, because the phase needs them and the MIT text does not have them. On the second pass I work each
chapter page here in order, write its code, and do its self-check without looking.

For every chapter I keep one TypeScript file with the chapter's algorithms and a test for every edge

book's odd-numbered answers, which is the main reason to use its exercises: I can check my work without
a teacher.

## What I learn from it

- **1. The Foundations: Logic and Proofs.** Propositions, connectives and truth tables; translating
  English; equivalences and De Morgan; predicates and quantifiers, nested quantifiers and their
  negation; rules of inference; direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, existence and
  uniqueness proofs, and how to choose one.
- **2. Basic Structures.** Sets and set builder notation, power sets, Cartesian products; set
  operations and identities; functions, one-to-one, onto, inverses, floor and ceiling; sequences and
  summation formulas; countable and uncountable sets and the diagonal argument; matrices and their
  arithmetic, including Boolean matrix products.
- **3. Algorithms.** What makes a procedure an algorithm; searching, sorting, greedy algorithms and the
  halting problem; big-O, big-Omega and big-Theta with witnesses; time complexity of the standard
  algorithms; tractable and intractable problems, P and NP.
- **4. Number Theory and Cryptography.** Divisibility and the division algorithm; modular
  arithmetic; base expansions and integer algorithms; fast modular exponentiation; primes, the
  fundamental theorem of arithmetic, gcd and Euclid; Bézout and inverses; the Chinese remainder theorem;
  Fermat's little theorem; hashing, pseudorandom numbers, check digits; classical ciphers and RSA.
- **5. Induction and Recursion.** Ordinary and strong induction, well-ordering; recursive definitions
  and structural induction; recursive algorithms and their correctness; merge sort; preconditions,
  postconditions, loop invariants and partial correctness.
- **6. Counting.** Sum, product, subtraction and division rules; tree diagrams; pigeonhole and its
  generalisation; permutations, combinations, combinatorial proofs; the binomial theorem, Pascal's
  identity, Vandermonde; repetition and stars and bars; distributing objects into boxes; generating
  permutations and combinations in order.
- **7. Discrete Probability.** Laplace's definition; probability distributions; conditional
  probability and independence; Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution; random variables;
  the birthday problem; probabilistic algorithms; Bayes' theorem and spam filtering; expectation,
  linearity, geometric distribution, variance and Chebyshev.
- **8. Advanced Counting Techniques.** Modelling with recurrences; dynamic programming; solving linear
  homogeneous and nonhomogeneous recurrences; divide-and-conquer recurrences and the master theorem;
  generating functions; inclusion-exclusion, onto functions and derangements.
- **9. Relations.** Properties of relations and their combinations; n-ary relations and relational
  algebra; matrices and digraphs for relations; reflexive, symmetric and transitive closures, Warshall's
  algorithm; equivalence classes and partitions; partial orders, Hasse diagrams, lattices and
  topological sorting.
- **10. Graphs.** Graph models; degree and the handshake theorem; special graphs and bipartite
  graphs; adjacency representations and isomorphism; paths, connectivity and counting walks; Euler and
  Hamilton paths; Dijkstra's algorithm and the travelling salesperson problem; planarity and Euler's
  formula; colouring and the four colour theorem.
- **11. Trees.** Properties of trees; binary search trees, decision trees, prefix codes and Huffman
  coding, game trees; traversals and expression notation; spanning trees by depth-first and
  breadth-first search, backtracking; minimum spanning trees by Prim and Kruskal.
- **12. Boolean Algebra.** Boolean functions and identities, duality; sum-of-products form and
  functional completeness; logic gates and adders; minimisation by Karnaugh maps and Quine-McCluskey.
- **13. Modeling Computation.** Phrase-structure grammars and the Chomsky types, derivation trees, BNF;
  finite-state machines with output; finite-state automata, nondeterminism and the subset construction;
  Kleene's theorem and regular expressions; limits of finite machines; Turing machines and
  computability.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Bigger book, better book." Rosen is wide; MIT's text is deeper on proofs and probability. Use each
  for what it is good at.
- "Reading the worked examples is practising." Doing the exercises is practising.
- "Boolean algebra is only for hardware people." Every mask, flag and condition in code is Boolean
  algebra.
- "Greedy works because it worked on the example." It works only with an assumption about the input.
- "Discrete maths is a prerequisite I get through once." It is the vocabulary of every later phase:
  complexity, databases, compilers, cryptography and distributed systems all speak it.
