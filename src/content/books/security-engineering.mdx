## Why this book

This is the security half of the primary reading for phase 11 of the
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>,
software engineering and security, and it comes back in the
<a href={withBase('/plans/software-ai-developer/')}>software AI developer</a> plan and the
<a href={withBase('/plans/underrated-fundamentals/')}>underrated fundamentals</a>. Most
security material I meet as a web developer is a list of bugs. This book
treats security as a property of a whole system: people, incentives,
protocols, hardware and code together. That is the view the phase 11 gate
asks for when it says I should explain the security tradeoffs of my
capstone without hand-waving.

The author keeps chapters of the third edition free on his site. The
printed Wiley edition and the author's online list name three chapters
slightly differently (13, 18 and 25); I use the printed titles and note
the online ones where they differ.

## How I would learn it

I would not read 29 chapters in three weeks. Phase 11 needs part I whole,
and chapters 21, 26, 27 and 28 from the later parts. Part II is a tour of
real systems (banks, alarms, nuclear command, phones, printing) and I read
it as case studies, one a week, long after the phase, because each one
shows the same few principles failing in a new place.

For every chapter I write down one attack against something I have built
or use every day, and one change that would stop it. The threat model I
write for my capstone is assembled from those notes. The test of whether a
chapter landed is whether it changed something in my own code or design.

## Part I

### 1. What Is Security Engineering?

**Covers:** The frame for the whole book: policy, mechanism, assurance and incentive, worked through example settings such as a bank, a military base, a hospital and a home, and the vocabulary of the field.

<Figure art={`
          +-------------+
          |   POLICY    |   what must be true
          +------+------+
                 |
   +-------------+-------------+
   |                           |
+--+-----------+     +---------+----+
|  MECHANISM   |     |  ASSURANCE   |
| how it is    |     | why I trust  |
| enforced     |     | the mechanism|
+--------------+     +--------------+
                 |
          +------+------+
          |  INCENTIVE  |   who gains, who pays
          +-------------+
`} caption="Four questions to ask of any security design. A gap in any one of them is where attacks land." />

<Levels label="Chapter 1, What Is Security Engineering?">
<Level name="Beginner">

Security engineering is building systems that stay dependable when someone is trying to make them fail. Malice is what sets it apart from ordinary reliability work.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Every design answers four questions: what the policy is, what mechanism enforces it, why I believe the mechanism works, and who has the incentive to keep it working. A password field is a mechanism; "only the account owner reads these messages" is the policy.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

The vocabulary matters: a vulnerability is a property of the system, a threat is someone able and willing to use it, and a risk weighs both. Security, safety, privacy and reliability overlap but are not the same goal.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A system with no users has no security problem and no value. Once value appears, so does an opponent; "nobody would bother" is a statement about value, and it expires.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A safety mechanism and a security mechanism can fight: a fire door that must open from inside is also an exit for a thief. Two good policies meeting at one door is where judgement is needed.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In a real service the policy is rarely written down. The first useful act is to write it: who may read, write and delete which data, and what must happen when that is violated.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask "what would make this fail on purpose?" before "does it work?". The habit is to treat every feature as also an attack surface.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Policy belongs to the product, mechanism to the code, assurance to the tests and reviews, and incentive to the organisation. A security review that only looks at code covers one of four.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write the four-box diagram for my capstone before reading further, then revise it at the end of part I. Done when my capstone has a written policy and each policy line names its mechanism and the test that assures it.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 2. Who Is the Opponent?

**Covers:** The attackers a system actually faces: spies and states, cybercriminals, geeks and researchers, and the bully, the abuser and the insider, with what each wants and can do.

<Levels label="Chapter 2, Who Is the Opponent?">
<Level name="Beginner">

Security is defined against an opponent. A lock good against a passer-by fails against a locksmith; the same is true of software.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Opponents differ in goal, skill, money, patience and access. A criminal wants scale and money, a state wants persistence and secrecy, an abusive partner already has the phone's passcode.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Attack tooling industrialises: what a skilled attacker does once becomes a kit others buy. So the bar for "capable opponent" rises over the life of a system.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

The insider is the boundary case of "attacker": legitimate access, no exploit needed. Most access-control models assume the attacker is outside, and fail here.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

An insider who is also coerced by an outside party combines the insider's access with the outsider's goals. Logging what admins do covers both at once.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

For a web app I name three concrete opponents: a credential-stuffing bot, a curious user poking at IDs, and a careless or hostile admin. Each gets its own controls and its own alerts.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Never design against "a hacker". Design against a named opponent with a budget. Vague opponents produce vague defences.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

The opponent list feeds the threat model, the threat model feeds the controls, and the controls feed the monitoring. If an opponent has no row in monitoring, I will not see them.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write an opponent table (goal, skill, access, budget) for my capstone and an everyday app I use. Done when every row of my capstone's table has at least one control and one way I would notice an attack.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 3. Psychology and Usability

**Covers:** How people actually behave around security: deception and phishing, the limits of what users can be asked to do, passwords and their real-world failure, and CAPTCHAs.

<Compare left="Blaming the user" right="Designing for the user" rows={[["Users pick weak passwords", "Allow long passphrases and check against breached lists"], ["Users click phishing links", "Use phishing-resistant login such as passkeys"], ["Users ignore warnings", "Warn rarely, so each warning means something"]]} caption="The same failures, read two ways. Only the right-hand column changes outcomes." />

<Levels label="Chapter 3, Psychology and Usability">
<Level name="Beginner">

People are part of the system. A control that people cannot use correctly does not exist in practice.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Phishing works because it targets how people decide under time pressure, not a bug in code. Passwords fail because people are asked to remember many strong secrets, which human memory cannot do.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Security warnings habituate: shown often, they are clicked through without reading. Good designs make the safe path the default and the unsafe path deliberate.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Password rules at the extremes backfire: forced monthly rotation produces `Summer2026!` then `Autumn2026!`. A rule that ignores memory makes passwords weaker, not stronger.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A strong password stored in a browser on a shared family laptop meets a usability shortcut. The password is strong, the device policy is not; both edges at once undo each other.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In a login flow I rate-limit, check new passwords against breached-password lists, support a password manager's autofill, and offer passkeys. Each of those reduces how much I depend on the user being perfect.

```ts
// Hash the password before checking a breached-password API by prefix,
// so the full hash never leaves the server (k-anonymity).
import { createHash } from 'node:crypto';
const sha1 = createHash('sha1').update(password).digest('hex').toUpperCase();
const prefix = sha1.slice(0, 5); // only this is sent
```

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

When users keep doing the wrong thing, the design is wrong. The habit is to treat human error as a requirement, not an excuse.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Usability decisions sit in the auth service, the recovery flow and the support desk. Account recovery is usually the weakest of the three, because it is designed for people who forgot everything.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I walk my capstone's sign-up, login and recovery flows as a tired user on a phone and note every moment I could be tricked. Done when recovery is at least as hard to abuse as login, and I can show why.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 4. Protocols

**Covers:** Security protocols as rules of conversation: passwords and challenge–response, attacks that replay, reflect or sit in the middle, message integrity, changing environments, chosen-protocol attacks, managing keys, and design assurance.

<Flow steps={["Client: hello", "Server: random challenge", "Client: MAC(key, challenge)", "Server: verifies and accepts"]} caption="Challenge–response. The fresh challenge is what stops an old answer being replayed." />

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Protocols">
<Level name="Beginner">

A protocol is a fixed exchange of messages that lets two parties establish something, such as who the other is. Most protocol failures come from a message that can be reused somewhere it was not meant for.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Freshness stops replay: a nonce or timestamp makes each answer valid once. Binding stops reflection: the answer must say who it is for and in which direction.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

A man-in-the-middle relays between two honest parties and is invisible unless the protocol binds identities into what is signed or MACed. That is why TLS signs over the handshake transcript.

```ts
import { createHmac, timingSafeEqual } from 'node:crypto';
function verify(key: Buffer, challenge: Buffer, answer: Buffer) {
  const expected = createHmac('sha256', key).update(challenge).digest();
  return answer.length === expected.length && timingSafeEqual(answer, expected);
}
```

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A nonce that repeats, because the random generator was seeded badly or a counter reset after a restart, quietly turns challenge–response back into replayable passwords.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A protocol proven correct in one environment meets a new one: a car key fob designed for a short radio range meets a relay attacker who extends the range. The protocol did not change; its assumption did.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Webhooks are protocols. I sign the body with a timestamp, reject old timestamps, compare in constant time, and record seen IDs to stop replays inside the window.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Write down every assumption a protocol relies on (fresh randomness, a clock, a short range, a trusted channel). Attacks live in the assumptions, not the arithmetic.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Protocols sit at every trust boundary: browser to server, service to service, webhook to receiver. Each boundary needs its own answer to replay, reflection and the middle.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write the message sequence of every authentication exchange in my capstone and attack each one on paper for replay, reflection and relay. Done when each attack is stopped by a named field or check I can point to in code.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 5. Cryptography

**Covers:** Cryptography for engineers: historical ciphers, the random oracle model, symmetric primitives and modes of operation, hash functions, asymmetric primitives and signatures, and how crypto fails in practice.

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Cryptography">
<Level name="Beginner">

Cryptography gives three things: confidentiality (encryption), integrity (MACs and signatures) and authenticity (keys tied to identities). It does not give policy or good key handling.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Symmetric crypto shares one key; asymmetric uses a key pair. Real systems use asymmetric crypto to agree a key and symmetric crypto for the data. Hashes are not encryption, and password hashing needs a slow, salted function.

```ts
import { scryptSync, randomBytes } from 'node:crypto';
const salt = randomBytes(16);
const hash = scryptSync(password, salt, 64); // store salt and hash
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

A block cipher alone is not enough: the mode decides what leaks. ECB shows patterns, CBC without a MAC allows padding-oracle attacks, and authenticated encryption such as AES-GCM gives confidentiality and integrity together.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Reusing a nonce with GCM is a single boundary that destroys both confidentiality and integrity for the messages involved. "Never reuse" is a rule with no tolerance.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A correct algorithm with a timing leak in comparison, or an error message that reveals which check failed, combines a correct primitive with an observable side effect. The maths is fine; the system is broken.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

I use a well-reviewed library's high-level API, never my own construction, keep keys out of source and logs, and plan rotation from day one. Most crypto failures in production are key management, not cipher breaks.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Do not invent crypto, and do not trust crypto I cannot name. The question is always "what exactly does this primitive promise, and is that what I need?"

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Crypto lives in the TLS layer, the secrets store, the password store, token signing and data-at-rest encryption. Each has a different key, owner and rotation story.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I list every place my capstone uses crypto, with the primitive, the library call, where the key lives and how it rotates. Done when every row is filled and none says "custom".

</Level>
</Levels>

### 6. Access Control

**Covers:** Who may do what: operating-system access control and its groups and roles, access control lists and capabilities, hardware protection, and what goes wrong when the controls are bypassed.

<Compare left="Access control list" right="Capability" rows={[["Stored with the object", "Held by the subject"], ["Easy to ask: who can read this file?", "Easy to ask: what can this process do?"], ["Revoke by editing the list", "Revoke is hard once handed out"]]} caption="The two ways to store the access matrix, and what each makes easy." />

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Access Control">
<Level name="Beginner">

Access control decides whether a subject may perform an action on an object. Authentication says who you are; authorisation says what you may do.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Groups and roles scale permissions: give the role, not the person. In a web app, role-based access control is the usual first model.

```ts
type Role = 'viewer' | 'editor' | 'admin';
const can: Record<Role, Set<string>> = {
  viewer: new Set(['read']),
  editor: new Set(['read', 'write']),
  admin: new Set(['read', 'write', 'delete']),
};
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Roles are not enough when ownership matters: an editor may edit only their own team's documents. Every check needs subject, action and the specific object, which is where most broken access control lives.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

The object that does not exist: returning "forbidden" for some IDs and "not found" for others tells an attacker which IDs are real. Answer the same way for both.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A permission revoked while a long-lived token still carries the old role combines a boundary in time with a cached decision. Short token life or a revocation check closes it.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

I put authorisation in one place that every handler calls, deny by default, and write a test per role per endpoint. A missing check on one route is the most common real bug.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Least privilege: every subject gets the smallest set of rights that lets it do its job, and every new right needs a reason.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Access control appears in the database (row-level policies), the API (middleware), the OS (service accounts) and the cloud (IAM). A gap at any layer can be the one that is used.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I build the access matrix for my capstone (roles by actions by object types) and generate tests from it. Done when every cell has a test and a change to a role fails a test somewhere.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 7. Distributed Systems

**Covers:** Security where many machines meet: concurrency and its races, fault tolerance and failure recovery, naming, and how centralised and distributed designs trade off.

<Levels label="Chapter 7, Distributed Systems">
<Level name="Beginner">

Once a system spans machines, there is no single moment of truth. Security checks that assume one consistent state can be raced.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Time-of-check to time-of-use: a balance is checked, then debited, and two requests in between both pass the check. The fix is to make check and use one atomic step.

```sql
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 100
WHERE id = $1 AND balance >= 100;  -- check and use together
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Fault tolerance and security pull in opposite directions: redundancy adds copies to protect, and recovery paths bypass normal checks. Byzantine faults are where the two meet: a faulty node may also be a lying one.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A replica that lags by one write may still serve a revoked permission. Reads used for security decisions should come from the primary or carry a version.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A retry after a timeout combined with a non-idempotent payment executes twice. A failure recovery path plus a side effect is a classic double spend.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

I give every state-changing request an idempotency key, do security checks inside the transaction that changes state, and make names (user IDs, hostnames) unambiguous across services.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Assume any two requests can interleave in the worst order. If the result is wrong in that order, it will happen in production.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

This chapter is the bridge to phase 10. Centralised designs are easier to secure and harder to scale; distributed ones the reverse. The choice is a security decision, not only a performance one.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write a test that fires two concurrent withdrawals at my capstone and asserts the balance never goes negative. Done when that test passes a thousand runs in a row.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 8. Economics

**Covers:** Why security fails for economic reasons: classical and information economics, game theory, auction theory, and the economics of security and dependability, where incentives and externalities decide outcomes.

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Economics">
<Level name="Beginner">

Security often fails not because nobody knew how to fix it, but because the people who could fix it are not the ones who suffer when it breaks.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Externalities: a vendor ships insecure defaults, users pay the cost. Asymmetric information: buyers cannot tell secure products from insecure ones, so they pay for features instead.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Game theory frames attack and defence as choices with payoffs. A defender must cover every hole; an attacker needs one. That asymmetry shapes which defences are worth paying for.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

When a defence costs nothing to the person deciding, it gets switched on; when it costs them anything, it gets argued about. Defaults are where economics meets code.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A bug bounty pays researchers to report, while a grey market pays more to keep the bug secret. Two incentive schemes meet on one person's decision.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In a team, the cost of a security fix lands on the sprint and the benefit on a future incident. I make the benefit visible: a written risk with a likely cost, next to the estimate.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

When something stays insecure, ask who pays for the failure and who pays for the fix. If they are different people, expect the failure.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Incentives are a design input: who owns the data, who carries liability, who sees the logs. Aligning them is as real a control as a firewall.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

For three known weaknesses in tools I use, I write who pays for the failure and who would pay for the fix. Done when my capstone's threat model lists, for each risk, who bears it.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Part II

### 9. Multilevel Security

**Covers:** Security policies built on classification levels: the Bell–LaPadula model, the Biba integrity model, real multilevel systems, and the practical problems such as covert channels and polyinstantiation.

<Figure art={`
  TOP SECRET   ^ read down OK      | write up OK
  SECRET       |                   |
  CONFIDENTIAL |  no read up       v  no write down
  PUBLIC
`} caption="Bell–LaPadula in one picture: information may flow up, never down." />

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Multilevel Security">
<Level name="Beginner">

Multilevel security labels data and people with levels and allows information to flow only upward. It is the oldest formal security model.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Bell–LaPadula protects confidentiality: no reading above your level, no writing below it. Biba is its mirror for integrity: no reading below, no writing above, so dirty data cannot contaminate trusted data.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Both are information-flow policies, not permission lists. They constrain where data can go after it is read, which ordinary role checks never do.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A user who must legitimately move a document down (declassification) breaks the model by design. Every real system needs a trusted, audited exception path.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Covert channels: a high process signals to a low one through timing or disk usage, combining two allowed behaviours into a forbidden flow.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

The web version is taint tracking: data from a request is low integrity until validated. I mark untrusted input in types so it cannot reach a query unvalidated.

```ts
type Untrusted<T> = T & { readonly __untrusted: unique symbol };
declare function validateEmail(s: Untrusted<string>): string; // only way out
```

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Think in flows, not checks. Ask where a value came from and where it may go, not only who is calling.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Information flow shows up in multi-tenant systems: tenant A's data must never flow into tenant B's response, cache or log.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I trace one piece of user input in my capstone from request to storage to every output. Done when every path passes through a validation or encoding step I can name.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 10. Boundaries

**Covers:** Keeping compartments apart sideways rather than by level: compartmentation and the lattice model, privacy for tiger teams, health records as a case study, and the limits of lateral boundaries.

<Levels label="Chapter 10, Boundaries">
<Level name="Beginner">

Not all data sits on a ladder of levels. Some must be kept in separate compartments: one team's project from another's, one patient's record from another's.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Need-to-know: access depends on which compartment a person belongs to as well as their level. The lattice model combines levels and compartments into one ordering.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Health records show the tension: clinicians need fast access in emergencies, yet the records are among the most sensitive data there is. Strict boundaries cost lives; loose ones cost privacy.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

The emergency override ("break glass") is the boundary case. It must exist, and every use must be logged and reviewed.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A person who belongs to two compartments becomes a bridge between them. Combined with a shared export tool, they can join data neither compartment would release.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In a multi-tenant SaaS, the tenant ID is a compartment. I enforce it in the data layer, not only in handlers, so a forgotten filter cannot leak across tenants.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Boundaries need owners. If nobody is responsible for the line between two compartments, it erodes one convenience feature at a time.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Compartments map to databases, schemas, row policies, encryption keys and network segments. The stronger the separation, the higher the cost of every legitimate join.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write a test that logs in as tenant A and tries every endpoint with tenant B's IDs. Done when the test exists in CI and fails if a tenant filter is removed anywhere.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 11. Inference Control

**Covers:** Protecting individuals inside data that is released in aggregate: the early history of the problem, differential privacy, and the gap between what the theory promises and what released data really protects.

<Levels label="Chapter 11, Inference Control">
<Level name="Beginner">

Removing names does not make data anonymous. Enough other facts (postcode, birth date, gender) can point to one person.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Queries can leak: the average salary of a department, then the average without one person, reveals that person's salary. Inference control limits what aggregates can reveal.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Differential privacy adds calibrated noise so the output barely changes whether or not any one person is in the data. The privacy budget limits how many queries can be answered before the guarantee wears out.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Small groups: a count of one is a disclosure. Suppressing small cells is the crude but common defence.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Two separately safe releases joined together re-identify people. Each release passed review alone; the combination did not.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Analytics events often carry user IDs, IPs and timestamps that make "anonymous" dashboards personal. I strip or coarsen them at collection, not at reporting.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask what an attacker already knows. Anonymity is relative to outside knowledge, and outside knowledge only grows.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Inference control belongs at the analytics boundary: the export job, the reporting API, the data warehouse. Production databases should not be the analytics source.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I take one analytics event in my capstone and list every field that could identify a person. Done when each field is removed, coarsened, or justified in writing.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 12. Banking and Bookkeeping

**Covers:** The origins of bookkeeping and double-entry controls, how bank computer systems work, wholesale payment systems, automatic teller machines, credit cards, EMV payment cards, online banking, and the problems of non-bank payments.

<Levels label="Chapter 12, Banking and Bookkeeping">
<Level name="Beginner">

Banking security started as bookkeeping: double entry makes every movement appear twice, so errors and theft show up as imbalances.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Separation of duties means no single person can both create and approve a payment. Dual control and audit trails turn an insider's fraud into something that needs collusion.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Card systems layer cryptography on top: chip cards authenticate the card, the issuer authorises the transaction. Fraud moves to wherever the layers are weakest, such as card-not-present payments online.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Rounding and currency conversion at the smallest unit are boundaries where money can be created or lost. I store money as integers in minor units, never floats.

```ts
const totalCents = items.reduce((s, i) => s + i.priceCents * i.qty, 0);
```

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A refund issued on a reversed payment combines two legitimate flows into a loss. State machines for payments must forbid such paths explicitly.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

I keep an append-only ledger, reconcile it against the payment provider daily, and alert on any mismatch. Reconciliation catches what code review missed.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Design so that mistakes and fraud leave evidence. A system that cannot show where money went cannot prove it was not stolen.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Ledgers, payment providers, reconciliation jobs and approval workflows form one control system. Each part assumes the others exist.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I model my capstone's money or quota flows as a double-entry ledger and write a reconciliation check. Done when a deliberately injected bad entry is caught by the check.

</Level>

### 13. Locks and Alarms

**Covers:** Physical protection: threats and barriers, the economics and mechanics of locks, and alarms with their sensors, communications and the ways they are defeated. The author's online list names this chapter Physical Protection.

<Levels label="Chapter 13, Locks and Alarms">
<Level name="Beginner">

Physical security buys time. A lock is rated by how long it resists, and an alarm by how fast someone responds.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Deter, detect, delay, respond: a barrier only matters if detection and response arrive before the delay runs out.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Alarms can be defeated by attacking the communication path or by causing so many false alarms that people stop responding. The second is psychology, not engineering.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A strong lock on a weak door frame: security is set by the weakest part of the path, not the most impressive one.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A power cut that disables the alarm and unlocks electronic doors for fire safety combines a failure with a safety rule into an open door.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

The software equivalent is rate limiting plus alerting: delay the attacker, notice them, and have someone on call. A limit with no alert just slows an attack nobody sees.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Every defence is a delay. The real question is whether I notice and respond before the delay is used up.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Data centres, laptops and phones are physical. Disk encryption and remote wipe are the software answers to physical theft.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

For my capstone's login, I calculate how long a credential-stuffing attack takes under my rate limit and how long until I would be alerted. Done when the alert arrives well inside the delay.

### 14. Monitoring and Metering

**Covers:** Systems that measure and bill: prepayment tokens, taxi meters and tachographs, smart meters and smart grids, and the ways people attack the measurement itself.

<Levels label="Chapter 14, Monitoring and Metering">
<Level name="Beginner">

When a device measures usage for billing, the person being measured often has a reason to cheat. The meter is in the attacker's hands.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Prepayment tokens must not be copied or replayed; tachographs must record honestly even when the driver controls the vehicle. Both are protocols run on hostile ground.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Attacks often go around the cryptography: tampering with the sensor that feeds the secure device. Securing the record does not secure the measurement.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A meter that loses power or its clock: what it records in the gap decides whether that gap can be exploited.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A smart meter that both bills and can switch off supply combines a billing bug with a safety consequence. A remote off switch is an attack surface at grid scale.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Usage-based billing in software (API calls, seats, storage) is metering. I count on the server, never trust client-reported usage, and make counters idempotent.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask who controls the sensor. If it is the party who benefits from a wrong reading, the reading needs independent checks.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Metering sits between product, billing and abuse prevention. Quotas are both a pricing feature and a security control.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I list every number my capstone trusts from a client and move each to the server or justify it. Done when no billing or quota decision uses a client-supplied count.

### 15. Nuclear Command and Control

**Covers:** How nuclear weapons are protected from unauthorised use: the evolution of command and control, unconditionally secure authentication, shared control schemes, tamper resistance, treaty verification, and what goes wrong.

<Levels label="Chapter 15, Nuclear Command and Control">
<Level name="Beginner">

Here the policy is extreme: the weapon must always work when properly ordered and never work otherwise. Both failures are catastrophic.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Shared control (two-person rules) and secret sharing split authority so no single person can act alone.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Secret sharing schemes split a key into parts so that any k of n parts recover it and fewer reveal nothing. The same idea protects root keys in ordinary organisations.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

If too few holders are reachable, authorised use fails. The threshold k is a tradeoff between theft and availability.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A crisis that demands speed meets a two-person rule designed for calm. Procedures must be designed for the worst day, not the average one.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Production deploys and database deletions can take a two-person rule: a second approval for destructive actions, recorded in the audit log.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

For actions that cannot be undone, design for two independent failures before harm. One careful person is not a control.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Break-glass access, root key ceremonies and protected branches are the everyday versions of command and control.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I list the irreversible actions in my capstone and its CI. Done when each one requires a second check, human or automated, and the check is recorded.

### 16. Security Printing and Seals

**Covers:** Banknotes, documents and seals that show tampering: the history, the threat model, inks, printing and other techniques, packaging and seals, systemic vulnerabilities, and how to evaluate a seal.

<Levels label="Chapter 16, Security Printing and Seals">
<Level name="Beginner">

A seal does not stop opening; it shows that something was opened. Tamper evidence is detection, not prevention.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Security printing makes copies hard to produce and easy to check. The check must be one an ordinary person actually does.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Seals fail through the inspection process more often than through the seal: nobody looks, or they look at the wrong thing.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A seal that is hard to fake but easy to buy blank is no seal at all. Supply of the materials is part of the design.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A genuine seal moved onto a different container combines two valid parts into a fraud. The seal must be bound to what it protects.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Signed commits, signed releases and checksums are digital seals. They only help if CI verifies them and refuses on mismatch.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Evidence is only useful if someone checks it. Design the check first, then the seal.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

In a software supply chain, a lockfile's integrity hashes are seals bound to specific packages. That binding is what makes them work.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I make my capstone's CI install with the lockfile in a mode that fails on any hash mismatch. Done when tampering with one integrity line breaks the build.

### 17. Biometrics

**Covers:** Identifying people by their bodies: handwritten signatures, face recognition, fingerprints, iris codes, voice and other biometrics, and what goes wrong with each.

<Levels label="Chapter 17, Biometrics">
<Level name="Beginner">

A biometric measures something about a person's body or behaviour. It is convenient because it cannot be forgotten, and risky because it cannot be changed.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Every biometric trades false accepts against false rejects. Tightening one loosens the other; the threshold is a policy choice.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Matching is statistical, so error rates grow with database size: a rate that is fine for one-to-one checks becomes many false matches when searching millions.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

People whose fingerprints are worn, or whose faces the system was not trained on, hit high false-reject rates. The edge is a group of people, not a number.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A spoofed fingerprint presented to a sensor with no liveness check combines a copied trait with a weak sensor into a full bypass.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

On phones, biometrics unlock a key held in secure hardware; the fingerprint never leaves the device. That is the safe pattern: biometric as a local unlock, not as a password sent to a server.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Never treat a biometric as a secret. Treat it as an identifier that is usually present and sometimes copied.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Passkeys use device biometrics to unlock a private key, combining what the device holds with who is holding it.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I explain, in writing, why passkeys with device biometrics are safer than sending a face scan to a server. Done when the explanation names where the template lives and what an attacker would need.

</Level>

### 18. Tamper Resistance

**Covers:** Hardware that protects secrets inside it: history, hardware security modules, evaluation, smartcards and microcontrollers, the trusted execution environment, and what goes wrong. The author's online list names it Physical Tamper Resistance.

<Levels label="Chapter 18, Tamper Resistance">
<Level name="Beginner">

Tamper-resistant hardware keeps a key inside and does operations with it, so the key never appears in ordinary memory.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Hardware security modules guard bank and certificate-authority keys; smartcards and phone secure elements do the same at small scale. The application asks for a signature; it never sees the key.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

The API of the device is part of its security. A device that resists drilling but offers a sequence of legitimate calls that exports a key is broken through its front door.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Resistance is a matter of cost and time. A device rated against casual attack falls to a funded lab.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A trusted execution environment that is secure alone, combined with a side channel from the shared processor, leaks what it was meant to protect.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In the cloud, a key management service is my HSM. I sign and decrypt through its API and grant my service only the operations it needs.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask what the device's API lets a legitimate caller do in sequence. Attackers are often legitimate callers.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Root keys live in hardware; application keys are wrapped by them. The hierarchy limits what any one compromise exposes.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I move one secret in my capstone from an environment variable to a managed key service and narrow its permissions. Done when the application can use the key but cannot read it.

</Level>

### 19. Side Channels

**Covers:** Leaks through physical and timing effects: emission security, passive and active attacks, side channels in cryptography such as timing and power analysis, environmental side channels, and social side channels.

<Levels label="Chapter 19, Side Channels">
<Level name="Beginner">

A side channel leaks information through how a system behaves (time, power, sound, radio) rather than through what it outputs.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Timing is the side channel web developers meet: comparing a secret byte by byte returns faster when the first byte is wrong.

```ts
// Leaks: returns at the first mismatch.
const bad = (a: string, b: string) => a === b;
// Constant time over equal-length buffers.
import { timingSafeEqual } from 'node:crypto';
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Power and electromagnetic analysis read keys from devices by measuring what they draw or radiate. Cache-timing attacks do the same in software between processes that share hardware.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Length is a side channel: `timingSafeEqual` throws on different lengths, so I compare fixed-length digests rather than raw secrets.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Compression before encryption plus attacker-controlled input leaks secrets through the ciphertext's size. Two safe features combine into a leak.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Response times and error messages that differ for "user not found" and "wrong password" reveal which accounts exist. I make both paths do the same work and return the same answer.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Anything observable is output: time, size, error text, order. Ask what each observation tells someone watching.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Shared hardware in the cloud is a side-channel risk that providers manage; my job is to not add new channels in my own code.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I time my capstone's login for an existing and a missing user over a thousand tries each. Done when the two distributions are indistinguishable and the messages are identical.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 20. Advanced Cryptographic Engineering

**Covers:** Crypto put to work in whole systems: full-disk encryption, the Signal protocol, Tor, hardware security modules, enclaves and blockchains, and crypto ideas that did not work out.

<Levels label="Chapter 20, Advanced Cryptographic Engineering">
<Level name="Beginner">

Real systems combine primitives into protocols: disk encryption, end-to-end messaging, anonymity networks. The combination is where the engineering is.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

End-to-end messaging needs forward secrecy (a stolen key does not reveal old messages) and recovery after compromise (new keys heal the session). Ratcheting gives both.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Anonymity networks route traffic through several relays so no single relay knows both ends. Traffic analysis at the edges is their known limit.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Disk encryption protects a powered-off laptop, not a running one with the key in memory. The boundary is the power state.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Strong end-to-end encryption on a phone with a compromised operating system: the crypto holds and the screen leaks.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Most applications need TLS, encrypted storage and well-managed tokens, not novel cryptography. I spend the effort on key handling and on what metadata I keep.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask what the crypto protects against and at which moment. A guarantee true at rest may be false in use.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Metadata (who talked to whom, when) often matters as much as content. Logging decisions are privacy decisions.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write a one-page explanation of forward secrecy with a diagram and check it against the Signal protocol's public documentation. Done when I can say what my capstone's TLS setup does and does not give forward secrecy for.

</Level>

<Levels label="Chapter 21, Network Attack and Defence">

Network attacks exploit protocols that trust too much (spoofed addresses, open resolvers) or software that is reachable from the internet.

### 22. Phones

**Covers:** Telephone and mobile security: attacks on phone networks and billing, mobile network security across its generations, and platform security on smartphones.

<Levels label="Chapter 22, Phones">
<Level name="Beginner">

The phone network was designed for a small set of trusted operators. Many attacks still come from that legacy trust.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

SIM swapping moves a victim's number to an attacker's SIM through the carrier's support process, so SMS codes go to the attacker.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Smartphone platforms sandbox apps and gate permissions, which made phones more secure than desktops in many ways. The weak points move to app-store review and the permissions users grant.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A phone number reassigned to a new owner inherits the old owner's SMS-based logins.

SMS-based account recovery plus a SIM swap gives full takeover of an account protected by a strong password.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

I treat SMS as a weak second factor, prefer authenticator apps or passkeys, and never let SMS alone recover an account.

<Level name="Mindset">

Every channel I use for security inherits that channel's weakest process, including its support desk.

<Level name="System design">

Mobile apps talk to my API from devices I do not control. The API must assume the client is modified.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Security is a feature I add later." It is a property of the whole design; added later it costs more and covers less.
- "Strong crypto means a secure system." Most failures are in key handling, protocols, people and incentives.
- "Users are the weakest link." Usually the design is; people respond to what the design makes easy.
- "Part II is irrelevant to web work." The banking, metering and DRM chapters are the same problems a web app has.
- "A threat model is a compliance document." It is a design tool that should change what I build.

- My capstone has a threat model with real findings, each fixed or accepted with a reason: the phase 11 gate.

- I can attack an authentication protocol on paper for replay, reflection and relay.
