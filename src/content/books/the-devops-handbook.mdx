## Why this book

The <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a> lists this as
optional depth for Phase 0, and the <a href={withBase('/plans/software-ai-developer/')}>software AI developer</a>
plan cites it for its own orientation phase. It is a book about organisations: value streams,
deployment pipelines, telemetry, blameless postmortems. I am one person with a study repository, so
why read it now?

Because the three ideas underneath it (make work flow in small batches, get fast feedback, and learn
from every failure) are exactly how a 14-month study plan survives. I read it as a book about my own
loop first, and about teams later. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-0')}>Phase 0 strategy</a> uses it that way.

## How I would learn it

In Phase 0 I read Part I (chapters 1 to 4) in full, and Part III's chapters 9 to 11, because they
describe the build-and-test loop I set up that fortnight. Part IV I read in Phase 11 alongside CI and
incident response. Part II is mostly about organisations; I skim it and read it properly when I work in
a team that is changing how it ships. Part VI goes with Phase 11's security work.

- **Translate every chapter into my repo.** For each one I write: "the team version is X; my version
  is Y; I will do Z this week".
- **Skip the case studies on the first read.** They are evidence for the argument, not the argument. I
  come back to two or three that match what I am doing.
- **Measure the four numbers.** Even alone I can track how often I commit, how long a change takes
  from start to green, how often a change breaks the build, and how long it takes me to fix it.

I know I am done with the first pass when my study repo builds and tests with one command, CI runs that
same command, and my bug log has a rule for every entry.

## What I learn from it

Every chapter has its own page, linked in the contents below. One line per
chapter on what I come away with:

- [ ] **1. Agile, Continuous Delivery, and the Three Ways**: value streams, lead time versus process time, percent complete and accurate, and the Three Ways.
- [ ] **2. The First Way: The Principles of Flow**: visible work, limits on work in progress, small batches, fewer handoffs, constraints and waste.
- [ ] **3. The Second Way: The Principles of Feedback**: working safely in complex systems, seeing problems as they occur, swarming them, quality at the source, designing for downstream work.
- [ ] **4. The Third Way: The Principles of Continual Learning and Experimentation**: a learning culture, institutionalised improvement of daily work, turning local discoveries into global ones, resilience through practice, leaders who reinforce learning.
- [ ] **5. Selecting Which Value Stream to Start With**: greenfield versus brownfield, systems of record versus engagement, sympathetic teams, expanding from early wins.
- [ ] **6. Understanding the Work in Our Value Stream, Making it Visible, and Expanding it Across the Organization**: the value stream team, mapping the stream, a dedicated transformation team, shared goals and visible work.
- [ ] **7. How to Design Our Organization and Architecture with Conway's Law in Mind**: functional, matrix and market-oriented teams, generalists, funding services not projects, loosely coupled architecture, small teams.
- [ ] **8. How to Get Great Outcomes by Integrating Operations into the Daily Work of Development**: shared self-service operations, embedded or liaised operations engineers, operations in team rituals.
- [ ] **9. Create the Foundations of Our Deployment Pipeline**: environments on demand, a single source of truth in version control, rebuilding rather than repairing, a definition of done that includes running in a production-like environment.
- [ ] **10. Enable Fast and Reliable Automated Testing**: the deployment pipeline, the test pyramid, fast feedback, parallel tests, test-driven development, automating manual tests, performance and non-functional tests, the andon cord.
- [ ] **11. Enable and Practice Continuous Integration**: the cost of long branches, trunk-based development, small batches merged daily, gated commits.
- [ ] **12. Automate and Enable Low-Risk Releases**: automated, self-service deploys, decoupling deployment from release, blue-green, canary and feature-flag patterns, continuous delivery versus continuous deployment.
- [ ] **13. Architect for Low-Risk Releases**: loosely coupled, service-oriented architectures, the strangler fig pattern for evolving away from a monolith.
- [ ] **14. Create Telemetry to Enable Seeing and Solving Problems**: centralised telemetry, logging that helps production, telemetry for problem solving, metrics at every layer, information radiators.
- [ ] **15. Analyze Telemetry to Better Anticipate Problems and Achieve Goals**: means and standard deviations, anomaly detection, non-Gaussian data, alerting on what predicts failure.
- [ ] **16. Enable Feedback So Development and Operations Can Safely Deploy Code**: telemetry that makes deploys safer, developers on call, watching changes downstream, launch readiness reviews and hand-back mechanisms.
- [ ] **17. Integrate Hypothesis-Driven Development and A/B Testing into Our Daily Work**: experiments as part of features, A/B tests in releases and planning.
- [ ] **18. Create Review and Coordination Processes to Increase Quality of Our Current Work**: the dangers of change approval boards and excessive controls, peer review, pair programming, small changes reviewed well.
- [ ] **19. Enable and Inject Learning into Daily Work**: just culture, blameless post-incident reviews published widely, lowering incident tolerance to find weaker signals, game days and injected failure.
- [ ] **20. Convert Local Discoveries into Global Improvements**: chat rooms and bots that record knowledge, automated standardised processes in shared code, one source repository, non-functional requirements encoded once.
- [ ] **21. Reserve Time to Create Organizational Learning and Improvement**: improvement blitzes, teaching and learning time, internal conferences, sharing what was learned.
- [ ] **22. Information Security Is Everyone's Job Every Day**: security in the backlog and shared tools, security tests in the pipeline, the software supply chain, security telemetry.
- [ ] **23. Protecting the Deployment Pipeline**: standard, normal and urgent changes, separation of duties met by peer review, evidence for auditors produced by the pipeline itself.

The chapter titles are checked against the publisher's contents page. The
book's front matter, part introductions and conclusions, the closing call
to action, the afterword and the appendices are short connecting pieces,
not chapters; their ideas are taught on the chapter pages of the part
they belong to. Section headings inside each chapter
are not listed on the publisher's page, so the chapter pages use plain
descriptive headings in the book's order.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"DevOps is a team or a job title."** It is a set of practices
  across development, operations, security and the business.
- **"Speed comes at the cost of stability."** Small batches and fast feedback
  improve both.
- **"Blameless means nobody is accountable."** It means accountability for
  learning and fixing conditions.
- **"Automate everything first."** Find the constraint first; automate that.
