## Why this book

The <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a> lists this as
optional depth for Phase 0, and the <a href={withBase('/plans/software-ai-developer/')}>software AI developer</a>
plan cites it for its own orientation phase. It is a book about organisations: value streams,
deployment pipelines, telemetry, blameless postmortems. I am one person with a study repository, so
why read it now?

Because the three ideas underneath it (make work flow in small batches, get fast feedback, and learn
from every failure) are exactly how a 14-month study plan survives. I read it as a book about my own
loop first, and about teams later. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-0')}>Phase 0 strategy</a> uses it that way.

## How I would learn it

In Phase 0 I read Part I (chapters 1 to 4) in full, and Part III's chapters 9 to 11, because they
describe the build-and-test loop I set up that fortnight. Part IV I read in Phase 11 alongside CI and
incident response. Part II is mostly about organisations; I skim it and read it properly when I work in
a team that is changing how it ships. Part VI goes with Phase 11's security work.

- **Translate every chapter into my repo.** For each one I write: "the team version is X; my version
  is Y; I will do Z this week".
- **Skip the case studies on the first read.** They are evidence for the argument, not the argument. I
  come back to two or three that match what I am doing.
- **Measure the four numbers.** Even alone I can track how often I commit, how long a change takes
  from start to green, how often a change breaks the build, and how long it takes me to fix it.

I know I am done with the first pass when my study repo builds and tests with one command, CI runs that
same command, and my bug log has a rule for every entry.

## Part I, The Three Ways

### 1. Agile, Continuous Delivery, and the Three Ways

**Covers:** The value stream from idea to running software, lead time versus process time,
the cost of rework, and the three principles the rest of the book is organised
around.

<Levels label="Chapter 1, Agile, Continuous Delivery, and the Three Ways">

<Level name="Beginner">

A value stream is every step a change goes through until it helps someone. Lead
time is the clock time for the whole trip; process time is the time anyone is
actually working on it.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Most lead time is waiting: for review, for a test environment, for a release.
Shorten the wait before you speed up the work.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The share of work that arrives complete and accurate (no rework needed) is the
quality signal for each step. Low numbers upstream poison everything
downstream.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Short lead time with frequent rework is not flow; it is churn.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A step with zero waiting but heavy rework looks efficient on a dashboard and is
the real bottleneck.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Teams measure deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and time to
restore. They trade off far less than people expect.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Optimise the whole stream, not your local step.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Architecture that needs a coordinated release of five services lengthens lead
time no matter how good the pipeline is.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I map my own stream for one phase exercise: idea, code, test, review, commit,
note. I write down where it waits. Done when the map shows where my changes wait, with times.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 2. The First Way: The Principles of Flow

**Covers:** Making work visible, limiting work in progress, reducing batch size and
handoffs, finding the constraint, and removing waste.

<Levels label="Chapter 2, The First Way: The Principles of Flow">

<Level name="Beginner">

Finish things before starting new ones. Make the work visible on a board or a
list.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Small batches move faster and fail smaller. One feature per branch and one idea
per commit is batch size at the smallest scale.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The theory of constraints: improving any step other than the bottleneck does
not improve throughput. Find it, exploit it, subordinate the rest to it.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Limiting work in progress too hard leaves people blocked and idle; the limit is
tuned, not absolute.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

When the constraint moves (you fixed testing, now review is slowest), the old
optimisation can become waste.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Typical constraints in order: environment creation, deployment, test setup,
tightly coupled architecture.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Unfinished work is inventory, and inventory is risk.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Flow is a property of the architecture as much as the process.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

In Phase 0, my work-in-progress limit is one phase exercise and one note at a
time. Done when I have kept a work-in-progress limit of one for two weeks.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 3. The Second Way: The Principles of Feedback

**Covers:** Working safely in complex systems: seeing problems as they happen, swarming them
until they are solved, pushing quality closer to the source, and optimising for
the people downstream.

<Levels label="Chapter 3, The Second Way: The Principles of Feedback">

<Level name="Beginner">

Find out quickly when something is wrong. Fix it before doing more work on top
of it.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The andon cord: anyone can stop the line. In code, a red build stops new
merges until it is green.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

In complex systems, cause and effect are not obvious in advance, so safety comes
from detecting and recovering fast, not from predicting everything.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Feedback that is too noisy (flaky tests, alert storms) trains people to ignore
it.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Approval by someone far from the work feels like feedback but adds delay without
adding information.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Quality at the source: developers own tests and operability, not a separate QA
or ops gate at the end.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Problems are information. Swarm them while the context is fresh.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Telemetry, tests and staged rollouts are the system's feedback organs.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

In Phase 0 the feedback loop is `make check`: tests, warnings as errors, and
sanitizers, under a minute. Done when a red check stops me before I commit, every time.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 4. The Third Way: The Principles of Continual Learning and Experimentation

**Covers:** A learning culture, safety to report problems, turning local discoveries into
shared knowledge, injecting resilience patterns into daily work, and leaders
who reinforce learning.

<Levels label="Chapter 4, The Third Way: The Principles of Continual Learning and Experimentation">

<Level name="Beginner">

After something goes wrong, ask what happened and what you will change, not who
is to blame.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Write it down where others can find it. A fix that lives in one head is lost.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Deliberately inject failure (kill a process, corrupt a file) to learn how the
system reacts before a real incident teaches you.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Blameless does not mean consequence-free; it means the analysis looks at
conditions, not character.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A culture that celebrates firefighting rewards the people who caused the fire.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Postmortems with actions that are tracked to completion; game days.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Every failure is tuition already paid. Get the lesson.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Resilience is designed through repeated, controlled failure.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

My bug log (symptom, cause, fix, rule) and the monthly synthesis are the
one-person Third Way. Done when the bug log has a rule for each entry and I re-read it monthly.
</Level>

</Levels>

## Part II, Where to Start

### 5. Selecting Which Value Stream to Start With

**Covers:** Greenfield versus brownfield, systems of record versus systems of engagement,
and starting with the teams most likely to succeed.

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Selecting Which Value Stream to Start With">

<Level name="Beginner">

Start small, where change is welcome and success is visible.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Brownfield work often gains more than greenfield, because the pain is already
measurable.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Systems of record value correctness; systems of engagement value speed. They
need different release rhythms, sometimes in the same organisation.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A pilot chosen because it is easy proves nothing to sceptics.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A system that is both a record and a user-facing product needs both rhythms
split by a boundary.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Expand from innovators to early adopters to the majority, with evidence at each
step.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Pick the first battle you can win and learn from.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Splitting a system of record from its engagement layer is an architectural
decision with a process payoff.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

For me: start with the Phase 0 baseline programs, not the whole roadmap. Done when I can say why the baseline programs are the right place to start.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 6. Understanding the Work in Our Value Stream, Making it Visible, and Expanding it Across the Organization

**Covers:** Identifying everyone involved, value stream mapping, a dedicated transformation
team, shared goals, and keeping improvement planning short.

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Understanding the Work in Our Value Stream">

<Level name="Beginner">

Draw every step from request to release, with who does it and how long it
takes.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Add lead time, process time and the share done right first time to each box.
The map shows where to start.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Technical debt is paid down by reserving capacity (a fixed
share of each cycle), not by hoping for a quiet quarter.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A map drawn by managers alone misses the invisible work.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Making ops work visible on the same board as features exposes trade-offs that
were hidden.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Shared tooling and a common backlog for development and operations.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

You cannot improve what you cannot see.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The map often reveals that the bottleneck is an architectural coupling.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I map the stream for writing one note: study, code, draft, review, publish. Done when the note-writing map has times on each step.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 7. How to Design Our Organization and Architecture with Conway's Law in Mind

**Covers:** Conway's Law, functional versus market-oriented teams, loosely coupled
architecture, team size, and service boundaries.

<Levels label="Chapter 7, How to Design Our Organization and Architecture with Conway's Law in Mind">

<Level name="Beginner">

Systems end up shaped like the teams that build them.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Teams organised around a product can ship without waiting for others; teams
organised by function hand work over and wait.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Loose coupling with clear APIs lets small teams deploy independently. The
inverse Conway manoeuvre designs teams to get the architecture you want.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Splitting into services without splitting the teams just adds network calls.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

One team owning many tiny services has the overhead of microservices and none of
the independence.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Teams small enough to share context; APIs as contracts between them.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Org charts are architecture diagrams.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

This is the bridge to Phase 10 and to modular monoliths: boundaries first.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

For a solo learner, the lesson is module boundaries: each phase directory has
its own build and tests. Done when each phase directory builds and tests on its own.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 8. How to Get Great Outcomes by Integrating Operations into the Daily Work of Development

**Covers:** Shared services that make developers productive, embedding or liaising
operations engineers, and including operations in team rituals.

<Levels label="Chapter 8, How to Get Great Outcomes by Integrating Operations into the Daily Work of Development">

<Level name="Beginner">

The people who build it should understand how it runs.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Self-service platforms (create an environment, deploy, see logs) remove
tickets and waiting.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Operations concerns (logging, config, deploy) are requirements from day one, not
a hand-off at the end.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

"You build it, you run it" without tooling or on-call support is burnout.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A platform team that becomes a ticket queue recreates the wall it was meant to
remove.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Ops in stand-ups, retrospectives and design reviews.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Running it is part of building it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Platforms are products with users: the developers.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

In my phases, every program gets a README section on how to run, configure and
debug it. Done when every program has a run, configure and debug section in its README.
</Level>

</Levels>

## Part III, The First Way: The Technical Practices of Flow

### 9. Create the Foundations of Our Deployment Pipeline

**Covers:** On-demand environments, one repository of truth for everything, infrastructure
that is easier to rebuild than repair, and a definition of done that includes
running in a production-like environment.

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Create the Foundations of Our Deployment Pipeline">

<Level name="Beginner">

Everything needed to rebuild the project lives in version control.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Environments come from scripts or containers, so "works on my machine" becomes
"works in this image".

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Immutable infrastructure: replace, do not patch. Done means it runs in a
production-like environment, not on my laptop.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Secrets must not live in the repository even when everything else does.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A pinned base image that is never updated becomes a security liability.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Infrastructure as code, reviewed and tested like application code.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

If it cannot be rebuilt from the repo, it is a pet, not a system.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Reproducible environments are the base of every later pipeline stage.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Phase 0: a Dockerfile or devcontainer for the Linux environment, committed. Done when a fresh container builds the repo from a clone with one command.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 10. Enable Fast and Reliable Automated Testing

**Covers:** A deployment pipeline that runs on every change, the test pyramid, fast tests
first, running tests in parallel, test-driven development, and automating
manual tests.

<Levels label="Chapter 10, Enable Fast and Reliable Automated Testing">

<Level name="Beginner">

Tests run automatically on every change.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests, few end-to-end tests. Run the
fast ones first.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

When a slower test catches a bug, write a faster test that would have caught it.
Non-functional requirements (performance, security) get tests too.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Flaky tests destroy trust faster than missing tests.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A large-file test that is too slow for every commit still needs a scheduled run.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Red build stops the line; fixing it beats new work.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Tests are the safety net that makes speed safe.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The pyramid is a heuristic; its shape follows where the risk is.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Phase 1's gate tests (happy path, empty file, invalid input, large file) map
onto this pyramid. Done when the Phase 1 tests run fast-first and the slow large-file test is separate.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 11. Enable and Practice Continuous Integration

**Covers:** Small batches, trunk-based development, and the cost of long-lived branches.

<Levels label="Chapter 11, Enable and Practice Continuous Integration">

<Level name="Beginner">

Merge to the main line at least daily.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Long-lived branches drift and make merges painful; feature flags let unfinished
work sit on trunk safely.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Integration cost grows faster than branch age; that is why small batches win.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Trunk-based development without a fast test suite breaks trunk often.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A branch per published note (how this site works) is fine because each one is
one file and short-lived.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Gated merges, required checks and quick reverts.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Integrate early so the pain is small.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

CI is the heartbeat of the pipeline.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

My study repo works on main with small commits; experiments get a branch that
lives a day. Done when no branch in my repo lives longer than a day.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 12. Automate and Enable Low-Risk Releases

**Covers:** Automated, repeatable deployment; separating deployment from release;
blue-green, canary and dark launches; feature toggles.

<Levels label="Chapter 12, Automate and Enable Low-Risk Releases">

<Level name="Beginner">

Deploy with one command, the same way every time.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Deploying puts code on servers; releasing turns it on for users. Separate them.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Blue-green switches traffic between two environments; canaries expose a small
share of users first; dark launches run code without showing results.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Database migrations make rollback hard; design them to be backwards compatible.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Feature toggles that are never removed become a second, hidden configuration
language.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Automated rollback on health-check failure.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Releases should be boring.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Deployment patterns depend on statelessness and versioned interfaces.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Previewed in Phase 0, practised in the Phase 12 capstone. Done when I can explain deploy versus release with an example.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 13. Architect for Low-Risk Releases

**Covers:** Architectures that let teams deploy independently, service-oriented design,
and the strangler fig pattern for migrating safely.

<Levels label="Chapter 13, Architect for Low-Risk Releases">

<Level name="Beginner">

If changing one part forces redeploying everything, releases are risky.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Bounded contexts with APIs let parts change independently.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The strangler fig replaces an old system piece by piece behind a routing layer,
instead of a big-bang rewrite.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A shared database couples services even when the code is separate.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A strangler that never finishes leaves two systems to run forever.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Versioned APIs and contract tests between services.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Evolve, do not rewrite.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Architecture is the multiplier on every practice in Part III.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I revisit it in Phase 10 and Phase 12. Done when I can sketch a strangler migration for one of my own programs.
</Level>

</Levels>

## Part IV, The Second Way: The Technical Practices of Feedback

### 14. Create Telemetry to Enable Seeing and Solving Problems

**Covers:** Centralised telemetry, logging levels, metrics at every layer, and making
telemetry visible to everyone.

<Levels label="Chapter 14, Create Telemetry to Enable Seeing and Solving Problems">

<Level name="Beginner">

Log what the program did, with levels: debug, info, warning, error.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Metrics at the business, application and infrastructure layers answer
different questions.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Telemetry is a product: structured, searchable and visible on shared
dashboards.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Logging secrets or personal data turns telemetry into a liability.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Logging inside a hot loop changes the performance you are trying to observe.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Every incident should be visible in telemetry before a user reports it.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Instrument first, guess second.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Logs, metrics and traces each answer a different question.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

The Phase 1 CLI writes errors to stderr with context and exits with a meaningful
code. That is telemetry for a CLI. Done when every error path in the CLI prints context and returns a distinct exit code.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 15. Analyze Telemetry to Better Anticipate Problems and Achieve Goals

**Covers:** Means and standard deviations, anomaly detection, alerting on what predicts
failure, and why non-Gaussian data breaks simple alerts.

<Levels label="Chapter 15, Analyze Telemetry to Better Anticipate Problems and Achieve Goals">

<Level name="Beginner">

Alert when a number goes outside its normal range.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Use the mean and standard deviation to define normal, for data that is roughly
bell-shaped.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Many operational metrics are skewed; percentiles and smoothing work better than
means.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Weekly seasonality makes Monday look anomalous against Sunday.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

An average latency can improve while the slowest users get worse.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Alert on symptoms users feel, not on every cause.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Statistics before alarms.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

This connects directly to Phase 2's probability and the confidence interval in
its gate.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I come back to this after Phase 2, with my own simulation as practice. Done when I can explain why a mean alert fails on skewed latency data.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 16. Enable Feedback So Development and Operations Can Safely Deploy Code

**Covers:** Telemetry that makes deploys safer, developers sharing on-call, developers
following their work downstream, and launch-readiness reviews.

<Levels label="Chapter 16, Enable Feedback So Development and Operations Can Safely Deploy Code">

<Level name="Beginner">

Watch the system right after you deploy.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Put deploys on the metrics graphs so cause and effect line up.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Developers on call feel the cost of their own decisions, which changes them.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

New services may need a production-readiness review before ops takes them on.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A service handed back to its developers when it gets unstable keeps incentives
honest.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Launch guidance and readiness checklists.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Follow your work to where it hurts.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Ownership boundaries align with service boundaries.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

For me: re-run my own programs a week later from the README alone. Done when a stranger could run my programs from the README alone.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 17. Integrate Hypothesis-Driven Development and A/B Testing into Our Daily Work

**Covers:** Treating features as experiments, A/B testing in releases and in planning.

<Levels label="Chapter 17, Integrate Hypothesis-Driven Development and A/B Testing into Our Daily Work">

<Level name="Beginner">

State what you expect a change to do, then check.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

An A/B test shows two variants to random users and compares an outcome.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Many features do not improve the metric they target; experiments find out
cheaply.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Small samples produce confident-looking noise.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Running many tests at once and reporting the winner is a multiple-comparisons
trap.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Feature flags plus analytics plus a decision rule written before the test.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Ideas are hypotheses, not facts.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Experimentation needs consistent user assignment and event pipelines.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Phase 2's probability and Bayes are what make this chapter legible. Done when I can state a hypothesis and a decision rule before an experiment.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 18. Create Review and Coordination Processes to Increase Quality of Our Current Work

**Covers:** Peer review instead of distant approval, pair programming, small changes, and
the dangers of change-approval boards.

<Levels label="Chapter 18, Create Review and Coordination Processes to Increase Quality of Our Current Work">

<Level name="Beginner">

Have someone else read your change before it ships.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Small changes get real reviews; huge ones get rubber stamps.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

External approval boards add delay without lowering failure rates; peer review
close to the work does better.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Pairing replaces some review but not all.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Review that only checks style misses design.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"DevOps is a team or a job title."** It is a set of practices
  across development, operations, security and the business.
- **"Speed comes at the cost of stability."** Small batches and fast feedback
  improve both.
- **"Blameless means nobody is accountable."** It means accountability for
  learning and fixing conditions.
- **"Automate everything first."** Find the constraint first; automate that.
