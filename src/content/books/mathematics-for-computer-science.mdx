## Why this book

This is the primary book for Phase 2 of the <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>,
discrete mathematics, and the free one: MIT publishes it with the 6.042J course, lectures included. I
follow the Spring 2015 revision because that is the one the plan links. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-2')}>Phase 2 strategy</a> is built
around it.

I write software for a living, and most of my reasoning about it is "I tried it and it worked". This
book replaces that with proofs, counting and probability: the tools that say what must be true for
every input, not just the ones I tried. It does not cover Boolean circuits or linear algebra, which
the phase also asks for; for those I use
<a href={withBase('/books/discrete-mathematics-and-its-applications/')}>Rosen</a> and a short linear
algebra source.

## How I would learn it

Phase 2 is four weeks (weeks 6 to 9), and the book is over 900 pages, so I do not read all of it. I
read the chapters the phase's builds and gate need, with the matching 6.042J lecture first, and I
leave the rest for when a later phase reaches back for them.

For each chapter: watch the lecture, read the chapter once, then write two proofs in the proof
notebook without looking, and only then check them against the text. Anything computable, I compute:
a truth table generator, the modular toolkit, the simulations, a graph checker. A proof that I can
also test is a proof I understand twice.

## What I learn from it

Each line is one chapter; the chapter pages below teach every section in
full, from beginner to strategy. Titles follow the Spring 2015 contents.

**Part I, Proofs**

- 1. What is a Proof? Propositions, predicates, axioms, direct proof, contrapositive, iff, cases, contradiction, and writing proofs others can check.
- 2. The Well Ordering Principle: proving claims by a least counterexample, the proof template, and factoring into primes.
- 3. Logical Formulas: connectives, propositional logic in code, equivalence and validity, the algebra of propositions, SAT, and predicate formulas.
- 4. Mathematical Data Types: sets, sequences, functions, binary relations, and counting finite sets with bijections.
- 5. Induction: ordinary and strong induction, how they relate to well ordering, and state machines with invariants.
- 6. Recursive Data Types: recursive definitions, structural induction, matched brackets, recursive functions, and arithmetic expressions.
- 7. Infinite Sets: countable and uncountable sets, diagonal arguments, the halting problem, and the logic of sets.

**Part II, Structures**

- 8. Number Theory: divisibility, gcd and Euclid, primes, unique factorisation, modular arithmetic, inverses, Euler's theorem and RSA.
- 9. Directed Graphs and Partial Orders: degrees, walks and paths, adjacency matrices, DAGs and scheduling, partial and linear orders, equivalence relations.
- 10. Communication Networks: trees, routing, diameter, switch count, latency, congestion, 2-D arrays, butterfly and Beneš networks.
- 11. Simple Graphs: degrees, common graphs, isomorphism, bipartite matching, stable marriage, colouring, walks, connectivity, forests and trees.
- 12. Planar Graphs: planar embeddings, Euler's formula, edge bounds, K5 and K3,3, colouring planar graphs and polyhedra.

**Part III, Counting**

- 13. Sums and Asymptotics: annuities, sums of powers, integral bounds, products and Stirling, double sums, and asymptotic notation.
- 14. Cardinality Rules: bijections, product and sum rules, the division rule, subsets, repetitions, poker hands, pigeonhole, inclusion-exclusion and combinatorial proofs.
- 15. Generating Functions: series as counting tools, partial fractions, solving linear recurrences, and formal power series.

**Part IV, Probability**

- 16. Events and Probability Spaces: the four-step method, strange dice, the birthday principle, and set theory for probability.
- 17. Conditional Probability: Monty Hall, tree diagrams, the law of total probability, Simpson's paradox, and independence.
- 18. Random Variables: independence, distribution functions, expectation, and linearity of expectation.
- 19. Deviation from the Mean: Markov and Chebyshev, variance, sampling and confidence, sums of random variables and Chernoff bounds.
- 20. Random Walks: gambler's ruin and random walks on graphs, including stationary distributions.

**Part V, Recurrences**

- 21. Recurrences: Towers of Hanoi, merge sort, linear recurrences, divide-and-conquer recurrences, and a feel for their growth.

The text ends with a bibliography, a glossary of symbols and an index. The
glossary is the page I keep open in the first weeks, because the notation is
where most early confusion comes from.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "A proof is a long explanation." It is a short chain of steps a sceptic can check, each justified.
- "Checking many cases proves it." It is evidence; only the induction step or the general argument
  proves it.
- "Big O means fast." It means a bound on growth for large inputs; constants and small inputs still
  matter.
- "Probability is intuition with numbers." The sample space comes first; intuition about conditional
  probability is reliably wrong.
- "A 95 percent interval has a 95 percent chance of containing the truth." The 95 percent describes the
  procedure over many repetitions.
- "It covers all of discrete maths." It leaves out Boolean algebra and linear algebra, which Phase 2
  also needs.
