## Why this book

This is the primary book for Phase 2 of the <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>,
discrete mathematics, and the free one: MIT publishes it with the 6.042J course, lectures included. I
follow the Spring 2015 revision because that is the one the plan links. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-2')}>Phase 2 strategy</a> is built
around it.

I write software for a living, and most of my reasoning about it is "I tried it and it worked". This
book replaces that with proofs, counting and probability: the tools that say what must be true for
every input, not just the ones I tried. It does not cover Boolean circuits or linear algebra, which
the phase also asks for; for those I use
<a href={withBase('/books/discrete-mathematics-and-its-applications/')}>Rosen</a> and a short linear
algebra source.

Phase 2 is four weeks (weeks 6 to 9), and the book is over 900 pages, so I do not read all of it. I
read the chapters the phase's builds and gate need, with the matching 6.042J lecture first, and I
leave the rest for when a later phase reaches back for them.

For each chapter: watch the lecture, read the chapter once, then write two proofs in the proof
notebook without looking, and only then check them against the text. Anything computable, I compute:
a truth table generator, the modular toolkit, the simulations, a graph checker. A proof that I can
also test is a proof I understand twice.

## Part I, Proofs

### 1. What is a Proof?

**Covers:** Propositions and predicates, the axiomatic method, proving an implication and an if-and-only-if, proof by cases, proof by contradiction, and what makes a proof good in practice.

<Levels label="Chapter 1, What is a Proof?">

<Level name="Beginner">

A proposition is a statement that is true or false. A proof is a chain of steps, each following from
axioms or earlier steps, that ends at the claim. The smallest proof that works: "if `n` is even then
`n * n` is even", because `n = 2k` gives `n * n = 2(2k * k)`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Real proofs combine methods. To prove "P if and only if Q" I prove two implications. To prove a claim
about all integers I may split into cases (even and odd) and handle each. A proof by contradiction
assumes the claim is false and derives something impossible: the classic one shows the square root
of 2 is not a fraction in lowest terms.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

An implication "if P then Q" is false only when P is true and Q is false. So it is true whenever P is
false, which is why "if pigs fly, then 1 = 2" is a true statement. Every step in a proof must be one
I could justify to a sceptic; "clearly" is where errors hide.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A claim about all integers checked for zero, one and a large number is still unproved. The empty
case matters: a statement about every element of an empty set is true, vacuously. A proof that
divides by something must show it is not zero.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Proof by cases where the cases overlap or miss one: "x is positive or negative" forgets zero, and if
zero is also where the formula divides, a missing case and a boundary meet in one hole.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A test checks some inputs; a proof covers all of them. In production that difference is the gap
between "passes CI" and "cannot happen". I use proofs for the invariants that must never break: a
balance never negative, a lock always released.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Ask "how do I know?" of every step. That habit is the antidote to "it works on my machine".

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Specifications, type systems and model checkers are proofs at scale. A type checker proves a whole
class of errors absent; a property-based test searches for a counterexample to a claimed theorem.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Write one proof a day in the phase 2 proof notebook, then read it aloud to check every step. Done
when I can prove that the square root of 2 is irrational and explain each step without notes.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 2. The Well Ordering Principle

**Covers:** Every nonempty set of nonnegative integers has a smallest element, how to use that to prove things, a template for well ordering proofs, factoring into primes, and well ordered sets.

<Levels label="Chapter 2, The Well Ordering Principle">

<Level name="Beginner">

Any nonempty set of natural numbers has a least element. It sounds obvious, and it is a proof tool:
assume a counterexample exists, take the smallest one, and show a smaller one must also exist.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The template: define the set of counterexamples, assume it is nonempty, take its least element `m`,
derive a contradiction, usually a smaller counterexample. Proving that every integer greater than 1
is a product of primes works this way.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Well ordering, ordinary induction and strong induction are equivalent ways to say the same thing
about the natural numbers. It fails for the integers or the rationals: there is no smallest negative
integer and no smallest positive fraction.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The set must be nonempty and bounded below; the principle says nothing about an empty set, and the
proof must not assume the least element is 0 or 1 when the base differs.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A "smallest counterexample" argument that produces a smaller element which is not in the domain
(say, a negative number) proves nothing. Base boundary plus the domain boundary is the trap.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Termination arguments are well ordering in disguise: a loop terminates if some nonnegative integer
measure strictly decreases on every iteration. That is how I argue a retry loop or a parser ends.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Look for the smallest thing that goes wrong. Debugging by shrinking a failing input is the same move.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Property-based test frameworks shrink a failing case to a minimal one: well ordering, automated.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Prove prime factorisation exists with the template, then write a termination argument for a while
loop in my phase 1 tool. Done when I can state the decreasing measure for every loop in it.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 3. Logical Formulas

**Covers:** Building propositions with connectives, propositional logic inside programs, equivalence and validity, the algebra of propositions, the SAT problem, and predicate formulas with quantifiers.

<Levels label="Chapter 3, Logical Formulas">

<Level name="Beginner">

AND, OR, NOT, implies and iff build formulas from simple propositions. A truth table lists every
combination of inputs, so a formula of `n` variables has `2^n` rows.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Every `if` in a program is a formula. Simplifying `if (!(a && b))` into `if (!a || !b)` is De
Morgan's law, and rewriting a guard is only safe when the two formulas are equivalent.

```ts
// equivalent guards, by De Morgan
const blocked = !(user.active && user.verified);
const blocked2 = !user.active || !user.verified;
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Two formulas are equivalent when they agree on every row; a formula is valid when it is true on every
row and satisfiable when it is true on at least one. Predicate logic adds "for all" and "there
exists"; negating swaps them: not every x is P means some x is not P.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

"For all x in an empty domain, P(x)" is true; "there exists x in an empty domain" is false. JavaScript
agrees: `[].every(f)` is `true` and `[].some(f)` is `false`.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Short-circuit evaluation plus side effects: `a() && b()` is logically symmetric but not in a program,
because `b` may never run. Logic meets evaluation order.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Permission checks, feature flags and validation rules are formulas; bugs come from wrong negation
and wrong quantifiers. Writing them as small named predicates and testing the truth table catches
both.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Negate carefully, quantifier first. Most logic bugs are a misplaced "not".

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

SAT is the canonical hard problem, and SAT solvers are used in dependency resolution, scheduling and
verification. When a package manager resolves versions, it is solving a satisfiability problem.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Write a truth-table generator in TypeScript that checks two formulas for equivalence. Done when it
confirms both De Morgan laws and finds a counterexample to a wrong one.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 4. Mathematical Data Types

**Covers:** Sets, sequences, functions, binary relations, and finite cardinality: the vocabulary the rest of the book is written in.

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Mathematical Data Types">

<Level name="Beginner">

A set is an unordered collection with no duplicates; a sequence is ordered and may repeat; a function
maps each input to exactly one output. These are the same ideas as `Set`, arrays and pure functions.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Functions are classified by how they map: injective (no two inputs share an output), surjective
(every output is hit), bijective (both). Bijections are how we prove two sets have the same size.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

A binary relation is any set of pairs; a function is a relation with exactly one output per input.
For finite sets, an injection from A to B means A is no bigger than B, and a surjection means A is no
smaller.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The empty set is a subset of every set. A function from the empty set exists and is unique. The power
set of a set with `n` elements has `2^n` elements, including the empty set.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A "function" defined by a rule that gives two outputs for one input, or none, is not a function. In
code: a lookup that may return two rows, or nothing, has quietly become a relation.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Data modelling is choosing these types. A foreign key is a function from rows to rows; a many-to-many
table is a relation. Getting injective versus not wrong is a duplicate-key bug.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Name the type before writing the code: is this a set, a sequence, a function or a relation?

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Schemas, APIs and type systems are mathematical data types with syntax. A map with unique keys is a
function; an index is a relation stored for fast lookup.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Classify ten functions from my own code as injective, surjective or bijective, with one-line reasons.
Done when each classification survives a counterexample search in a small test.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 5. Induction

**Covers:** Ordinary induction, strong induction, how they relate to well ordering, and state machines with invariants.

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Induction">

<Level name="Beginner">

To prove P(n) for all natural numbers: prove P(0), then prove that P(n) implies P(n + 1). Like
dominoes. The first example in most courses: `1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n + 1) / 2`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Strong induction assumes P for every smaller value, not just the one before. It proves that every
amount of postage above a threshold can be made from two stamp sizes, and that a recursive function
is correct when it recurses on any smaller input.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Ordinary induction, strong induction and well ordering are equivalent in power; pick the one that
makes the proof shortest. A state machine invariant is induction over steps: true initially, and
preserved by every transition, so true forever.

```ts
// invariant: lo <= answer <= hi, maintained by every step
function isqrt(n: number): number {
  let lo = 0, hi = n + 1;            // lo*lo <= n < hi*hi
  while (hi - lo > 1) {
    const mid = Math.floor((lo + hi) / 2);
    if (mid * mid <= n) lo = mid; else hi = mid;
  }
  return lo;
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The base case must match the claim's domain: proving from 1 when the claim starts at 0 leaves 0
unproved. A step that only works for `n` at least 2 needs extra base cases.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

The classic false proof that all horses are the same colour: the step from 1 to 2 fails because two
sets of size 1 do not overlap. A step that silently needs `n` at least 2, plus a base case at 1.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Loop invariants are how I review a tricky loop: state it, check it holds on entry, check each
iteration preserves it, check it plus the exit condition gives the answer. That is a proof, in a code
review.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Prove the step, not the examples. Ten checked cases are evidence; the step is the proof.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Distributed protocols are state machines, and their safety properties are invariants proved by
induction over transitions. TLA+ and model checkers check exactly this.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Prove the sum formula, the postage claim and the invariant of my `isqrt`. Done when I can prove a
recurrence by induction on paper, which is part of the phase 2 gate.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 6. Recursive Data Types

**Covers:** Recursive definitions and structural induction, strings of matched brackets, recursive functions on the nonnegative integers, arithmetic expressions, and induction in computer science.

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Recursive Data Types">

<Level name="Beginner">

A recursive data type is defined by base cases and constructors: a list is empty or an element
followed by a list; a tree is a leaf or a node with subtrees. TypeScript's union types say exactly
this.

```ts
type Expr =
  | { kind: 'num'; value: number }
  | { kind: 'add'; left: Expr; right: Expr }
  | { kind: 'mul'; left: Expr; right: Expr };
```

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Functions on recursive types follow the definition: one case per constructor. An evaluator for
arithmetic expressions is a switch on `kind` that calls itself on the parts.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Structural induction proves a property for every value of the type: prove it for the base cases, and
show each constructor preserves it. It is how to prove an evaluator correct, or that every string of
matched brackets has as many opens as closes.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The empty list and the single leaf are the base cases where most recursive functions are wrong.
A definition with no base case defines nothing.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A recursive definition that is ambiguous: two different ways to build the same value, plus a function
defined per construction, gives a function with two answers. Grammars hit this with operator
precedence.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Recursion over user-supplied nested data can blow the stack; deeply nested JSON is a known
denial-of-service. Production parsers bound the depth or use an explicit stack.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Let the data's shape decide the code's shape. One case per constructor, and the compiler checks I
handled them all.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

ASTs, JSON, file systems and the DOM are recursive types. Phase 9's interpreter is this chapter at
full size.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Write the expression type and its evaluator, prove by structural induction that it terminates, and
fuzz it. Done when an exhaustive switch makes the compiler reject a missing case.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 7. Infinite Sets

**Covers:** Infinite cardinality, countable and uncountable sets, the halting problem, the logic of sets, and whether the foundations really work.

<Levels label="Chapter 7, Infinite Sets">

<Level name="Beginner">

Two sets have the same size when there is a bijection between them. The integers and the even
integers are the same size, which is the first surprise of infinite sets.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A set is countable if it can be listed: the integers, the rationals, all finite strings. The reals
are not: Cantor's diagonal argument builds a number not on any list.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The same diagonal argument shows there is no program that decides, for every program and input,
whether it halts. Assume one exists, build a program that does the opposite of what it predicts
about itself, and contradiction follows.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

"Infinite" is not one size. The set of all subsets of a countable set is uncountable, so there are
more problems than programs.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Self-reference plus a "contains everything" set gives Russell's paradox: the set of all sets that do
not contain themselves. Unrestricted definitions meeting self-reference is why axioms exist.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Undecidability is practical: no linter can find every infinite loop, no analyser can be both sound and
complete for interesting properties. Tools choose to over-warn or under-warn.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Some questions have no algorithm. Knowing that saves me from building the impossible checker.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Static analysers, type systems and timeouts are engineering answers to undecidability: restrict the
language, approximate, or bound the run time.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Write the diagonal argument and the halting proof in my own words. Done when I can explain to a
colleague why a perfect infinite-loop detector cannot exist.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Part II, Structures

### 8. Number Theory

**Covers:** Divisibility, the greatest common divisor, primes and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, modular and remainder arithmetic, multiplicative inverses, Euler's theorem, RSA public key encryption, and the link to SAT.

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Number Theory">

<Level name="Beginner">

`a` divides `b` when `b` is a multiple of `a`. The greatest common divisor of two numbers is found by
Euclid's algorithm: replace the pair with the smaller number and the remainder until the remainder
is zero.

```ts
const gcd = (a: bigint, b: bigint): bigint => (b === 0n ? a : gcd(b, a % b));
```

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The extended algorithm also returns `x` and `y` with `a*x + b*y = gcd(a, b)`. When the gcd is 1, `x`
is the inverse of `a` modulo `b`, which is what RSA key generation needs.

```ts
function egcd(a: bigint, b: bigint): [bigint, bigint, bigint] {
  if (b === 0n) return [a, 1n, 0n];
  const [g, x, y] = egcd(b, a % b);
  return [g, y, x - (a / b) * y];
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Arithmetic modulo `n` respects addition and multiplication, so huge powers can be reduced at every
step. Euler's theorem says `a` to the power phi(n) is 1 modulo `n` when `a` and `n` share no factor;
RSA decrypts because the exponents `e` and `d` multiply to 1 modulo phi(n).

```ts
function modexp(base: bigint, exp: bigint, mod: bigint): bigint {
  let result = 1n; base %= mod;
  while (exp > 0n) {
    if (exp & 1n) result = (result * base) % mod;
    base = (base * base) % mod; exp >>= 1n;
  }
  return result;
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

`gcd(0, 0)` is conventionally 0; `gcd(a, 0)` is `a`. Anything modulo 1 is 0. A number with no
inverse (sharing a factor with the modulus) makes "division" undefined.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

JavaScript's `%` is a remainder, not a modulo: `-7 % 3` is `-1`. A negative intermediate plus a
modular reduction gives a wrong inverse. Normalise with `((a % n) + n) % n`. And with plain `number`,
products above 2 to the 53 lose precision silently: use `bigint`.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Never write your own cryptography for real use. Textbook RSA without padding is breakable, timing
leaks through non-constant-time code, and key generation needs a secure random source. The toolkit
is for understanding.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Big numbers are made safe by reducing early and often. The same idea keeps counters and hashes in
range.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Hashing, checksums, random number generators, sharding by `id % n` and public key cryptography all
rest on modular arithmetic. Consistent hashing exists because `id % n` reshuffles everything when
`n` changes.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Build the phase 2 modular toolkit: gcd, extended gcd, inverse, modexp and a toy RSA round trip with
`bigint`, tested against brute force for small numbers. Done when the round trip works for random
messages and I can explain why decryption inverts encryption.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 9. Directed graphs & Partial Orders

**Covers:** Vertex degrees, walks and paths, adjacency matrices, walk relations, directed acyclic graphs and scheduling, partial and linear orders, product orders, and equivalence relations.

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Directed graphs & Partial Orders">

<Level name="Beginner">

A directed graph is vertices and arrows. In-degree counts arrows in, out-degree arrows out. A walk
follows arrows; a path never repeats a vertex.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A directed acyclic graph models dependencies: tasks and "must come before". A topological sort lists
the tasks so every arrow points forward, which is what a build tool or a task runner computes.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The adjacency matrix raised to the power `k` counts walks of length `k`. A partial order is reflexive,
antisymmetric and transitive; an equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive and
splits a set into classes. The longest path in a DAG bounds how fast parallel work can finish.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A graph with no edges has every order as a topological sort. A self-loop is already a cycle and makes
a topological order impossible.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two tasks with no dependency between them are incomparable in a partial order, so a sort may put
them either way; code that relies on one particular order works until the sort changes.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Dependency cycles in packages, imports or migrations show up as a failed topological sort. A build
system that reports the cycle, not just "failed", saves hours.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Most "order" in software is partial. Ask which pairs really must be ordered.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Build systems, package managers, CI pipelines, spreadsheets and React's rendering all schedule a DAG.
Critical path analysis says where adding workers helps.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Implement topological sort with cycle detection in TypeScript and run it on my repo's import graph.
Done when it prints a valid order or names the cycle.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 10. Communication Networks

**Covers:** Networks as graphs: complete binary trees, routing problems, diameter, switch count, latency, congestion, and the 2-D array, butterfly and Benes networks.

<Levels label="Chapter 10, Communication Networks">

<Level name="Beginner">

A communication network routes packets from inputs to outputs through switches. Its quality is
measured by a few numbers: how many switches, the longest route (diameter), and how badly routes
share switches (congestion).

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A complete binary tree has short routes but every cross-tree route goes through the root: the root is
a bottleneck. A 2-D grid spreads load but routes are long. Every design trades one number for
another.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The butterfly network has logarithmic diameter and few switches, but some permutations of traffic
congest it badly. The Benes network, two butterflies back to back, can route any permutation without
congestion.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

With one input and one output, every design is optimal. The comparison only matters as the number of
endpoints grows.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A network optimised for diameter meets an adversarial traffic pattern: average latency looks fine and
one permutation saturates a single switch.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Data centre fabrics, on-chip networks and load balancers face the same trade-offs. Real traffic is
not uniform; design for the bad permutation.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Measure a design by its worst case, then by its cost. A pretty average hides the bottleneck.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

This is the bridge to phase 7, networks, and phase 10, distributed systems: topology determines
latency, and bottlenecks determine throughput.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Draw a tree, a grid and a small butterfly, and compute diameter, switch count and the worst
congestion for one permutation. Done when I can say which design I would pick for which traffic.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 11. Simple Graphs

**Covers:** Vertex adjacency and degrees, common graphs, isomorphism, bipartite graphs and matchings, the stable marriage problem, coloring, simple walks, connectivity, and forests and trees.

<Levels label="Chapter 11, Simple Graphs">

<Level name="Beginner">

A simple graph has undirected edges, no loops and no repeated edges. The sum of all degrees is twice
the number of edges, so the number of odd-degree vertices is always even.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Connected components, bipartiteness and cycles are the properties the phase 2 graph checker reports.
A breadth-first search that 2-colours as it goes finds all three.

```ts
function isBipartite(adj: number[][]): boolean {
  const color = new Array(adj.length).fill(-1);
  for (let s = 0; s < adj.length; s++) {
    if (color[s] !== -1) continue;
    color[s] = 0; const queue = [s];
    while (queue.length) {
      const u = queue.shift()!;
      for (const v of adj[u]) {
        if (color[v] === -1) { color[v] = 1 - color[u]; queue.push(v); }
        else if (color[v] === color[u]) return false;   // odd cycle
      }
    }
  }
  return true;
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

A graph is bipartite exactly when it has no odd cycle. A tree is connected with no cycles, and has
exactly one fewer edge than vertices. Hall's theorem says when a perfect matching exists. Stable
matching has an algorithm that always terminates with a stable result.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A graph with one vertex is connected and a tree. The empty graph is bipartite. A disconnected graph
needs the outer loop over all start vertices, or components are missed.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Disconnected plus odd cycle in a later component: a checker that searches only from vertex 0 reports
"bipartite" for a graph that is not.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Graph colouring is register allocation, exam scheduling and frequency assignment; exact colouring is
hard, so production tools use heuristics. Recursion depth in DFS on a long path graph overflows the
stack; use an explicit queue or stack.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Many problems become easy once I see the graph in them.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Social graphs, dependency graphs, network topologies and matching markets are all simple graphs at
scale. Phase 3 stores them; phase 4 searches them.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Build the phase 2 graph checker: components, cycle detection and bipartiteness, tested on random
graphs against brute force for small sizes. Done when it handles disconnected graphs and I can prove
the odd-cycle theorem.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 12. Planar Graphs

**Covers:** Drawing graphs in the plane, definitions of planar graphs, Euler's formula, bounding the number of edges, K5 and K3,3, coloring planar graphs, classifying polyhedra, and another characterisation of planarity.

<Levels label="Chapter 12, Planar Graphs">

<Level name="Beginner">

A planar graph can be drawn with no crossing edges. Some cannot: the complete graph on five vertices
and the complete bipartite graph on three plus three.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Euler's formula links vertices, edges and faces of a connected planar drawing: `v - e + f = 2`. From it
follows that a simple planar graph has at most `3v - 6` edges, which proves K5 is not planar.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Every planar graph can be coloured with six colours by an induction on vertices, and in fact five,
and famously four. The formula also classifies the regular polyhedra: there are exactly five.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Euler's formula needs the graph connected; for `c` components it becomes `v - e + f = 1 + c`. The edge
bound needs at least three vertices.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A graph that meets the edge bound may still not be planar; the bound is necessary, not sufficient.
A necessary condition mistaken for a sufficient one is a common logical error.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Circuit board layout and map drawing care about crossings; graph-drawing tools use planarity tests to
lay out diagrams cleanly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Counting arguments can prove impossibility without trying every drawing.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Planarity is rarely a system design concern, but the move, a cheap invariant that rules out a whole
design space, is common.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Prove that K5 is not planar using the edge bound. Done when I can say why the bound is not enough to
prove a graph is planar.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Part III, Counting

### 13. Sums and Asymptotics

**Covers:** The value of an annuity, sums of powers, approximating sums with integrals, the overhanging book stack, products and Stirling's formula, double sums, and asymptotic notation.

<Levels label="Chapter 13, Sums and Asymptotics">

<Level name="Beginner">

A closed form replaces a sum with a formula: `1 + 2 + ... + n` is `n(n + 1) / 2`, and a geometric
sum `1 + x + ... + x^n` is `(x^(n+1) - 1) / (x - 1)`. Asymptotic notation, big O, Omega and Theta,
describes growth while ignoring constants.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A loop that does `i` units of work at step `i` costs the sum `1 + ... + n`, which is Theta of `n`
squared. Counting operations is summing, which is the phase 1 topic "counting instead of guessing".

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The harmonic sum `1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n` grows like the natural log of `n`, which is why some average
cases are logarithmic. Integrals bound sums from above and below. `f` is O of `g` means `f` is at
most a constant times `g` for large enough inputs, nothing more.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The geometric formula breaks at `x = 1`, where the sum is just `n + 1`. Big O says nothing about small
inputs, where the constant often dominates.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two algorithms with the same Theta and very different constants, at a small input size: the
"slower" one wins. Asymptotics meets the real input range.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Doubling a dynamic array costs a geometric sum, so appends are cheap on average. Quadratic
behaviour hidden in a library call inside a loop is the most common performance bug.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Ask how it grows before asking how fast it is.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Capacity planning is asymptotics with constants: how does cost grow with users, rows, nodes?

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Derive the cost of three loops from my phase 1 tool as sums, then benchmark at sizes that differ by
ten. Done when the measured ratios match the predicted growth.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 14. Cardinality Rules

**Covers:** Counting one thing by counting another, counting sequences, the generalised product rule, the division rule, counting subsets, sequences with repetitions, poker hands, the pigeonhole principle, inclusion-exclusion, and combinatorial proofs.

<Levels label="Chapter 14, Cardinality Rules">

<Level name="Beginner">

The sum rule adds counts of disjoint choices; the product rule multiplies counts of independent
choices. A 4-digit PIN has `10^4` possibilities.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Permutations order, combinations do not. Choosing `k` of `n` without order is `n choose k`, which is
the number of orderings divided by the orderings of each group: the division rule.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Bijections count hard sets by matching them to easy ones. The pigeonhole principle: `n + 1` items in
`n` boxes put two in one box. Inclusion-exclusion corrects for overlaps: the size of A or B is the
size of A plus B minus their overlap.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

`n choose 0` is 1: there is one way to choose nothing. `0!` is 1 for the same reason. Choosing more
than `n` gives 0.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Overcounting plus overlap: counting poker hands by picking cards in order counts each hand many
times, and two overlapping categories counted separately double count. Both fixes, divide and
subtract, are needed together.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Pigeonhole says hashes collide: more keys than buckets guarantees it. Counting bounds the size of a
key space, the number of test cases for exhaustive testing, and brute-force cost for a password.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Count a set two ways and the answers must agree. That is a self-check for any derivation.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

ID lengths, shard counts, cache key spaces and rate limits are counting problems. A 32-bit ID space
runs out; a counting argument says when.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Solve each counting rule's problems by formula, then check small cases by brute-force enumeration in
code. Done when formula and enumeration agree on every problem I try.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 15. Generating Functions

**Covers:** Infinite series, counting with generating functions, partial fractions, solving linear recurrences, and formal power series.

<Levels label="Chapter 15, Generating Functions">

<Level name="Beginner">

A generating function packs a sequence into the coefficients of a power series. The sequence 1, 1, 1,
... becomes `1 / (1 - x)`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Multiplying generating functions combines choices: the number of ways to make change for an amount is
a coefficient in a product. That turns counting into algebra.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Partial fractions split a rational function into simple pieces whose coefficients are known, which
gives closed forms for linear recurrences, such as the Fibonacci numbers.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Formal power series never need to converge; the variable is a placeholder, not a number. Treating it
as a number outside the radius of convergence leads to wrong "sums".

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A repeated root in the denominator changes the shape of the closed form (an extra factor of `n`);
missing that gives a formula right for small `n` and wrong later.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

This is rarely in production code, but its result is: closed forms and growth rates of recurrences
tell me the cost of a recursive algorithm before I run it.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Change the representation and a hard problem can become routine.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Dynamic programming over counts, such as the coin-change table, is a generating-function product
computed one coefficient at a time.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Derive the Fibonacci closed form with generating functions and check it in code for the first fifty
terms with `bigint`. Done when I can redo the derivation unaided.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Part IV, Probability

### 16. Events and Probability Spaces

**Covers:** The Monty Hall game, the four-step method, strange dice, the birthday principle, and set theory as the language of probability.

<Levels label="Chapter 16, Events and Probability Spaces">

<Level name="Beginner">

A probability space lists outcomes and their probabilities; an event is a set of outcomes. The
four-step method: find the sample space, define the events, assign outcome probabilities, compute.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Monty Hall: switching wins two times in three, because the host's choice depends on where the car is.
A tree diagram makes it obvious; a simulation confirms it.

```ts
function montyHall(trials: number, swap: boolean): number {
  let wins = 0;
  for (let t = 0; t < trials; t++) {
    const car = Math.floor(Math.random() * 3);
    const pick = Math.floor(Math.random() * 3);
    // the host opens a goat door; switching wins exactly when the first pick was wrong
    wins += swap ? Number(pick !== car) : Number(pick === car);
  }
  return wins / trials;
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The birthday principle: with `n` days, a collision becomes likely after about the square root of `n`
people; 23 people for 365 days. Strange dice show that "beats" need not be transitive.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

An event of probability zero in a finite space never happens; in a simulation, a rare event may
simply not appear in a small run, which is not evidence that it cannot.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A biased random generator plus a rare event: `Math.random()` is fine for this, but modulo bias from
`random % n` on small integer generators distorts exactly the tail I am measuring.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

The birthday bound tells me when random IDs collide: 64-bit random IDs collide around four billion
items. UUID choice and hash width are birthday calculations.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Write the sample space before arguing. Intuition is the least reliable tool in probability.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Collision probability decides hash sizes, idempotency keys and sampling rates.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Simulate Monty Hall and the birthday problem and compare with the exact answers. Done when each
simulation's estimate sits inside a confidence interval that contains the exact value.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 17. Conditional Probability

**Covers:** Monty Hall confusion, definition and notation, the four-step method with conditions, why tree diagrams work, the law of total probability, Simpson's paradox, independence, and mutual independence.

<Levels label="Chapter 17, Conditional Probability">

<Level name="Beginner">

The probability of A given B is the probability of both divided by the probability of B: the world
shrinks to B. Bayes' theorem flips the condition.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A test for a rare condition: 1 in 1000 has it, the test catches 99 percent and false-alarms on 1
percent. A positive result means about a 9 percent chance of having it, because false positives from
the large healthy group outnumber the true ones. That is the Bayes example the phase 2 gate wants.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The law of total probability sums over the branches of a tree. Independence means knowing B does not
change A. Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Conditioning on an event of probability zero is undefined. Conditioning on the trivial event changes
nothing.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Simpson's paradox: a treatment better in every subgroup can look worse overall when group sizes
differ. Aggregation plus an uneven split reverses the conclusion.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

A/B tests, alert thresholds and fraud scores are conditional probabilities; base rates decide whether
an alert is worth waking someone for.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Always ask: given what? And what is the base rate?

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Monitoring systems, recommendation and spam filters are Bayes in production; the base rate decides
the false alarm load on a team.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Work the medical-test example by tree, by formula and by simulation. Done when I can explain Bayes
with this example to someone who has never seen the formula.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 18. Random Variables

**Covers:** Examples of random variables, their independence, distribution functions, expectation, and linearity of expectation.

<Levels label="Chapter 18, Random Variables">

<Level name="Beginner">

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome: the sum of two dice, the number of heads. Its
expectation is the probability-weighted average.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Linearity of expectation: the expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations, independent or
not. The expected number of people who get their own hat back in a random shuffle is exactly 1.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Binomial, geometric and uniform distributions cover most first problems. The expected number of
trials until the first success with probability `p` is `1 / p`.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A variable with infinite expectation exists (the St. Petersburg game); an average of simulations of
it never settles.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Linearity holds without independence, but the expectation of a product needs independence. Mixing
those up in a dependent system gives a wrong answer with a correct-looking derivation.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Expected retries, expected queue lengths and expected cost per request are expectations; the
expectation alone hides the tail, which is what users feel.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Break a hard count into indicator variables and add their expectations.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Load estimates and capacity models start from expectations and must then look at variance and tails.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Compute the expected number of fixed points of a random permutation by linearity, then simulate it.
Done when the simulation agrees within its confidence interval.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 19. Deviation from the Mean

**Covers:** Markov's and Chebyshev's theorems, properties of variance, estimation by random sampling, confidence versus probability, sums of random variables, and stronger bounds.

<Levels label="Chapter 19, Deviation from the Mean">

<Level name="Beginner">

Variance measures spread around the mean; the standard deviation is its square root. Markov's bound
limits how often a nonnegative variable exceeds a multiple of its mean.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Chebyshev's bound uses the variance to limit deviation in both directions, and it justifies sampling:
the average of many independent samples concentrates near the true mean.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

For independent variables, variances add. A 95 percent confidence interval for a proportion is the
estimate plus or minus about 1.96 standard errors. Chernoff-style bounds give much tighter tails for
sums of independent variables.

```ts
function ci95(successes: number, n: number): [number, number] {
  const p = successes / n;
  const se = Math.sqrt((p * (1 - p)) / n);
  return [p - 1.96 * se, p + 1.96 * se];
}
```

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

With zero successes the simple interval collapses to a single point, which is wrong; small samples
and proportions near 0 or 1 need a better interval, such as Wilson's.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Dependent samples plus a confidence interval that assumes independence: the interval is far too
narrow. Correlated benchmark runs on one warm machine are the everyday example.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Benchmarks, latency percentiles and A/B results need intervals, not single numbers. "Faster" means
the intervals do not overlap, measured properly.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

A number without its uncertainty is a guess. Confidence is about the procedure, not about this one
interval.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Sampling for metrics and tracing, and canary analysis, are estimation by random sampling.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Add a confidence interval to the Monty Hall and birthday simulations and show how its width shrinks
with the square root of the trial count. Done when I can say what the interval does and does not mean,
which is part of the phase 2 gate.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 20. Random Walks

**Covers:** Gambler's ruin and random walks on graphs.

<Levels label="Chapter 20, Random Walks">

<Level name="Beginner">

A random walk moves up or down by chance at each step. Gambler's ruin asks the probability of
reaching a target before going broke.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

With a fair game, the chance of winning is the starting stake over the target. With even a slightly
unfair game, the chance of a large win collapses.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Random walks on graphs have stationary distributions: the long-run share of time spent at each
vertex. Ranking web pages by such a distribution is the idea behind PageRank.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Starting at the target or at zero ends the walk at step zero. A walk on a disconnected graph never
reaches the other component.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A slightly unfair game plus a long horizon: tiny bias, huge consequence. Expected duration can be
enormous even when ruin is certain.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Retry storms and queue lengths behave like walks with drift: a little more arrival than service and
the queue grows without bound.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Small persistent bias beats large random noise over time.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Load balancing by random choice and graph ranking use walks; queueing theory in phase 10 starts here.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Simulate gambler's ruin for fair and slightly unfair games and compare with the formulas. Done when
the simulation's intervals contain the exact values.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Part V, Recurrences

### 21. Recurrences

**Covers:** The Towers of Hanoi, merge sort, linear recurrences, divide-and-conquer recurrences, and a feel for how recurrences grow.

<Levels label="Chapter 21, Recurrences">

<Level name="Beginner">

A recurrence defines a sequence by earlier terms. Towers of Hanoi with `n` disks takes `T(n) = 2T(n -
1) + 1` moves, which is `2^n - 1`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Merge sort's cost is `T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n`, which solves to about `n log n`. Guess the closed form,
then prove it by induction: that is the phase 2 gate's recurrence proof.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Linear recurrences are solved through their characteristic roots, giving forms like Fibonacci's.
Divide-and-conquer recurrences are solved by a general theorem (Akra-Bazzi in this book, of which the
"master theorem" is a special case) that compares the work at the top with the work in the leaves.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

The base case decides the constant and sometimes the answer. `n` not a power of two needs floors and
ceilings, which usually do not change the growth.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A recurrence whose subproblems overlap, solved naively, explodes: plain recursive Fibonacci is
exponential. Overlap plus recursion is where memoisation enters.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Recursive algorithms in production need their recurrence: an innocent-looking recursion can be
exponential, and a deep one can overflow the stack.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Guess, then prove. Small values suggest the pattern; induction confirms it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Divide and conquer drives sorting, search, parallel map-reduce and tree-structured aggregation; the
recurrence says where the cost sits.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

Solve Hanoi and merge sort recurrences, prove one by induction, and check each against a counter in
code. Done when the counted operations match the closed form exactly for small inputs.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Misreadings to avoid

- "A proof is a long explanation." It is a short chain of steps a sceptic can check, each justified.
- "Checking many cases proves it." It is evidence; only the induction step or the general argument
  proves it.
- "Big O means fast." It means a bound on growth for large inputs; constants and small inputs still
  matter.
- "Probability is intuition with numbers." The sample space comes first; intuition about conditional
  probability is reliably wrong.
- "A 95 percent interval has a 95 percent chance of containing the truth." The 95 percent describes the
  procedure over many repetitions.
- "It covers all of discrete maths." It leaves out Boolean algebra and linear algebra, which Phase 2
  also needs.
