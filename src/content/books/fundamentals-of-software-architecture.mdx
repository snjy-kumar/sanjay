## Why this book

It is the primary book for phase 10 of the
<a href={withBase('/plans/software-ai-developer/')}>software AI developer roadmap</a>,
architecture and system design, and it sits on the "later" shelf of
<a href={withBase('/plans/books-that-still-matter/')}>the books that still matter</a>.
Phase 10 says architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to
reverse, and asks me to make them explicitly: modular monolith or services,
boundaries, events, consistency, ADRs and failure matrices. This book gives the
vocabulary for all of it: architecture characteristics, quanta, styles,
fitness functions, decision records, and the soft skills to defend a design.

I read the second edition, which adds chapters on the modular monolith,
architectural patterns, intersections with data, teams and generative AI, and a
revisit of the laws. My examples are TypeScript and Node, because that is my
capstone's stack. Everything below is my paraphrase and my own examples.

### 1. Introduction

**Covers:** Defining software architecture (structure, characteristics, decisions, design principles), the laws of software architecture, the expectations of an architect (decide, analyse continually, keep current, ensure compliance, know diverse technologies and the domain, interpersonal skills, politics), and the book's roadmap.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Design principles', note: 'guidelines, not rules: prefer async messaging between services' },
  { name: 'Architecture decisions', note: 'rules: only the service layer may call the database' },
  { name: 'Architecture characteristics', note: 'the "-ilities": scalability, availability, testability' },
  { name: 'Structure', note: 'the style: layered, microservices, event-driven' },
]} caption="Four dimensions that together describe an architecture. Naming only the structure leaves most of it out." />

<Levels label="Chapter 1, Introduction">

<Level name="Beginner">

Software architecture is more than the boxes on a diagram. It is the structure
(the style), the architecture characteristics the system must support (the
"-ilities"), the architecture decisions (rules for how it is built), and design
principles (guidelines). Two laws frame everything: every choice in architecture
is a trade-off, and why a decision was made matters more than how.

```md
Structure: modular monolith (Node, one deployable)
Characteristics: availability, testability, cost
Decision: modules talk only through their public index.ts
Principle: prefer events over direct calls between modules
```

Common mistake: describing a system as "microservices" and calling that the
architecture. Fix: write all four dimensions.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

An architect is expected to make decisions (and guide rather than dictate
technology choices), continually analyse the architecture as the system and
business change, keep current with trends, ensure the team complies with
decisions, have broad knowledge of technologies, understand the business domain,
and have the people skills and political awareness to get decisions adopted.

```ts
// compliance made checkable: no module imports another's internals
// .dependency-cruiser.cjs rule (sketch)
{ name: 'no-deep-imports', from: { path: '^src/modules/([^/]+)/' }, to: { path: '^src/modules/(?!$1)[^/]+/(?!index\\.ts$)' }, severity: 'error' }
```

Common mistake: decisions that live in a wiki and are never checked. Fix: turn
the important ones into automated checks.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Underneath, the laws are about uncertainty. If someone claims a choice has no
trade-off, they have not found it yet. And because the "how" changes (frameworks,
platforms), only the reasoning survives. That is why the book returns to the laws
in its last chapter, after showing them at work in every style. The architect's
role is less about drawing and more about continuous trade-off analysis
in context.

Common mistake: copying another company's architecture without their context.
Fix: write the "why" in their situation and check it against mine.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A tiny project with one developer: the four dimensions still
exist but fit on half a page. A legacy system with no recorded decisions: start by
writing down the decisions that are implied by the code. A team with no
architect title: someone is still making architecture decisions, implicitly.

Common mistake: "we do not have architecture". Fix: you do; it is just
undocumented.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A decision that is technically right plus politically
impossible (moving a team's database): it fails without negotiation. A trend
worth adopting plus a team without the skills: the new technology becomes a
liability. Compliance checks plus a deliberate exception: the check must allow a
documented exception, or people disable it.

Common mistake: architecture rules with no exception process. Fix: exceptions are
ADRs too.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production, architecture drifts: shortcuts under deadline erode the rules. The
architect's continual analysis is a habit of reviewing the system's real
structure (dependency graphs, deploy units, data flows) against the intended one,
and fixing or updating either.

```sh
npx depcruise src --output-type dot | dot -T svg > docs/arch/actual.svg
```

Common mistake: diagrams of the intended architecture only. Fix: generate the
actual one from code and compare.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Everything is a trade-off, and the reason is the deliverable. The habit is to
write the "why" for every structural choice, including what I gave up.

Common mistake: searching for the best architecture. Fix: search for the least
worst one for these requirements.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Architecture is the frame inside which design happens. Decisions at this level are
the ones expensive to reverse: deploy units, data ownership, communication
styles.

<Flow steps={['business drivers', 'architecture characteristics', 'style and structure', 'decisions and principles', 'design and code', 'continual analysis']} caption="From business needs to code, and back through analysis. Architecture is the middle of this chain, not its start." />

Common mistake: starting from a style. Fix: start from the business drivers.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the four dimensions for my capstone in one page and list three decisions
I have already made without noticing. Done when the page exists, and each
decision has its trade-off written next to it.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Part I, Foundations

### 2. Architectural Thinking

**Covers:** Architecture versus design, strategic versus tactical decisions, level of effort, the significance of trade-offs, technical breadth (the 20-minute rule, a personal radar), analysing trade-offs, understanding business drivers, balancing architecture and hands-on coding.

<Figure art={`
          +--------------------+
          |   stuff you know   |   depth
          +--------------------+
     +------------------------------+
     | stuff you know you don't know|  breadth
     +------------------------------+
  +------------------------------------+
  | stuff you don't know you don't know|
  +------------------------------------+
`} caption="The knowledge pyramid. An architect grows the middle layer: knowing that options exist and what they trade, without mastering each." />

<Levels label="Chapter 2, Architectural Thinking">

<Level name="Beginner">

Architectural thinking means seeing the system as a set of trade-offs. Rather
than a strict line between architecture and design, there is a spectrum:
strategic decisions (long-term, many people, hard to reverse) sit at the
architecture end, tactical ones at the design end. Level of effort helps place a
decision: how many people it touches, how long it lasts, how much work it takes
to change.

```md
Strategic: split the database per module (months, all teams, hard to undo)
Tactical: which date library (days, one module, easy to swap)
```

Common mistake: spending a week debating a tactical choice. Fix: decide fast
when the cost of reversal is low.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Technical breadth matters more than depth for an architect: knowing that five
messaging options exist and what each trades is more useful than mastering one.
The 20-minute rule sets aside a short daily slot for learning something new.
A personal radar sorts technologies into rings (hold, assess, trial, adopt) to
manage the breadth.

```md
Radar, Oct 2026
Adopt: Postgres, Vitest, OpenTelemetry
Trial: SQLite for edge, Temporal
Assess: event sourcing for audit log
Hold: microservices for the capstone
```

Common mistake: staying deep in one stack and calling it architecture. Fix: a
radar, updated quarterly.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Analysing trade-offs means listing what each option gives and costs on the
characteristics that matter. Example: a topic (publish-subscribe) versus queues
(point to point) for bid events. A topic is extensible (new consumers subscribe
with no change to the producer) but every consumer sees all data, it is harder to
monitor per consumer, and contract changes affect all consumers. Queues give per
consumer contracts, monitoring and scaling, but the producer must know each
consumer.

<Compare left="Topic (pub/sub)" right="Queues (point to point)" rows={[
  ['add consumers without touching the producer', 'producer changes for each new consumer'],
  ['all consumers get all data', 'each queue carries only what its consumer needs'],
  ['one contract for everyone', 'contract per consumer'],
  ['harder per-consumer scaling and monitoring', 'independent depth, scaling and alerts'],
]} caption="A trade-off table for one decision. Neither column wins; the characteristics decide." />

Common mistake: listing only advantages of the option I like. Fix: force at least
two costs per option.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A business driver nobody states (a sale planned in six
months that will triple traffic). An architect who no longer codes: decisions
drift from reality. A trade-off with no measurable side: an opinion, not an
analysis.

Common mistake: assuming the business driver is "make it fast". Fix: ask what
"fast" protects (revenue, retention, a deadline) and measure that.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Hands-on coding plus a bottleneck: if the architect owns
critical-path code, the team waits on them. Breadth plus decision fatigue: knowing
twenty options turns every choice into a survey. A strategic decision plus a tight
deadline: decided tactically and then lived with for years.

Common mistake: the architect taking the hardest feature. Fix: code proofs of
concept, tooling and non-critical features; leave the critical path to the team.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production architectural thinking shows up in how trade-offs are recorded and
revisited. When a characteristic becomes more important (scale suddenly
matters), I find the decisions that traded it away and reopen them.

```md
ADR-007 Synchronous calls between modules (2026-03)
Traded away: fault isolation. Revisit if: one module's error rate affects others' p95.
```

Common mistake: decisions with no revisit condition. Fix: each records what would
make it wrong.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Think in trade-offs and keep learning wide. The habit is to ask "what does this
cost?" for every option, and to spend a little time every day on something
outside my stack.

Common mistake: one favourite tool for every problem. Fix: always compare at least
two options.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Figure art={`
  strategic <-------------------------------------> tactical
  data ownership   deploy units   messaging   library   naming
  (architecture)                                       (design)
  many people, long-lived, costly to undo  ...  few, short, cheap to undo
`} caption="The architecture-design spectrum. Where a decision sits tells me how much analysis and documentation it deserves." />

Common mistake: drawing a hard line between architects and developers. Fix:
collaborate along the whole spectrum.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I build my personal radar, start a daily 20-minute slot, and write one full
trade-off table for a real capstone decision (queue or direct call for sending
email). Done when the radar exists, the trade-off table has at least two costs per
option, and the decision is recorded with a revisit condition.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 3. Modularity

**Covers:** Modularity versus granularity, defining modularity, measuring it (cohesion, coupling, core metrics including abstractness and instability, distance from the main sequence, connascence), and moving from modules to components.

<Figure art={`
 abstractness A
  1.0 |  zone of
      |  uselessness   .
      |             .
      |          .   <- main sequence: A + I = 1
      |       .
      |    .        zone of
  0.0 | .           pain
      +-------------------- instability I
     0.0                 1.0
  D = | A + I - 1 |   (distance from the main sequence)
`} caption="Distance from the main sequence. Concrete, stable modules are painful to change; abstract, unstable ones are useless." />

<Levels label="Chapter 3, Modularity">

<Level name="Beginner">

A module is a group of related code: a package, a namespace, a folder with a
public entry. Modularity is how well the system is divided into such groups.
Granularity is how big each piece is. Good modules are cohesive (their parts
belong together) and loosely coupled (they depend little on others).

```ts
// src/modules/billing/index.ts: the module's public surface
export { createInvoice } from './invoices';
export type { Invoice } from './types';
// everything else in billing/ is private by convention and by lint rule
```

Common mistake: folders named by technical layer only (controllers, services)
with no domain modules. Fix: group by domain, with a public entry per module.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Cohesion comes in kinds, from best to worst: functional (everything serves one
purpose), sequential, communicational, procedural, temporal, logical, and
coincidental. One metric, LCOM (lack of cohesion in methods), counts methods
that share no fields, which flags classes doing several unrelated things.

Coupling is measured by afferent coupling (how many depend on me), efferent
coupling (how many I depend on), and from them instability: efferent divided by
the sum of both. Abstractness is the share of abstract types (interfaces).

```ts
const ca = 6, ce = 2;                         // 6 modules import billing, billing imports 2
const instability = ce / (ca + ce);           // 0.25: stable, many depend on it
const abstractness = 3 / 12;                  // 3 interfaces among 12 types
console.log(Math.abs(abstractness + instability - 1).toFixed(2)); // 0.50: far from the main sequence
```

Common mistake: a "utils" module that everything imports and that has no clear
purpose. Fix: split it by purpose; coincidental cohesion is the worst kind.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Connascence describes coupling precisely: two parts are connascent if a change in
one requires a change in the other. Static connascence is visible in code: name,
type, meaning (magic values), position (argument order), algorithm (both sides
must use the same hash). Dynamic connascence appears at runtime: execution order,
timing, values that must change together, identity (two parts must reference the
same instance). Three properties guide refactoring: strength (dynamic is
stronger than static), locality (strong connascence is fine inside a module,
dangerous across modules), and degree (how many parts are affected).

```ts
// connascence of position: callers must know argument order
sendEmail('a@b.com', 'Hi', 'Body', true, false);
// refactor to connascence of name: weaker, safer across modules
sendEmail({ to: 'a@b.com', subject: 'Hi', body: 'Body', html: true, track: false });
```

Common mistake: treating all coupling as equally bad. Fix: reduce strong
connascence across module boundaries; tolerate it inside a module.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A module with zero dependents: its instability is 1 and it may
be dead code. A module with zero types: abstractness is undefined. A
single-function module: perfectly cohesive and probably too fine-grained. A
shared constant used in 40 places: connascence of meaning with a high degree.

```ts
const instability = (ce: number, ca: number) => (ca + ce === 0 ? NaN : ce / (ca + ce)); // isolated module: undefined
```

Common mistake: optimising metrics on tiny modules. Fix: metrics guide review;
they are not targets.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A stable concrete module plus frequent change (a core domain
model that everyone imports and the business keeps changing): the zone of pain.
Connascence of algorithm plus two languages (the frontend and backend both hash a
token the same way): a change in one silently breaks the other.

Common mistake: duplicating an algorithm across services. Fix: one implementation
or a shared contract test that both sides run.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I measure modularity from the code with tools that compute
dependency graphs and metrics, and run them in CI so coupling cannot creep in
unnoticed.

```js
// fitness function: no module depends on more than 4 others
const graph = await buildImportGraph('src/modules');
for (const [m, deps] of graph) if (deps.size > 4) throw new Error(`${m} depends on ${deps.size} modules`);
```

Common mistake: measuring once during a refactor and never again. Fix: CI checks.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Modularity is about change: a good module boundary is one where most changes stay
inside. The habit is to ask "if this changes, what else must change?" before
placing code.

Common mistake: boundaries by technical role. Fix: boundaries by reason to
change.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Modules are the logical building blocks; components (chapter 8) are their
architectural form; quanta (chapter 7) are their deployable form.

<Flow steps={['classes and functions', 'modules (packages, folders)', 'components (logical architecture)', 'deploy units', 'architecture quanta']} caption="From code to architecture. Coupling at each level shapes the one above." />

Common mistake: splitting into services before modules are clean. Fix: modularise
inside the monolith first.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I compute afferent and efferent coupling, instability and distance from the main
sequence for my capstone's modules with a script, and find one case of strong
connascence across a boundary to refactor. Done when the metrics run in CI and
one cross-module connascence of position or meaning has been weakened in a
commit.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 4. Architectural Characteristics Defined

**Covers:** What makes something an architecture characteristic and its link to system design; a partial list of operational, structural, cloud and cross-cutting characteristics; and why trade-offs lead to the least worst architecture.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Cross-cutting', note: 'security, accessibility, privacy, legal, usability' },
  { name: 'Cloud', note: 'on-demand scalability, elasticity, zone availability, provider portability' },
  { name: 'Structural', note: 'maintainability, extensibility, configurability, portability, upgradeability' },
  { name: 'Operational', note: 'availability, performance, scalability, reliability, recoverability' },
]} caption="The families of architecture characteristics. Each family pulls the design in a different direction." />

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Architectural Characteristics Defined">

<Level name="Beginner">

An architecture characteristic is a non-domain concern that shapes the structure
and is critical to success. It is not a feature: "users can pay" is domain;
"payments are available 99.95 percent of the time" is a characteristic. Examples:
availability, performance, scalability, elasticity, maintainability, security.

```md
Domain requirement: users can export their notes as PDF.
Characteristic it implies: none, unless exports must finish in under 2 seconds for 10k notes (performance, scalability).
```

Common mistake: listing every "-ility" as required. Fix: a characteristic must
influence a structural decision, or it is just a quality goal.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Operational characteristics cover running the system (availability, continuity,
performance, recoverability, reliability, robustness, scalability). Structural
characteristics cover the code (configurability, extensibility, installability,
reusability, localisation, maintainability, portability, upgradeability). Cloud
characteristics cover what cloud platforms make possible or necessary.
Cross-cutting ones touch everything (accessibility, authentication and
authorisation, legal, privacy, security, usability).

```ts
// availability as a number: 99.9% per 30 days
const minutes = 30 * 24 * 60;
console.log((minutes * (1 - 0.999)).toFixed(1), 'minutes of allowed downtime'); // 43.2
```

Common mistake: a vague "highly available". Fix: a number and what it allows in
downtime.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Characteristics interact: improving one often hurts another. Security adds
latency (encryption, checks) and hurts performance. Elasticity requires
statelessness, which can hurt simplicity. So the goal is the least worst
architecture: one that supports the few characteristics that matter most and
accepts weakness in the rest. Supporting every characteristic produces a system
too complex to build.

Common mistake: designing for the generic "best" architecture. Fix: pick the top
characteristics and design for them, accepting the cost elsewhere.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A characteristic that only matters on one day (a sale):
elasticity for that peak, not all year. A legal requirement (data residency):
non-negotiable, it overrides other trade-offs. Accessibility: often missing from
lists, yet cross-cutting and legally required in many places.

Common mistake: leaving accessibility and privacy off the list. Fix: always review
the cross-cutting family.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Scalability plus consistency: scaling writes across nodes
makes strong consistency expensive. Configurability plus testability: every
configuration option doubles the states to test.

```ts
const flags = 12;
console.log(2 ** flags, 'configurations to test'); // 4096
```

Common mistake: adding configuration options freely. Fix: each option is a
testability cost; remove the ones nobody uses.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production characteristics become service level objectives and budgets:
availability targets with error budgets, latency percentiles, recovery time and
recovery point objectives.

```yaml
slo:
  availability: 99.9% over 30d
  latency_p95_ms: 300
  rto_minutes: 30
  rpo_minutes: 5
```

Common mistake: characteristics that never become numbers. Fix: every
operational one gets an SLO.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Least worst, not best. The habit is to make the sacrifice explicit: what this
architecture will be bad at.

Common mistake: promising every quality. Fix: write a "we are not optimising for"
list.

</Level>

Common mistake: letting features alone choose the style. Fix: characteristics

</Level>

### 5. Identifying Architectural Characteristics

**Covers:** Extracting characteristics from domain concerns, composite characteristics, extracting them from requirements, practising with architecture katas (Silicon Sandwiches), explicit and implicit characteristics, and limiting and prioritising them.

<Compare left="Domain concern" right="Characteristics it implies" rows={[
  ['mergers and acquisitions', 'interoperability, scalability, extensibility'],
  ['time to market', 'agility, testability, deployability'],
  ['user satisfaction', 'performance, availability, fault tolerance, testability'],
  ['competitive advantage', 'agility, testability, deployability, scalability'],
  ['time and budget', 'simplicity, feasibility'],
]} caption="My version of translating business language into characteristics. Stakeholders speak the left column; architects design for the right." />

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Identifying Architectural Characteristics">

<Level name="Beginner">

Characteristics come from three places: domain concerns (what the business worries
about), requirements (statements like "10,000 users at launch"), and implicit
knowledge (things nobody says but everyone expects, like security). The skill is
translating business language into characteristics.

```md
Stakeholder: "We must beat competitors to market."
Translation: agility, which decomposes into testability, deployability, modularity.
```

Common mistake: asking stakeholders "which -ilities do you need?". Fix: ask about
their concerns and translate.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Some words are composite: "agility" is not measurable alone; it is made of
testability, deployability and modularity, which are. Katas are practice
exercises: a short brief (users, requirements, context) for which a small group
designs an architecture. The book's first kata, Silicon Sandwiches, is a sandwich
shop's online ordering system, from which characteristics are extracted line by
line.

```md
My paraphrase of a line: user count may grow by orders of magnitude -> scalability, elasticity
My paraphrase of a line: promotions differ per store and region       -> customisability
My paraphrase of a line: hand orders to an outside delivery partner   -> interoperability
```

Common mistake: treating composite words as one characteristic. Fix: decompose
into measurable parts.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Explicit characteristics are in the requirements. Implicit ones are not, but
failing them sinks the project: availability, reliability and security are almost
always implicit. Limiting matters: every characteristic adds complexity, so I keep
a short list. Rather than ranking all of them, picking the top three (in any
order) is easier for stakeholders to agree on.

```md
Candidate list (7): scalability, elasticity, customisability, interoperability, availability, performance, security
Top three: scalability, elasticity, customisability
Implicit, always: security, availability (handled by standard practices, not structure)
```

Common mistake: a list of twelve "must have" characteristics. Fix: top three plus
implicit basics.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A requirement with a number that is a guess ("millions one
day"): design for the near term, keep the option open. A domain concern nobody
says until late (an acquisition). A characteristic that is implicit for one
stakeholder and unknown to another (regulatory audit).

Common mistake: designing for "millions" on day one. Fix: design for the next
order of magnitude and record the path beyond.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Elasticity plus customisability: per-store customisation
logic must also scale on burst, so the customisation engine cannot be a
bottleneck. Interoperability plus security: every integration is another attack
surface.

Common mistake: choosing characteristics independently. Fix: check pairs for
conflicts.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I revisit the list when the business changes: an acquisition, a new
market, a new regulation. Each change can move a characteristic into or out of
the top three, which may change the architecture.

```md
2026-11 review: entering EU market -> privacy and data residency move into the top three.
Consequence: ADR-012, per-region storage.
```

Common mistake: a characteristics list written once. Fix: review it with every
major business change.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Listen for concerns, translate, and limit. The habit is to treat characteristics
as a negotiated short list, not a wish list.

Common mistake: architect-invented characteristics. Fix: trace each to a
stakeholder concern or a requirement line.

</Level>

Common mistake: skipping straight to a style. Fix: no style discussion until the
short list exists.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I run the Silicon Sandwiches kata alone, with a timer, then do the same for my

### 6. Measuring and Governing Architecture Characteristics

**Covers:** Measuring characteristics (operational, structural and process measures), and governing them with fitness functions.

<Flow steps={['characteristic', 'objective measure', 'fitness function (automated check)', 'run in CI or production', 'fail the build or alert']} caption="From a quality word to a check that fails. Governance means this chain exists for each important characteristic." />

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Measuring and Governing Architecture Characteristics">

<Level name="Beginner">

Characteristics must be measurable or they cannot be governed. Operational
measures: response time percentiles, error rates, availability. Structural
measures: cyclomatic complexity, coupling. Process measures: test coverage,
deployment frequency, lead time. A fitness function is any automated check that
tells me whether the architecture still has a characteristic.

```ts
// cyclomatic complexity: 1 + number of decision points
function grade(n: number) {   // 1
  if (n >= 90) return 'A';    // +1
  if (n >= 80) return 'B';    // +1
  return n >= 70 ? 'C' : 'F'; // +1
}                             // complexity 4
```

Common mistake: measuring average response time. Fix: percentiles (p95, p99),
because averages hide the slow tail.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Fitness functions take many forms: unit-test-like checks on code structure
(layer dependencies, cycles), metrics thresholds in CI, performance tests, chaos
experiments in production, and monitors. They can be atomic (one aspect) or
holistic (several together), triggered (on commit) or continual (always running).

```ts
// a layer fitness function in a test
import { test, expect } from 'vitest';
test('domain does not import infrastructure', async () => {
  const graph = await importGraph('src');
  const bad = graph.edges.filter((e) => e.from.startsWith('src/domain') && e.to.startsWith('src/infra'));
  expect(bad).toEqual([]);
});
```

Common mistake: architecture rules enforced by code review only. Fix: an
automated test that fails.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Governance is the ongoing practice of keeping the architecture as intended. The
book's angle is that fitness functions are to architecture what unit tests are
to code. They also cover characteristics that are hard to test directly: cyclic
dependencies (detect cycles in the module graph), distance from the main
sequence (fail if any module exceeds a threshold), and resilience (inject
failures and check the system degrades as designed).

```ts
function hasCycle(g: Map<string, string[]>) {
  const state = new Map<string, 0 | 1 | 2>();
  const visit = (n: string): boolean => {
    if (state.get(n) === 1) return true; if (state.get(n) === 2) return false;
    state.set(n, 1); for (const m of g.get(n) ?? []) if (visit(m)) return true; state.set(n, 2); return false;
  };
  return [...g.keys()].some(visit);
}
```

Common mistake: fitness functions that only run once a quarter. Fix: run cheap
ones on every commit, expensive ones on a schedule.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A performance test on an empty database: fast, meaningless. A
complexity threshold on generated code: always fails. A coverage target of 100
percent: tests written to hit lines, not to check behaviour. A check that is flaky:
people learn to ignore it.

Common mistake: performance tests with unrealistic data. Fix: seed realistic
volumes and distributions.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A strict structural rule plus a legitimate exception: without
an allowlist, the rule is deleted. A production chaos experiment plus no
abort condition: a test becomes an incident.

```ts
const allow = new Set(['src/domain/clock.ts -> src/infra/time.ts']); // documented in ADR-009
const bad = edges.filter((e) => !allow.has(`${e.from} -> ${e.to}`));
```

Common mistake: exceptions with no record. Fix: every allowlist entry points to an
ADR.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production fitness functions are split across the pipeline: structure checks
in CI, load tests before release, monitors and SLO alerts in production, and
periodic chaos experiments with abort conditions.

```yaml
ci: [layer-rules, no-cycles, max-coupling-4, complexity-max-15]
pre-release: [k6-load-p95-under-300ms]
production: [slo-availability-99.9, error-budget-burn-alert]
scheduled: [chaos-kill-one-worker-weekly]
```

Common mistake: governance as a document. Fix: governance as checks in the
pipeline.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

If it matters, measure it; if it is measured, automate the check. The habit is to
ask, for each important characteristic, "what test would fail if we lost this?"

Common mistake: metrics as targets. Fix: metrics as alarms, with judgement on
top.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Production monitors', note: 'SLOs, error budgets, chaos' },
  { name: 'Pre-release tests', note: 'load, performance, security scans' },
  { name: 'CI checks', note: 'layers, cycles, coupling, complexity' },
  { name: 'Editor and lint', note: 'import rules, fast feedback' },
]} caption="Where fitness functions run. Cheaper and faster checks sit lower and run more often." />

Common mistake: all checks at one layer. Fix: place each where it is cheapest
and still meaningful.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write five fitness functions for my capstone: layer rule, no cycles, max
coupling, a p95 load test and an availability SLO alert. Done when the first three
fail a deliberately bad commit in CI, the load test runs before release, and the
SLO alert fires in a test.

</Level>

### 7. The Scope of Architectural Characteristics

**Covers:** Architectural quanta and granularity, synchronous communication and its effect on scope, the impact of scoping on style, the Going Green kata, and scoping in the cloud.

<Figure art={`
   quantum 1                         quantum 2
  +-------------------+   async    +-------------------+
  | orders service    | ---------> | email service     |
  | + orders DB       |   events   | + its own store   |
  +-------------------+            +-------------------+
         | sync call (shares fate)
         v
  +-------------------+
  | pricing service   |   <- pulled INTO quantum 1: if pricing is slow,
  +-------------------+      orders is slow
`} caption="Quanta are drawn by deployability and by synchronous coupling. A synchronous call merges the characteristics of caller and callee." />

<Levels label="Chapter 7, The Scope of Architectural Characteristics">

<Level name="Beginner">

Characteristics apply to parts of a system, not always the whole. An
architecture quantum is an independently deployable piece with high functional
cohesion, together with whatever it is synchronously coupled to (including its
database). A monolith is one quantum. A system of services can have several, each
with its own characteristics.

```md
Quantum: checkout (checkout service + payments service via sync call + orders DB)
Characteristics: availability 99.95, p95 under 400 ms
Quantum: recommendations (async, own store)
Characteristics: elasticity, availability 99.5
```

Common mistake: one set of characteristics for the whole system. Fix: scope them
per quantum.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Synchronous communication merges quanta for the duration of the call: if A calls B
and waits, A's availability and performance depend on B's. Asynchronous
communication (events, queues) decouples them: A can continue while B is slow or
down. Scoping lets a system mix characteristics: a highly scalable order intake
and a less scalable reporting part.

```ts
// availability of a synchronous chain multiplies
const chain = [0.999, 0.999, 0.995];
console.log(chain.reduce((a, b) => a * b).toFixed(4)); // 0.9930 - worse than any one part
```

Common mistake: assuming services are decoupled because they deploy separately.
Fix: check runtime coupling; synchronous calls share fate.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Scope drives style. If all parts need the same characteristics, one quantum (a
monolith) is fine and simpler. If parts need different characteristics, a
distributed style with several quanta can give each part its own. The Going Green
kata (a device recycling business) shows this: the public-facing quoting part and
the internal assessment part have different needs, so they become different
quanta.

The cloud changes scoping: managed services, serverless functions and regional
deployments make it cheap to give one quantum high elasticity while another stays
small, but each managed dependency also joins the quantum it is synchronously
called from.

Common mistake: choosing a distributed style when all parts share
characteristics. Fix: count the distinct characteristic sets first.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A shared database: two services that share one database are
one quantum, however separately they deploy. A synchronous call with a timeout and
a fallback: weaker coupling, but still coupling while it waits. A third-party API
called synchronously: its availability is now part of my quantum.

Common mistake: forgetting the database when drawing quanta. Fix: draw data
stores inside the quantum boundary.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Async messaging plus a user waiting for a result: the user
experience is synchronous even if the plumbing is not. A shared database plus
separate teams: deployments are independent but schema changes are not.

Common mistake: calling a request-reply over a queue "asynchronous". Fix: if the
caller waits, it is synchronous coupling with extra steps.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production quanta show up in incident blast radius: when one part fails, which
others fail with it? Mapping synchronous dependencies and data sharing predicts it.

```ts
// from traces: which services sit in the synchronous path of checkout?
const path = traces.filter((t) => t.root === 'POST /checkout').flatMap((t) => t.spans.filter((s) => s.kind === 'client').map((s) => s.peer));
console.log([...new Set(path)]); // [ 'payments', 'pricing', 'inventory' ]
```

Common mistake: dependency maps drawn from memory. Fix: derive them from traces.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Characteristics have a scope. The habit is to ask "for which part?" whenever
someone says "the system must be scalable".

Common mistake: system-wide characteristics by default. Fix: scope each one.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="One quantum" right="Several quanta" rows={[
  ['one set of characteristics', 'a set per quantum'],
  ['simple deploy and data', 'distributed: network, consistency, operations'],
  ['fits when needs are uniform', 'fits when needs differ by part'],
]} caption="The scope decision. It is the first fork between monolithic and distributed styles." />

Common mistake: splitting by team org chart. Fix: split by characteristic sets,
and align teams to that.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I draw my capstone's quanta, including databases and every synchronous call, and
compute the availability of the checkout chain. Done when the diagram shows quanta
with their characteristics and the availability calculation explains whether I
need async decoupling anywhere.

</Level>

### 8. Component-Based Thinking

**Covers:** Defining logical components, logical versus physical architecture, creating a logical architecture (identify core components, assign user stories, analyse roles and responsibilities and characteristics, restructure), component coupling (static, temporal, the Law of Demeter), and the Going, Going, Gone case study.

<Flow steps={['identify core components', 'assign user stories', 'analyse roles and responsibilities', 'analyse characteristics', 'restructure', 'repeat']} caption="The component discovery loop. Components are found by iteration, not drawn right the first time." />

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Component-Based Thinking">

<Level name="Beginner">

A logical component is a building block of the system's behaviour, such as
"order placement" or "payment processing", usually a namespace or folder in the
code. The logical architecture is the set of components and how they relate; the
physical architecture is how they are deployed (services, databases). Logical
comes first.

```text
src/
  orders/placement/
  orders/history/
  payments/processing/
  notifications/email/
```

Common mistake: starting from the physical (services) before knowing the logical
components. Fix: find components, then decide deployment.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Creating a logical architecture is a loop: guess core components (from major
workflows or actors), assign user stories to them, check each component's roles
and responsibilities (does it do one coherent thing?), check characteristics (does
one part need different scalability?), and restructure. Repeat until stories land
cleanly.

```md
Story: "a bidder sees live bids during an auction"
Assigned to: Bid Streamer
Check: needs elasticity and low latency, unlike Bid Capture's need for reliability -> keep them separate
```

Common mistake: a component called "Manager" or "Utility" that collects
unrelated stories. Fix: a component name should state its one responsibility.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Component coupling has two kinds. Static coupling: one component depends on
another at compile or deploy time (imports, shared contracts). Temporal coupling:
components must run in a particular order or at the same time (a workflow). The
Law of Demeter (talk only to immediate collaborators) reduces knowledge coupling:
a component that orchestrates by reaching through others knows too much.

```ts
// knows too much: reaches through order into customer into address
const zip = order.getCustomer().getAddress().getZip();
// Demeter: ask the immediate collaborator
const zip2 = order.shippingZip();
```

The Going, Going, Gone case study (an online auction) walks through discovering
components by actors and actions, and splitting components where characteristics
differ.

Common mistake: centralising all workflow knowledge in one component. Fix: spread
knowledge so each component knows only its neighbours, or make an orchestrator
explicit and small.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A story that touches five components: either the components are
wrong or it is a workflow that needs an orchestrator. A component with one story:
maybe too fine-grained. A component everyone depends on: a hidden core that will
be hard to change.

Common mistake: forcing every story into one component. Fix: accept that
workflows span components and design the coordination.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Temporal coupling plus a distributed deployment: an order of
operations that was a function call becomes a distributed workflow with failure
in between. Demeter plus performance: hiding traversal behind methods can cause
many small queries (N+1).

Common mistake: splitting temporally coupled components into separate services.
Fix: keep them together, or design the saga explicitly (see the hard-parts book).

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production components should be visible in the code (one folder or package
each) so fitness functions can check their dependencies and size.

```ts
// component size fitness function: flag components above 15% of total code
for (const [c, loc] of locByComponent) if (loc / totalLoc > 0.15) console.warn(`${c} is ${(loc / totalLoc * 100).toFixed(0)}% of the codebase`);
```

Common mistake: components that exist only on a diagram. Fix: map each to a
directory and check it.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Components are discovered, not decreed. The habit is to iterate with real stories
and to restructure without attachment to the first guess.

Common mistake: defending the first diagram. Fix: let stories move the boundaries.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Physical architecture', note: 'deploy units, databases, networks' },
  { name: 'Logical architecture', note: 'components and their relationships' },
  { name: 'User stories and workflows', note: 'what the system must do' },
]} caption="Stories shape the logical architecture, which then maps onto the physical one." />

Common mistake: one-to-one mapping of components to services by default. Fix:
group components into deploy units by characteristics and coupling.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I list twenty user stories for my capstone, run three iterations of the component
loop, and map the final components to folders with a dependency check. Done when
every story lands in one component or a named workflow, and the folders match the

### 9. Foundations

**Covers:** Styles versus patterns, fundamental patterns (including the big ball of mud, unitary and client/server architectures), monolithic versus distributed architectures, and the fallacies of distributed computing along with other distributed considerations.

<Compare left="Monolithic" right="Distributed" rows={[
  ['one deploy unit, one quantum', 'many deploy units, possibly many quanta'],
  ['in-process calls: fast and reliable', 'network calls: slow, can fail, need contracts'],
  ['one transaction across the data', 'distributed transactions or eventual consistency'],
  ['simple to run and debug', 'needs tracing, service discovery, deploy automation'],
]} caption="The first fork among styles. Every distributed benefit is paid for with the fallacies." />

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Foundations">

<Level name="Beginner">

An architecture style names an overall structure (layered, microservices); a
pattern is a smaller reusable solution used inside styles (CQRS, circuit breaker).
The fundamental patterns appear underneath: the big ball of mud (no discernible
structure, everything coupled to everything), unitary (one system on one machine),
and client/server (a split between front end and back end, then into tiers).

Styles are either monolithic (one deploy unit) or distributed (many units
communicating over a network).

Common mistake: calling an unstructured codebase "a monolith" as if that were the
problem. Fix: the problem is the ball of mud; a well-structured monolith is a
valid style.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The fallacies of distributed computing are assumptions that feel true and are
not: the network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is infinite, the network
is secure, the topology never changes, there is one administrator, transport cost
is zero, the network is homogeneous. Each one is a failure I must design for in a
distributed style.

```ts
// fallacy 1 and 2 in code: every remote call needs a timeout and a retry policy
async function getPrice(id: string) {
  const ctrl = new AbortController();
  const t = setTimeout(() => ctrl.abort(), 300);
  try { return await (await fetch(`http://pricing/prices/${id}`, { signal: ctrl.signal })).json(); }
  finally { clearTimeout(t); }
}
```

Common mistake: calling a remote service as if it were a local function. Fix:
timeouts, retries with backoff, and a fallback, on every call.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Beyond the fallacies, distributed styles bring other problems: distributed
logging (a request's story is spread across services, so I need correlation IDs
and tracing), distributed transactions (no single commit; sagas and eventual
consistency instead), and contract maintenance and versioning (every interface is
a public API between teams).

```ts
// propagate a correlation id on every hop
app.use((req, res, next) => { req.id = req.headers['x-request-id'] ?? crypto.randomUUID(); res.setHeader('x-request-id', req.id); next(); });
const call = (url: string, req: { id: string }) => fetch(url, { headers: { 'x-request-id': req.id } });
```

Common mistake: logs per service with no shared ID. Fix: correlation IDs from the
edge, carried on every call and every message.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. Latency: one remote call at 50 ms is fine; twenty in sequence
is a second. Bandwidth: returning a whole customer record when only the name is
needed. Topology change: a hard-coded IP after a redeploy. Security: an internal
endpoint with no authentication because "it is internal".

```ts
const hops = 20, perHopMs = 50;
console.log(hops * perHopMs, 'ms just in network time'); // 1000
```

Common mistake: chatty service-to-service calls. Fix: coarse-grained calls that
return what the caller needs.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Retries plus a non-idempotent operation: a retried "charge
card" charges twice. Timeouts plus a slow but successful downstream: the caller
gives up while the work completes, leaving inconsistent state.

```ts
await fetch('http://payments/charges', { method: 'POST', headers: { 'idempotency-key': orderId }, body });
```

Common mistake: retrying writes without idempotency. Fix: idempotency keys on
every write that may be retried.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the fallacies become operational practices: timeouts and circuit
breakers on every client, tracing across services, mutual TLS or tokens between
services, service discovery, and contract tests in CI.

Common mistake: adopting a distributed style without the operational platform.
Fix: tracing, deployment automation and service discovery come first.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Distribution is a cost paid for specific benefits. The habit is to name the
benefit (independent scaling, fault isolation, team autonomy) before accepting the
fallacies.

Common mistake: distribution as modernisation. Fix: distribution only for a
characteristic a monolith cannot meet.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Figure art={`
  monolithic:   layered | modular monolith | pipeline | microkernel
  distributed:  service-based | event-driven | space-based | orchestration SOA | microservices
                  ^ every one of these pays the eight fallacies
`} caption="The styles of Part II split by deployment. I read each chapter knowing which side it is on." />

Common mistake: comparing styles on features. Fix: compare on characteristics and
on fallacy cost.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the eight fallacies on one card with my mitigation for each in my
capstone, and add correlation IDs, timeouts and idempotency keys to the one remote
call it has. Done when each fallacy has a named mitigation and a retried payment
call cannot charge twice in a test.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 10. Layered Architecture Style

**Covers:** The layered style's topology, layers of isolation, adding layers, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks (including the sinkhole anti-pattern), governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and when to use it.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Presentation', note: 'HTTP handlers, views' },
  { name: 'Business', note: 'rules and workflows' },
  { name: 'Persistence', note: 'repositories, queries' },
  { name: 'Database', note: 'tables' },
]} caption="The classic four layers. Closed layers mean each request goes down one layer at a time." />

<Levels label="Chapter 10, Layered Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

The layered style divides code by technical role: presentation, business,
persistence, database. Each layer has one kind of responsibility, which makes it
familiar, cheap and a natural starting point. It is a technical partitioning: the
top-level structure is by role, not by domain.

```text
src/controllers/orderController.ts
src/services/orderService.ts
src/repositories/orderRepository.ts
```

Common mistake: choosing layered without thinking because frameworks generate it.
Fix: choose it when the system is small, simple, or budget-constrained, and
accept its trade-offs knowingly.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Layers can be closed (a request must pass through each layer below in turn) or
open (it may skip). Closed layers give layers of isolation: a change in
persistence does not affect presentation. Adding a layer (a shared services layer
marked open) can hold common code without letting presentation call it directly.

```ts
// closed: controller -> service -> repository; never controller -> repository
export async function getOrder(req: Req) { return orderService.find(req.params.id); }
```

Common mistake: controllers that query the database directly "for speed". Fix:
keep layers closed and enforce it with a dependency rule.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The architecture sinkhole anti-pattern is when requests pass through layers that
do nothing but forward. Some is normal; if most requests are pass-throughs, the
layers add cost without value, and opening some layers or changing style may be
better. A domain change (add a field to orders) touches every layer, which is the
style's main weakness for agility.

```ts
// sinkhole: the service adds nothing
export const findOrder = (id: string) => orderRepo.find(id);
```

Common mistake: ignoring the sinkhole ratio. Fix: count pass-through methods; if
most are, rethink the structure.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A layer with one method per entity, all pass-through. A cross-
cutting concern (logging) that every layer needs. A single change that touches
four layers and four teams (if teams are split by layer).

Common mistake: teams by layer (UI team, backend team, DBA team). Fix: every
domain change crosses all teams; align teams to domains if change is frequent.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Closed layers plus a performance-critical query: forced
through every layer, it is slow, so someone opens the layer ad hoc. Layered plus
growth: one deploy unit and one database become the scalability limit.

Common mistake: ad hoc layer skipping. Fix: decide which layers are open and
document it.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the style is cheap and simple to deploy and test, but scales and
fails as one unit. Its governance is mostly dependency rules between layer
folders.

```js
// dependency-cruiser: presentation may not import persistence
{ name: 'closed-layers', from: { path: '^src/controllers' }, to: { path: '^src/repositories' }, severity: 'error' }
```

Common mistake: no enforcement, so the layers blur within a year. Fix: dependency
rules in CI.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Layered is a fine default for small systems. The habit is to know when it stops
fitting: frequent domain changes across layers, or parts that need different
scaling.

Common mistake: dismissing it as old-fashioned. Fix: judge it by my
characteristics.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Strong" right="Weak" rows={[
  ['cost, simplicity', 'deployability, agility for domain changes'],
  ['familiar to most developers', 'elasticity and scalability'],
  ['easy to start', 'fault tolerance: one unit fails as one'],
]} caption="My rating summary for layered. The book rates each style; this is my paraphrase of its general shape." />

Common mistake: using layered for a system whose top characteristic is
elasticity. Fix: pick a style rated well for it.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I build a small CRUD service in the layered style with closed layers enforced in
CI, then count sinkhole methods. Done when the dependency rule fails a bad import
and I know the pass-through ratio.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 11. Modular Monolith Architecture Style

**Covers:** The modular monolith's topology, how modules are structured (as monolithic structure or as separate built components), module communication (peer-to-peer and through a mediator), data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and use cases.

<Figure art={`
  +------------------- one deploy unit --------------------+
  |  [ orders ]      [ billing ]      [ notifications ]    |
  |   public API      public API       public API          |
  |   internals       internals        internals           |
  |     |                |                  |              |
  |  orders schema   billing schema   notifications schema |
  +--------------------------------------------------------+
`} caption="Domain-partitioned modules in one deploy unit, each with a public API and, ideally, its own schema." />

<Levels label="Chapter 11, Modular Monolith Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

A modular monolith is one deploy unit whose code is partitioned by domain:
orders, billing, notifications. Each module has a public API and private
internals. It keeps the simplicity of a monolith while giving domain changes a
home. This is the style phase 10 asks me to start my capstone with.

```ts
// src/modules/billing/index.ts is the only import other modules may use
export { createInvoice, type Invoice } from './internal/invoices';
```

Common mistake: domain folders that freely import each other's internals. Fix: a
public entry per module, enforced by lint.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Modules communicate either peer-to-peer (one module calls another's public API)
or through a mediator (a module or orchestrator coordinates workflows across
modules, so modules do not know each other). Modules can live as folders in one
codebase or as separately built packages (a monorepo with workspace packages)
assembled into one deploy.

```ts
// mediator: the checkout workflow knows both; orders and billing do not know each other
export async function checkout(cart: Cart) {
  const order = await orders.place(cart);
  await billing.createInvoice(order.id, order.total);
  await notifications.send(order.customerId, 'order-placed');
}
```

Common mistake: peer-to-peer calls in every direction, recreating the ball of mud
by domain. Fix: limit direction, or use a mediator for workflows.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Data topology: one database is typical, but giving each module its own schema
(and forbidding cross-schema joins) keeps data boundaries clean and makes later
extraction to a service far easier. The main risks: module boundaries erode,
shared code grows into a dumping ground, and one module's load or failure affects
all.

```sql
-- per-module schemas in one Postgres
CREATE SCHEMA orders; CREATE SCHEMA billing;
-- billing reads orders only via the orders module's API, never via a join
```

Common mistake: joining across module tables "just this once". Fix: per-module
schemas with database roles that cannot read others.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A shared "common" module: necessary in small doses, a magnet for
coupling in large ones. A module that needs different scaling (image processing):
it is a candidate to extract. A cross-module report: needs data from every
module, which argues for a read model.

Common mistake: forcing reports through every module's API. Fix: a reporting
read model fed by module events.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Per-module schemas plus a cross-module transaction (place
order and reserve stock atomically): one database can still do it, but it couples
modules; I note it as a seam that would become a saga if split. Mediator plus
growth: the mediator becomes a god module.

Common mistake: assuming module separation solves transactions. Fix: list cross-
module transactions explicitly; they are the hardest extraction points.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production it deploys and runs like any monolith, which is its strength. Its
governance is fitness functions: module import rules, schema access rules, module
size and coupling limits.

```ts
test('modules import each other only via index.ts', async () => {
  const bad = (await importGraph('src/modules')).edges.filter((e) => e.crossModule && !e.to.endsWith('/index.ts'));
  expect(bad).toEqual([]);
});
```

Common mistake: boundaries enforced by convention only. Fix: tests that fail.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Get the boundaries right where they are cheap to move. The habit is to treat a
modular monolith as the default and earn every service extraction with a
characteristic.

Common mistake: microservices first to "get boundaries right". Fix: boundaries are
cheaper to fix inside one deploy unit.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow steps={['modular monolith', 'module with distinct characteristics', 'extract behind same API', 'own data store', 'async events', 'independent service']} caption="The extraction path. Each step is reversible until the data moves." />

Common mistake: extracting modules with no characteristic reason. Fix: extract
only when scaling, fault isolation or team autonomy demands it.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I structure my capstone as a modular monolith with four modules, per-module
schemas and import rules in CI, plus a mediator for the checkout workflow. Done
when cross-module internal imports and cross-schema queries both fail in CI or at
the database level.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 12. Pipeline Architecture Style

**Covers:** The pipeline style's topology (pipes and filters: producers, transformers, testers, consumers), style specifics, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and examples.

<Flow steps={['producer: read events', 'tester: is it a deploy?', 'transformer: add duration', 'consumer: write to metrics']} caption="Pipes and filters. Each filter does one job and passes data one way." />

<Levels label="Chapter 12, Pipeline Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

The pipeline style (pipes and filters) passes data one way through a chain of
filters connected by pipes. Filter kinds: a producer (source), transformer
(changes data), tester (decides whether to pass data on), and consumer (sink).
Unix shell pipes and ETL jobs are the familiar examples.

```sh
cat access.log | grep ' 500 ' | awk '{print $7}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head
```

Common mistake: filters that do several jobs. Fix: one job per filter, so they
compose.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Filters are stateless and independent, so I can add, remove or reorder them.
Pipes are usually one-way and point to point. In Node, streams model this
directly.

```ts
import { pipeline } from 'node:stream/promises';
import { Transform } from 'node:stream';
const onlyErrors = new Transform({ objectMode: true, transform(e, _, cb) { cb(null, e.status >= 500 ? e : undefined); } });
await pipeline(readEvents(), onlyErrors, writeMetrics());
```

Common mistake: filters that share mutable state. Fix: pass all needed data
through the pipe.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Pipeline is monolithic in its basic form (one process), but filters can be
deployed separately with queues as pipes, which gives distributed
characteristics. Risks: error handling (a bad record in the middle stops the
whole pipe), and backpressure (a slow filter must slow its producer, or memory
fills).

```ts
const parse = new Transform({ objectMode: true, transform(line, _, cb) {
  try { cb(null, JSON.parse(line)); } catch { deadLetters.push(line); cb(); } // skip and record, do not crash
} });
```

Common mistake: one malformed record aborting a nightly job. Fix: a dead-letter
path and a count.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. An empty input: the pipeline should finish cleanly and report
zero. A huge record: can overflow a filter's buffer. A tester that drops
everything: silent success with no output. A filter order change that alters
results.

Common mistake: no metrics per filter. Fix: count in and out at each filter.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Distributed filters plus at-least-once queues: duplicates
appear, so filters must be idempotent. A stateful aggregation (counts per minute)
plus a restart: lost state unless checkpointed.

Common mistake: aggregations in a "stateless" pipeline without checkpoints. Fix:
checkpoint state or push aggregation to the sink.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production pipelines are cheap and simple for data processing, integration
and ETL. Governance is per-filter tests and in/out counts monitored for drift.

```ts
metrics.gauge('filter_in', inCount, { filter: 'parse' }); metrics.gauge('filter_out', outCount, { filter: 'parse' });
```

Common mistake: pipelines with no observability between stages. Fix: counts and
dead letters per filter.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Small composable steps. The habit is to spot workflows that are really one-way
transformations and model them as pipelines.

Common mistake: request-response systems forced into pipelines. Fix: pipelines
for one-way data flow.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Strong" right="Weak" rows={[
  ['simplicity, cost', 'fault tolerance in monolithic form'],
  ['modularity via filters', 'elasticity unless filters are distributed'],
  ['testability per filter', 'interactive, two-way workflows'],
]} caption="My summary of where pipeline fits. It shines for data flow, not for conversations." />

Common mistake: monolithic pipeline for bursty load. Fix: queue-backed filters
that scale independently.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I build a log processing pipeline for my capstone with Node streams: parse, filter
errors, enrich, write metrics, with a dead-letter file and per-filter counts.
Done when a malformed line goes to dead letters and the counts reconcile.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 13. Microkernel Architecture Style

**Covers:** The microkernel (plug-in) style's topology: the core system and plug-in components, the registry, contracts, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and examples.

<Figure art={`
           [ plug-in: iPhone assessment ]
                       |
  [ plug-in: tax rules ] -- [ CORE SYSTEM ] -- [ plug-in: Android assessment ]
                       |      + registry
           [ plug-in: export to CSV ]
`} caption="A minimal core with plug-ins registered through a contract. New behaviour is added by adding a plug-in, not by editing the core." />

<Levels label="Chapter 13, Microkernel Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

The microkernel style has a minimal core system and plug-ins that add or vary
behaviour. Editors with extensions, browsers with add-ons, and tax software with
per-jurisdiction rules are examples. Plug-ins are independent of each other and
talk to the core through a contract.

```ts
export interface AssessmentPlugin { device: string; assess(input: DeviceInput): Promise<Quote>; }
const registry = new Map<string, AssessmentPlugin>();
export const register = (p: AssessmentPlugin) => registry.set(p.device, p);
```

Common mistake: plug-ins that call each other. Fix: plug-ins depend only on the
core contract.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The registry tells the core which plug-ins exist and how to reach them. Plug-ins
can be loaded at compile time (in the same build), at runtime (dynamic import
from a folder), or reached remotely (a service). The contract defines input,
output and errors, and should be versioned.

```ts
for (const file of await readdir('plugins')) register((await import(`./plugins/${file}`)).default);
const quote = await registry.get('iphone-15')?.assess(input) ?? defaultAssess(input);
```

Common mistake: a core full of `if (type === ...)` branches. Fix: move each branch
into a plug-in.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Risks: the core grows as features creep in, plug-ins need data the contract does
not expose, and a bad plug-in can crash the core when they run in-process. Data:
the core usually owns the shared database; plug-ins either use the core's data or
keep their own small stores. Remote plug-ins add the fallacies of distributed
computing.

```ts
async function safeAssess(p: AssessmentPlugin, input: DeviceInput) {
  try { return await Promise.race([p.assess(input), timeout(500)]); }
  catch (e) { log.warn({ plugin: p.device, e }); return defaultAssess(input); }
}
```

Common mistake: trusting plug-ins with the core's process and data. Fix: timeouts,
error isolation and a narrow contract.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. Two plug-ins claiming the same key. A plug-in for a contract
version the core no longer supports. No plug-in for an input: the core needs a
default. A plug-in that is slow on one input type.

Common mistake: last registration silently wins. Fix: reject duplicate keys at
registration.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Contract change plus many third-party plug-ins: a breaking
change breaks everyone. Runtime loading plus security: loading arbitrary code is a
supply chain risk.

Common mistake: changing the contract in place. Fix: version it and support the
old one during migration.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the style suits product-based software and systems with many
variants of one rule set. Governance checks that plug-ins depend only on the
contract and that each passes a contract test suite.

```ts
for (const p of registry.values()) test(`${p.device} meets contract`, async () => { expect(await p.assess(sample)).toMatchObject({ amount: expect.any(Number) }); });
```

Common mistake: no contract tests for plug-ins. Fix: one shared suite every
plug-in runs.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Separate the stable core from the variable edges. The habit is to look for
variation that keeps growing (rules per country, per device) and give it a
plug-in seam.

Common mistake: plug-in architecture where there is no variation. Fix: use it only
when the variants keep coming.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Strong" right="Weak" rows={[
  ['extensibility via plug-ins', 'scalability: the core is one unit'],
  ['simplicity and cost', 'fault tolerance if plug-ins run in-process'],
  ['testability per plug-in', 'core changes ripple to all plug-ins'],
]} caption="Where microkernel fits. It is extensibility in a monolithic shape." />

Common mistake: a "core" that is most of the system. Fix: keep the core minimal.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I refactor one switch statement in my capstone (export formats) into a
microkernel with a registry, a versioned contract, a default and a contract test
suite. Done when adding a new format means adding one file and the suite passes.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 14. Service-Based Architecture Style

**Covers:** The service-based style's topology: a user interface, separately deployed coarse-grained domain services and a shared database; topology variants; service design and granularity; database partitioning; cloud considerations; common risks; governance; team considerations; style characteristics; and when to use it.

<Figure art={`
          [ user interface ]
          /       |        \\
  [ orders ]  [ billing ]  [ reporting ]     4 to 12 coarse domain services
          \\       |        /
        [ one shared database ]             partitioned by logical schema
`} caption="Service-based: a few coarse domain services, deployed separately, usually sharing one database." />

<Levels label="Chapter 14, Service-Based Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

Service-based architecture splits a system into a small number of coarse-grained
domain services (often four to twelve), each deployed separately, usually sharing
one database, with a user interface in front. It is a pragmatic middle ground:
some benefits of distribution without microservices' complexity.

```text
services/orders   (deploys alone, owns order workflows)
services/billing  (deploys alone)
services/reporting
db: one Postgres, schemas orders, billing, reporting
```

Common mistake: assuming distributed means many tiny services. Fix: a few coarse
services are often enough.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Because services are coarse, a whole business workflow usually stays inside one
service, so it can use a normal database transaction. Variants: one UI or one UI
per service, one database or a few. Partitioning the database by logical schema
(and libraries of entity definitions per schema) limits the blast radius of
schema changes.

```ts
// inside one coarse service, the workflow is one transaction
await db.transaction(async (tx) => { await tx.insert(orders).values(o); await tx.insert(orderLines).values(lines); });
```

Common mistake: one shared entity library for the whole database. Fix: per-schema
libraries, so a billing change does not redeploy orders.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Granularity trades: coarse services keep transactions local and simplify
orchestration, but each service is bigger to test and deploy. Risks: the shared
database becomes the coupling point; a schema change can affect several services;
services start calling each other synchronously and recreate a distributed
monolith.

```sql
-- schema change governance: who reads this table?
SELECT DISTINCT application_name FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE query ILIKE '%billing.invoices%';
```

Common mistake: unknown table consumers. Fix: per-service database roles so access
is explicit.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A service that needs very different scaling (search): may need
its own database. A cross-service workflow: needs orchestration. A UI that calls
four services per page: chatty, add an API layer.

Common mistake: service-to-service calls for every page. Fix: an API layer or
coarser services.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Shared database plus independent deploys: deploys are
independent, schema migrations are not, so migrations must be backward
compatible. Coarse services plus team growth: two teams in one service step on
each other.

Common mistake: breaking schema migrations. Fix: expand and contract migrations.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the style is a common target when moving off a monolith: extract
domain services, keep the database, then split data only where needed.

```md
Migration plan: 1. carve modules  2. deploy orders and billing separately  3. per-service DB roles  4. split reporting DB (read replica)
```

Common mistake: splitting the database first. Fix: split deploy units first; data
last and only where needed.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Pragmatic distribution. The habit is to consider service-based before
microservices, because it delivers most of the deployability for far less
operational cost.

Common mistake: treating it as a failed microservices. Fix: it is its own style
with its own sweet spot.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Service-based" right="Microservices" rows={[
  ['a handful of coarse services', 'many fine-grained services'],
  ['usually a shared database', 'database per service'],
  ['local ACID transactions in workflows', 'sagas and eventual consistency'],
  ['moderate operational cost', 'high operational cost'],
]} caption="The comparison I would make most often in practice." />

Common mistake: microservices for a team of five. Fix: service-based or modular
monolith.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I sketch a service-based version of my capstone with three services and per-

### 15. Event-Driven Architecture Style

**Covers:** The event-driven style's topology, events versus messages, derived events, triggering extensible events, asynchronous capabilities, broadcast capabilities, event payload (data-based versus key-based), the anemic event, swarm of gnats, error handling, preventing data loss, request-reply, mediated event-driven architecture, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and examples.

<Flow steps={['order placed (initiating event)', 'payment processor', 'payment applied (derived event)', 'fulfilment + notification + analytics', 'order fulfilled (derived event)']} caption="Choreographed events. Each processor reacts and announces what it did; nobody orchestrates." />

<Levels label="Chapter 15, Event-Driven Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

In event-driven architecture, components react to events: something that happened
("order placed"). An event is broadcast, and any interested processor may react.
A message is different: a command or request sent to a specific receiver
("charge this card"). Processors emit derived events after they act, which other
processors pick up.

```ts
bus.subscribe('order.placed', async (e) => {
  await payments.charge(e.orderId, e.total);
  await bus.publish('payment.applied', { orderId: e.orderId, at: new Date().toISOString() });
});
```

Common mistake: naming events as commands ("sendEmail"). Fix: events are past
tense facts; commands are messages to one receiver.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Asynchronous processing improves responsiveness: the user gets an answer when the
order is accepted, not when every step finishes. Broadcasting lets me add a new
processor (analytics) with no change to the producer, which is extensibility.
Even if nobody uses an event yet, emitting it keeps the system extensible.

Payload design matters. Data-based events carry the data processors need;
key-based events carry only an id and make processors call back for data.
Anemic events carry too little to be useful. A swarm of gnats is the opposite
problem: too many tiny events for one business action.

```ts
// data-based: processors need nothing else
{ type: 'order.placed', orderId: 'o1', customerId: 'c9', total: 4200, currency: 'INR', lines: 3 }
// key-based: every consumer calls the orders service, adding load and coupling
{ type: 'order.placed', orderId: 'o1' }
```

Common mistake: key-based events everywhere, so the "decoupled" system calls the
orders service a hundred times per order. Fix: include what consumers need, with a
schema version.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Error handling in async systems: when a processor fails, the producer has moved
on. Options include retries, dead-letter queues, and a workflow event pattern
where a repair processor fixes failed events and resubmits them. Data loss can
happen at three points: publishing (persist before publishing or use an outbox),
in the broker (persistent queues), and consuming (acknowledge only after
processing).

```ts
// outbox: write the event in the same transaction as the state change
await db.transaction(async (tx) => { await tx.insert(orders).values(o); await tx.insert(outbox).values({ type: 'order.placed', payload: o }); });
// a relay publishes outbox rows and marks them sent
```

Request-reply over messaging exists for when a caller needs a response; it is
synchronous coupling carried by queues. Mediated event-driven architecture adds an
orchestrator for workflows that need control, error handling and ordering.

Common mistake: publishing to the broker after the database commit without an
outbox, losing events on crash. Fix: the outbox pattern.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. Duplicate delivery (at-least-once): processors must be
idempotent. Out of order delivery: "order cancelled" before "order placed".
Poison messages that fail every retry. A processor added later that needs
historical events.

```ts
if (await processed.has(e.id)) return; await handle(e); await processed.add(e.id);
```

Common mistake: assuming exactly-once delivery. Fix: idempotent handlers keyed on
event id.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Choreography plus a workflow error: no single place knows the
order is stuck, so the user waits forever. Data-based events plus a schema change:
every consumer must cope with both versions.

Common mistake: choreography for workflows with complex error handling. Fix: a
mediator for those, choreography for simple reactions.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I need event schemas in a registry, consumer lag monitoring,
dead-letter alerting, tracing across async hops, and a replay tool.

```yaml
alerts:
  - consumer_lag{group="fulfilment"} > 1000 for 5m
  - dead_letter_count{queue="payments"} > 0
```

Common mistake: no visibility into consumer lag. Fix: lag and dead-letter metrics
per consumer group.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

React to facts, design for failure in the gaps. The habit is to ask for every
event: what if it is lost, duplicated, late, or out of order?

Common mistake: event-driven for its own sake. Fix: use it where responsiveness,
extensibility or decoupled scaling matter.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Choreography (broker)" right="Mediator" rows={[
  ['highly decoupled, extensible', 'more coupled to the mediator'],
  ['hard to see workflow state', 'workflow state in one place'],
  ['error handling spread out', 'central error handling and recovery'],
  ['scales and performs well', 'mediator can become a bottleneck'],
]} caption="Two topologies of event-driven architecture. Many systems use both, chosen per workflow." />

Common mistake: one topology for all workflows. Fix: choose per workflow.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I add an outbox, an "order.placed" event with a versioned data payload, and two
idempotent consumers with dead-letter handling to my capstone. Done when killing
the process mid-publish loses no events and a duplicated event is processed once.

</Level>

### 16. Space-Based Architecture Style

**Covers:** The space-based style's topology: processing units, virtualised middleware (messaging grid, data grid, processing grid, deployment manager), data pumps, data writers and data readers; data collisions; deployment and caching choices (replicated versus distributed caching, near-cache); example use cases; and style characteristics.

<Figure art={`
  requests --> [ messaging grid ] --> [ PU 1 | cache ] [ PU 2 | cache ] [ PU 3 | cache ]
                                             \\     replicated data grid     /
                                              +----------+----------------+
                                                         | data pumps (async)
                                                    [ data writer ] --> database
                                                    [ data reader ] <-- database (on start)
`} caption="Space-based: processing units with in-memory replicated data, and the database taken off the hot path." />

<Levels label="Chapter 16, Space-Based Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

Space-based architecture removes the database from the synchronous request path,
because the database is usually the bottleneck under extreme load. Processing
units hold application code and an in-memory copy of the data, replicated between
units. Writes go to memory, and data pumps send changes asynchronously to data
writers, which update the database.

```ts
// a processing unit serves from memory and enqueues persistence
async function buyTicket(eventId: string, seat: string) {
  if (grid.get(`seat:${eventId}:${seat}`)) throw new Error('taken');
  grid.set(`seat:${eventId}:${seat}`, { at: Date.now() });
  await pump.send({ type: 'seat.sold', eventId, seat });
}
```

Common mistake: using space-based for normal CRUD traffic. Fix: it is for extreme,
spiky concurrency, like ticket sales.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The virtualised middleware manages it: a messaging grid routes requests, a data
grid replicates data between units, a processing grid orchestrates when several
units are involved, and a deployment manager starts and stops units with load.
Data readers load data into units at start or after a crash.

Data collisions happen when two units update the same key before replication
reaches the other. Their likelihood depends on the number of units, update rate,
cache size and replication latency.

```ts
// a rough sense of collision risk: more units and slower replication, more collisions
const updatesPerSec = 200, replicationMs = 100, units = 5;
console.log(Math.round(updatesPerSec * (replicationMs / 1000) * (units - 1)), 'updates in flight in other units');
```

Common mistake: ignoring replication latency. Fix: measure it, and keep contended
data small or partitioned.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Replicated caching (every unit has the full data) is fast and fault tolerant but
limited by memory and prone to collisions; distributed caching (a central cache
cluster) is consistent but adds a network hop and a single point of failure.
Near-cache (a local front cache over a distributed one) is risky because local
copies diverge between units. The database being asynchronous means data loss is
possible if a pump message is lost, so pumps must be durable.

Common mistake: a near-cache with no invalidation story. Fix: pick replicated or
distributed deliberately; avoid hybrids without consistency rules.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A dataset too large for memory: replicated caching fails. A
cold start after all units die: data readers must reload before traffic. A
report that needs the full database: fine, the database is still updated, just
later.

Common mistake: serving reads from the database "for freshness". Fix: that
reintroduces the bottleneck the style removes.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Burst elasticity plus cold start: new units take time to load
data, so the burst hits before they are ready. Async persistence plus a payment
flow: a crash between memory write and pump means a sold seat never reaches the
database.

Common mistake: space-based for data that must be durable at once. Fix: durable
pumps, or keep those writes synchronous.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the style is complex and costly to build and test, because
reproducing extreme load is hard. It is chosen only for its elasticity and
performance under spikes, usually with a commercial or open source data grid.

Common mistake: load testing at normal traffic. Fix: test the spike shape and the
cold start.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Remove the bottleneck rather than scale it. The habit is to find what limits
scale (often the database) and ask whether it can leave the hot path.

Common mistake: reaching for space-based because it sounds powerful. Fix: it is
the right answer to a rare problem.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Strong" right="Weak" rows={[
  ['elasticity, scalability', 'simplicity, cost'],
  ['performance under spikes', 'testability at real scale'],
  ['database off the hot path', 'consistency and collisions'],
]} caption="Where space-based fits: concert tickets and auctions, not internal tools." />

Common mistake: using it where a cache in front of the database would do. Fix: try
the simpler option first.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I model a ticket sale in memory with two simulated processing units, simulated
replication delay and a durable pump, and count collisions as I vary the delay.
Done when I can show collision counts against replication latency and explain the
cold start problem.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 17. Orchestration-Driven Service-Oriented Architecture

**Covers:** The history and philosophy of orchestration-driven SOA, its topology and taxonomy (business services, enterprise services, application services, infrastructure services, the orchestration engine), message flow, the reuse-and-coupling trade-off, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, and style characteristics.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Business services', note: 'abstract: "execute trade", "place order"' },
  { name: 'Orchestration engine (ESB)', note: 'workflows, mediation, transactions' },
  { name: 'Enterprise services', note: 'shared, reusable: "create customer"' },
  { name: 'Application and infrastructure services', note: 'one-off and cross-cutting' },
]} caption="Orchestration-driven SOA's taxonomy. Reuse was the goal; coupling was the price." />

<Levels label="Chapter 17, Orchestration-Driven Service-Oriented Architecture">

<Level name="Beginner">

Orchestration-driven SOA came from an era when software licences and hardware
were expensive, so enterprises aimed for maximum reuse. It is a technical
partitioning of services into layers: business services, enterprise services
(shared building blocks), application services, infrastructure services, and an
orchestration engine (often an enterprise service bus) that wires them together.

Common mistake: equating SOA with microservices. Fix: SOA aims for reuse through
shared services; microservices aim for decoupling through bounded contexts.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A request enters through a business service, the orchestration engine runs the
workflow, calling enterprise services, which may call application and
infrastructure services. Everything flows through the engine.

```ts
// SOA-style: one shared Customer service used by every business process
const customer = await enterprise.customer.get(id); // used by billing, shipping, marketing, support
```

Common mistake: a shared entity service that every domain uses. Fix: recognise it
as a coupling point; every change ripples everywhere.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The key lesson is reuse versus coupling: the more a service is reused, the more
coupled its consumers are, and the harder it is to change. The orchestration
engine becomes the bottleneck for change and performance, and a single team often
owns it. Data is typically a shared enterprise database, which adds more
coupling.

Common mistake: designing for maximum reuse. Fix: reuse where the thing is truly
stable; duplicate where domains differ.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A tiny change to an enterprise service: many teams must test.
A workflow change: needs the integration team's time. A new business process:
easy if the services fit, painful if not.

Common mistake: one integration team for all workflows. Fix: push workflow
ownership to domain teams.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Distributed transactions plus an orchestration engine: the
engine coordinates them, which works but is slow and fragile. Reuse plus
domain-specific needs: the shared service gains flags for each consumer.

```ts
customerService.get(id, { forBilling: true, includeLoyalty: false, legacyFormat: true });
```

Common mistake: option flags to make one service fit every consumer. Fix: split by
consumer need.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I mostly meet this style as legacy: an ESB with critical workflows
nobody wants to touch. Migration usually extracts domain capabilities one at a
time behind the bus.

Common mistake: a big-bang replacement of the bus. Fix: strangle one workflow at a
time.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Learn from history. The habit is to notice when I am rebuilding SOA's shared
services under another name.

Common mistake: a "platform" team building shared domain services for every
team. Fix: share infrastructure, not domain logic.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Orchestration SOA" right="Microservices" rows={[
  ['technical partitioning', 'domain partitioning'],
  ['maximise reuse', 'maximise decoupling'],
  ['central orchestration engine', 'smart endpoints, dumb pipes'],
  ['shared enterprise data', 'database per service'],
]} caption="Why microservices rejected SOA's goals. The contrast is the main lesson of the chapter." />

Common mistake: treating the comparison as good versus bad. Fix: both were
answers to their era's constraints.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I find one shared module in my capstone that several modules use, list who
depends on it and how often it changes, and decide whether to keep or split it.
Done when the decision is an ADR with the reuse-versus-coupling trade-off stated.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 18. Microservices Architecture

**Covers:** The history of microservices, topology, distribution, bounded contexts (granularity, data isolation), the API layer, operational reuse (sidecars and the service mesh), frontends (monolithic and micro-frontends), communication (choreography and orchestration), transactions and sagas, data topologies, cloud considerations, common risks, governance, team considerations, style characteristics, and examples.

<Figure art={`
          [ API layer ]
      /        |         \\
 [orders|db] [billing|db] [shipping|db]     bounded contexts, database per service
     |           |            |
  [sidecar]   [sidecar]   [sidecar]        operational concerns: mesh
      \\__________|___________/
             service mesh
`} caption="Microservices: bounded contexts with their own data, an API layer in front, and operational reuse through sidecars." />

<Levels label="Chapter 18, Microservices Architecture">

<Level name="Beginner">

Microservices split a system into small services, each modelling one bounded
context (from domain-driven design) with its own data. Services deploy
independently. The style values decoupling over reuse: duplicating code between
services is accepted so that they do not share implementation.

```text
services/orders   -> postgres orders_db
services/billing  -> postgres billing_db
services/shipping -> postgres shipping_db
```

Common mistake: microservices sharing one database. Fix: each owns its data;
others ask through an API or events.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Granularity is the hardest decision. Too fine, and workflows become chatty and
transactions spread. Purpose (one cohesive domain function), transactions (keep
transactional work inside one service) and choreography (avoid services that
must constantly talk) guide boundaries. The API layer sits in front for routing
and cross-cutting concerns but must not hold business logic. Sidecars handle
operational reuse (logging, metrics, mTLS) so each service does not reimplement
them; a service mesh connects the sidecars.

```ts
// granularity smell: one user action needs 7 synchronous hops
console.log(trace.spans.filter((s) => s.kind === 'client').length); // 7
```

Common mistake: one microservice per entity. Fix: one per bounded context, sized
by transactions and communication.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Communication is choreography (services react to events) or orchestration (a
coordinator service drives a workflow). Transactions across services cannot be
ACID; sagas coordinate a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions
on failure. The book's advice is that if a workflow needs distributed
transactions often, the boundaries are probably wrong.

```ts
// orchestrated saga with compensation
try { await orders.create(o); await payments.charge(o); await shipping.schedule(o); }
catch (e) { await payments.refund(o).catch(log); await orders.cancel(o).catch(log); throw e; }
```

Frontends are either a monolithic UI over many services or micro-frontends
aligned with services.

Common mistake: sagas everywhere. Fix: merge services whose operations must be
atomic.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A service with no data: is it a library? Reporting across all
services: needs an analytical data store. A shared library of domain objects:
coupling disguised as reuse. A service owned by two teams.

Common mistake: a shared domain model package. Fix: share only technical
libraries; let each context model its own entities.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Independent deploys plus a breaking contract change: deploys
are independent only with backward-compatible contracts. Fine granularity plus
latency budgets: the chain blows the budget.

Common mistake: versioning by coordinated release. Fix: consumer-driven contract
tests and backward-compatible changes.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production microservices need a platform: automated deploys, a service mesh or
equivalent, tracing, centralised logs, contract tests, and teams that own services
end to end.

```yaml
platform-prereqs: [ci-cd-per-service, tracing, central-logs, mesh-or-mtls, contract-tests, on-call-per-team]
```

Common mistake: adopting the style before the platform. Fix: platform first, or
choose a simpler style.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Decoupling is the goal. The habit is to judge each boundary by whether teams can
change and deploy independently.

Common mistake: counting services as progress. Fix: count independent deploys and
cross-team coordination per change.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Strong" right="Weak" rows={[
  ['deployability, testability per service', 'performance: network hops'],
  ['scalability and elasticity per service', 'cost and operational complexity'],
  ['fault isolation', 'simplicity, distributed transactions'],
  ['evolvability', 'data consistency across services'],
]} caption="My summary rating. The strengths need a platform and the right boundaries to appear." />

Common mistake: microservices for a small team. Fix: a modular monolith until the
team or characteristics demand more.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I design (not build) a microservices version of my capstone with bounded
contexts, data ownership and one saga, and write why I am not building it now.
Done when an ADR records the characteristic and team size that would make me
switch.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 19. Choosing the Appropriate Architecture Style

**Covers:** Shifting fashion in architecture, decision criteria for choosing a style (domain, characteristics, data, organisational factors, process, domain and architecture isomorphism), and worked examples of choosing a monolith or a distributed style for the book's katas.

<Flow steps={['top characteristics and scope', 'how many quanta?', 'monolithic or distributed', 'domain or technical partitioning', 'synchronous or asynchronous', 'data: shared or per quantum', 'style']} caption="The style decision as a sequence of smaller decisions. Each answer narrows the options." />

<Levels label="Chapter 19, Choosing the Appropriate Architecture Style">

<Level name="Beginner">

There is no best style; there is the one that fits these characteristics, this
domain, this data and this organisation. Fashion shifts (yesterday SOA, then
microservices, now modular monoliths again), and each shift is a reaction to the
last one's costs.

```md
Inputs: top three characteristics, number of distinct characteristic sets, team size and skills, data shape, deadline.
Output: a style and the ADR that explains it.
```

Common mistake: choosing the style currently in fashion. Fix: choose from my
inputs.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Decision criteria: the domain (how it partitions), the architecture
characteristics (and how many quanta), data architecture (shared or separate),
organisational factors (cost, team topology, mergers), process (how agile the
team is), and domain/architecture isomorphism (some domains naturally fit a shape,
such as a highly customisable rules engine and microkernel).

```ts
const characteristicSets = ['checkout: availability, p95', 'reports: cost', 'search: elasticity'];
console.log(characteristicSets.length > 1 ? 'consider distributed' : 'monolith likely fine');
```

Common mistake: ignoring team size and skills. Fix: an architecture the team cannot
operate is wrong for them.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The choice is a series of decisions: monolithic or distributed (from the number of
quanta), where data lives, synchronous or asynchronous communication between
services (async by default for decoupling, sync only where needed). The book
applies this to its katas: one gets a modular monolith because its needs are
uniform; another gets a distributed style because parts differ.

Common mistake: deciding sync versus async globally. Fix: per interaction, with
the reason.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A start-up with an unclear domain: a monolith that can evolve.
A system with one extreme part: a monolith plus one extracted service. A merger:
interoperability may dominate everything else.

Common mistake: designing the final architecture on day one. Fix: design for
evolution, starting simple.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A distributed style plus a small team: operational load
exceeds capacity. A monolith plus very different characteristics: one part's
needs are not met, or the whole system is over-engineered for it.

Common mistake: forcing one style on everything. Fix: hybrid styles are normal.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the choice is recorded as an ADR with the criteria, options
considered and the conditions for revisiting. Fitness functions check the
characteristics that drove the choice.

```md
ADR-001 Modular monolith for v1
Drivers: availability 99.9, testability, cost; one team of 3; uniform characteristics.
Rejected: service-based (no distinct sets yet), microservices (ops cost).
Revisit if: search needs elasticity beyond one instance, or team exceeds 8.
```

Common mistake: a style chosen with no recorded criteria. Fix: the ADR.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Choose for now, design for change. The habit is to write what would make me
change the style.

Common mistake: treating the style as permanent. Fix: revisit conditions.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

  ['characteristics are uniform', 'parts need different characteristics'],
  ['small team, tight budget', 'several teams needing autonomy'],
  ['domain still unclear', 'domain boundaries are known'],
]} caption="The first decision summarised. Everything else follows from it." />

Common mistake: skipping the first decision. Fix: decide quanta before styles.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write ADR-001 for my capstone's style using the criteria above, with two
rejected options and revisit conditions. Done when the ADR is in the repo and a

### 20. Architectural Patterns

**Covers:** Recurring patterns that sit inside or across architecture styles, for concerns such as reuse, communication and coordination between parts of a system, and infrastructure.

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Style', note: 'overall shape: modular monolith, microservices' },
  { name: 'Architectural patterns', note: 'recurring solutions inside the shape' },
  { name: 'Design patterns', note: 'code-level: strategy, adapter, observer' },
]} caption="Patterns sit between styles and code. A style can host many patterns." />

<Levels label="Chapter 20, Architectural Patterns">

<Level name="Beginner">

Styles describe the overall shape; patterns solve recurring problems within it.
Examples I use: an orchestrator versus choreography for workflows, a sidecar for
operational concerns, CQRS for separating reads and writes, the outbox for
reliable events, the circuit breaker for failing dependencies.

```ts
// circuit breaker: stop calling a failing dependency for a while
class Breaker { fails = 0; openUntil = 0;
  async call<T>(fn: () => Promise<T>) {
    if (Date.now() < this.openUntil) throw new Error('circuit open');
    try { const r = await fn(); this.fails = 0; return r; }
    catch (e) { if (++this.fails >= 5) this.openUntil = Date.now() + 30_000; throw e; }
  }
}
```

Common mistake: confusing a pattern with a style. Fix: CQRS is a pattern; it can
live in a monolith.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Reuse patterns decide how shared code travels: a shared library (compile-time
coupling, versioned), a shared service (runtime coupling, one deploy), a sidecar
(operational code only), or deliberate duplication. Communication patterns decide
coordination: orchestration gives control and visibility; choreography gives
decoupling and scale.

<Compare left="Shared library" right="Shared service" rows={[
  ['compile-time coupling, versioned', 'runtime coupling, always latest'],
  ['no network call', 'network call, failure modes'],
  ['each consumer upgrades when ready', 'one change affects all at once'],
]} caption="Two ways to reuse code, with opposite failure modes." />

Common mistake: shared services for trivial helpers. Fix: libraries for stable
helpers, services only for shared state or heavy logic.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Patterns combine and conflict. CQRS plus event-driven gives scalable read models,
at the cost of eventual consistency. An orchestrator plus sagas gives visible
workflow state, at the cost of a coupling point. Infrastructure patterns
(sidecars, meshes, API gateways) move operational concerns out of services but add
moving parts.

```ts
// CQRS read model fed by events
bus.subscribe('order.placed', (e) => readDb.upsert('order_summaries', { id: e.orderId, total: e.total, status: 'placed' }));
```

Common mistake: adopting CQRS for a simple CRUD screen. Fix: only where read and
write needs truly differ.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A circuit breaker on a dependency that fails briefly and often:
it trips constantly. A read model that lags: the user sees stale data right after
writing. A sidecar that fails: the service is unreachable even though it is
healthy.

Common mistake: no read-your-writes handling with CQRS. Fix: return the written
state to the writer, or read from the write side briefly after a write.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Retries plus a circuit breaker: retries can trip the breaker
faster; order them deliberately. A shared library plus many services: a security
fix means redeploying all of them.

Common mistake: retries outside the breaker, multiplying load. Fix: retry inside a
budget, breaker outside.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production patterns are recorded in the design doc with where they apply and
why, and each has a fitness function or monitor (breaker state, read-model lag,
outbox backlog).

```yaml
monitors: [breaker_open{dep="pricing"}, readmodel_lag_seconds > 5, outbox_unsent > 100]
```

Common mistake: patterns adopted without monitoring their failure modes. Fix: one
monitor per pattern.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Patterns are named trade-offs. The habit is to state what each one costs before
adding it.

Common mistake: pattern collecting. Fix: each pattern answers a specific
problem in the design doc.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow steps={['problem in the design', 'candidate patterns', 'trade-off of each', 'choose and record', 'monitor its failure mode']} caption="Adding a pattern is a small architecture decision. It deserves the same loop." />

Common mistake: patterns from a blog post. Fix: patterns from my problems.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I add a circuit breaker around my capstone's one external API, an outbox for
events, and a read model for the dashboard, each with a monitor. Done when each
pattern has an entry in the design doc and a monitor that I have seen fire.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 21. Architectural Decisions

**Covers:** Architectural decision anti-patterns (Covering Your Assets, Groundhog Day, Email-Driven Architecture), architecturally significant decisions, and architectural decision records (basic structure, storing ADRs, ADRs as documentation, using ADRs for standards, and ADRs for existing systems).

<Flow steps={['significant decision?', 'write ADR: context, decision, consequences', 'review and accept', 'store with the code', 'link from wiki and PRs', 'supersede, never edit']} caption="The ADR lifecycle. An accepted ADR is changed only by a new ADR that supersedes it." />

<Levels label="Chapter 21, Architectural Decisions">

<Level name="Beginner">

Architecture decisions deserve a record. An architectural decision record (ADR)
is a short document with a title, status, context, decision, and consequences,
plus optional parts such as compliance and notes. The "why" lives in context and
decision; the trade-offs in consequences.

```md
# ADR-003 Use Postgres for all modules
Status: Accepted (2026-10-20)
Context: four modules, one team, relational data, need transactions within a module.
Decision: one Postgres instance, one schema per module, no cross-schema joins.
Consequences: simple ops; a database outage affects all modules; extraction later needs data split.
Compliance: database roles per module; CI test for cross-schema queries.
```

Common mistake: decisions recorded in chat or email. Fix: an ADR in the repo, linked
from the discussion.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Three anti-patterns. Covering Your Assets: avoiding or delaying a decision for fear
of being wrong; fix it by deciding at the last responsible moment and working with
the team on feasibility. Groundhog Day: the same decision is debated again and
again because nobody recorded why; fix it with justification in the ADR, including
business value. Email-Driven Architecture: decisions are lost in email threads;
fix it by notifying people with a link to the single source.

Architecturally significant decisions are those that affect structure,
characteristics, dependencies, interfaces, or construction techniques.

Common mistake: an ADR for every library choice. Fix: ADRs for significant
decisions; a lighter log for the rest.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

ADRs can be stored with the code (docs/adr), in a wiki, or both, with the
repository as the source of truth. They double as documentation: a reader learns
the architecture by reading the ADR log. They can also set standards (all
services use OpenTelemetry), with the reason, which helps adoption. For existing
systems, writing ADRs for past decisions recovers lost reasons.

My own addition here is generative AI in decisions: an LLM can help
draft context, list options or find consequences, but it does not know my
constraints and cannot own the decision.

```sh
ls docs/adr
# 0001-modular-monolith.md  0002-ts-node.md  0003-postgres.md  0004-outbox.md (Superseded by 0007)
```

Common mistake: editing an accepted ADR when the decision changes. Fix: write a
new ADR that supersedes it, so the history stays.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A decision reversed after a week: supersede it, note what was
learned. A decision made under pressure with incomplete information: record what
was unknown. A decision nobody remembers making: write it as a retroactive ADR
with "context reconstructed".

Common mistake: ADRs that only record the winning option. Fix: list rejected
options and why.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A standard ADR plus a justified exception: record the
exception as its own ADR. An LLM-drafted ADR plus a reviewer who skims: plausible
consequences that are wrong for this system get accepted.

Common mistake: accepting generated ADR text without checking each consequence.
Fix: the author verifies each line against the system.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production ADRs are part of review: a pull request that changes architecture
links its ADR, and compliance sections map to fitness functions.

```yaml
# PR template
- [ ] Architecturally significant? If yes, ADR link:
- [ ] Fitness function added or updated for its compliance section
```

Common mistake: compliance sections that nobody checks. Fix: each maps to an
automated test.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The why is the deliverable. The habit is to write the ADR before or as the change
lands, not later.

Common mistake: "we will document it later". Fix: no merge of significant change
without its ADR.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Without ADRs" right="With ADRs" rows={[
  ['decisions re-argued (Groundhog Day)', 'reason is a link away'],
  ['knowledge in people and inboxes', 'knowledge in the repo'],
  ['standards ignored, reasons unknown', 'standards with their justification'],
]} caption="What a decision log changes. It is the cheapest architecture tool there is." />

Common mistake: ADRs in a tool nobody opens. Fix: next to the code.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write five ADRs for my capstone (style, language, database, events, auth), each
with rejected options and compliance, and a PR template that asks for ADR links.
Done when the five ADRs are in docs/adr and one compliance section is enforced by
a test.

</Level>

### 22. Analyzing Architecture Risk

**Covers:** The risk matrix (impact times likelihood), risk assessments, risk filtering, risk storming (identification, consensus, mitigation), and an example of risk storming applied to availability, elasticity and security.

<Figure art={`
              likelihood
               low(1)  med(2)  high(3)
  impact high(3)  3       6       9
  impact med(2)   2       4       6
  impact low(1)   1       2       3
           1-2 low   3-4 medium   6-9 high
`} caption="The risk matrix. Each cell is impact times likelihood; the score sorts what to mitigate first." />

<Levels label="Chapter 22, Analyzing Architecture Risk">

<Level name="Beginner">

Architecture risk is rated on two axes: the impact if something goes wrong and
the likelihood it happens, each low, medium or high (1 to 3). Multiplying gives a
score from 1 to 9. A risk assessment applies this to each area of the system
(components or services) against each characteristic.

```ts
const score = (impact: 1 | 2 | 3, likelihood: 1 | 2 | 3) => impact * likelihood;
const level = (s: number) => (s >= 6 ? 'high' : s >= 3 ? 'medium' : 'low');
console.log(level(score(3, 2))); // high
```

Common mistake: rating only impact. Fix: rate both; a catastrophic but nearly
impossible risk ranks below a likely medium one.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A risk assessment is a table: rows are characteristics (availability,
scalability, data integrity), columns are system areas, cells are scores. Totals
per row and column show where risk concentrates. Filtering (show only high risks)
keeps the meeting focused. Direction (getting better or worse) can be added with
arrows or a trend column.

```md
                 orders  payments  search  reports
availability       4        6        2       1
scalability        2        3        6       1
data integrity     4        9        1       2
```

Common mistake: an unfiltered wall of numbers. Fix: filter to high risks for
discussion.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Risk storming is a collaborative exercise. Identification: each participant rates
risks alone on a diagram, so ratings are independent. Consensus: put all ratings
together and discuss disagreements, which reveal what people know (a developer
knows the database is near its limit). Mitigation: for each high risk, propose
changes and estimate their cost, then negotiate with stakeholders.

The example in the book covers availability, elasticity and security in turn,
one characteristic per session.

Common mistake: rating risks as a group from the start, so the loudest voice
wins. Fix: individual ratings first.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. Everyone rates a risk 1 except one person who rates 9: that
person probably knows something. An area nobody understands: unknown is a risk in
itself. A risk with no affordable mitigation: accept it explicitly and monitor.

Common mistake: averaging away the outlier. Fix: ask the outlier why.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Mitigation plus cost: the fix for availability (multi-region)
may cost more than the risk; stakeholders must choose. Security risks plus a
storming session's visibility: write down sensitive findings carefully.

Common mistake: mitigating every high risk regardless of cost. Fix: present cost
and risk together; let the business decide.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I rerun the assessment after major changes and incidents, and keep
the matrix in the design doc with dates so trends are visible.

```md
2026-11 payments availability: 6 -> 3 after adding retries with idempotency (ADR-008)
```

Common mistake: a one-time assessment. Fix: revisit quarterly and after
incidents.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Make risk visible and shared. The habit is to rate independently, then discuss,
then decide with costs.

Common mistake: the architect alone rating risk. Fix: the whole team, because
knowledge is spread.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow steps={['architecture diagram', 'individual ratings per characteristic', 'consensus on differences', 'mitigations with cost', 'stakeholder decision', 'ADR and fitness function']} caption="A risk storm from diagram to decision. Mitigations become ADRs and checks." />

Common mistake: mitigations that stay as notes. Fix: an ADR and a check for each.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I run a solo risk storm on my capstone for availability and data integrity, then
ask one peer to rate independently and compare. Done when the matrix exists,
disagreements are explained, and the top risk has a costed mitigation recorded.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 23. Diagramming Architecture

**Covers:** Diagramming and presenting architecture: representational consistency, the audience-first approach, diagramming tools, diagramming standards (UML, C4, ArchiMate), and diagram guidelines (titles, lines, shapes, labels, colour, keys).

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Code', note: 'classes, rarely drawn by hand' },
  { name: 'Component', note: 'inside one container' },
  { name: 'Container', note: 'deployable units and data stores' },
  { name: 'Context', note: 'the system, its users and neighbours' },
]} caption="C4's four zoom levels. Representational consistency means always showing where a detailed view sits in the bigger one." />

<Levels label="Chapter 23, Diagramming Architecture">

<Level name="Beginner">

Diagrams communicate architecture, so they must be clear to their audience.
Representational consistency means showing the context before zooming in: a
detailed diagram should indicate which part of the bigger picture it is. Every
diagram needs a title, labels on boxes and lines, and a key for shapes and line
styles.

```md
Title: Capstone, container view (zoom of "Notes system" in the context view)
Key: solid line = synchronous HTTP; dashed = async event; cylinder = data store
```

Common mistake: diagrams with unlabelled arrows. Fix: label every line with what
flows and how.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Standards help. UML has many diagram types but few architects use more than a
handful. C4 gives four zoom levels (context, container, component, code). ArchiMate
covers enterprise architecture. Tools range from sketch tools (fast, for early
ideas) to diagram-as-code (versioned with the repo).

```text
C4Container
  Person(user, "Reader")
  Container(web, "Web app", "Astro")
  Container(api, "API", "Node, TypeScript")
  ContainerDb(db, "Database", "Postgres")
  Rel(user, web, "reads notes", "HTTPS")
  Rel(web, api, "fetches", "JSON/HTTPS")
  Rel(api, db, "reads/writes", "SQL")
```

Common mistake: polishing diagrams too early. Fix: low-fidelity first, so people
feel free to change them (irrational artefact attachment grows with effort).

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Audience first: an executive needs a context view and characteristics; a
developer needs containers and components; an operations engineer needs
deployment. Guidelines: use titles, keep line styles consistent (solid sync,
dashed async), use shapes consistently, label everything, use colour sparingly and
never as the only carrier of meaning, and always add a key.

Common mistake: one diagram for every audience. Fix: a set of views, each with a
named audience.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A diagram that needs a paragraph to explain: too dense, split
it. A diagram with no date or version: is it current? A colour-only distinction:
fails for colour-blind readers and on a black and white printout.

Common mistake: colour as the only meaning. Fix: shape or line style plus colour.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Diagram-as-code plus an evolving system: the diagram can be
checked in CI against the real dependency graph. Presentation slides plus
incremental reveal: building a diagram step by step helps the audience, but each
step must be consistent with the final.

Common mistake: hand-drawn diagrams that drift from code. Fix: generate or check
against code where possible.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I keep context and container diagrams as code next to the ADRs,
dated, and regenerate the dependency view from code.

```sh
npx depcruise src --include-only '^src/modules' --output-type archi | dot -T svg > docs/arch/components.svg
```

Common mistake: diagrams in a slide deck only. Fix: diagrams in the repo.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

A diagram is a conversation tool. The habit is to ask who will read it and what
decision it should help them make.

Common mistake: diagrams for completeness. Fix: diagrams for a question.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Sketch" right="Diagram-as-code" rows={[
  ['fast, low attachment', 'versioned, reviewable'],
  ['good for exploring options', 'good for the current truth'],
  ['drifts unless redrawn', 'can be checked against code'],
]} caption="Two ways to diagram, used at different moments of a design." />

Common mistake: one tool for both. Fix: sketch to explore, code to record.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I draw my capstone's context and container views in C4 as code with titles, keys
and labelled lines, and generate a component view from the code. Done when a
reader who has not seen the project can explain its main flows from the two
diagrams.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 24. Making Teams Effective

**Covers:** Collaboration between architects and developers, constraints and boundaries, architect personalities (control freak, armchair, effective), how much control to exercise, team warning signs (process loss, pluralistic ignorance, diffusion of responsibility), and leveraging checklists (developer code-completion, unit and functional testing, software release).

<Figure art={`
   too tight                    just right                   too loose
  control freak  <--------  effective architect  -------->  armchair architect
  dictates classes            sets boundaries,               draws boxes,
  and libraries               guides and supports            leaves the team alone
`} caption="Architect personalities as a spectrum of control. The right point moves with team experience, size and project complexity." />

<Levels label="Chapter 24, Making Teams Effective">

<Level name="Beginner">

An architect makes the team effective by setting appropriate boundaries: tight
enough to protect the architecture, loose enough to let developers design. Too
tight is the control freak (dictating every library and class); too loose is the
armchair architect (drawing boxes and disappearing). The effective architect
guides, explains and stays available.

Common mistake: choosing the team's date library for them. Fix: constrain the
decisions that affect the architecture; leave the rest.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

How much control depends on factors: team familiarity (a new team needs more
guidance), team size, overall experience, project complexity, and project duration.
I can score each factor and lean towards more or less control.

```ts
// a rough lean: positive means more control, negative less
const factors = { newTeam: +1, bigTeam: +1, mostlyJunior: +1, complex: +1, shortProject: -1 };
console.log(Object.values(factors).reduce((a, b) => a + b)); // 3: more hands-on guidance
```

Common mistake: the same level of control for every team. Fix: assess per team
and per phase.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Warning signs that a team is struggling. Process loss: adding people makes the
team slower because coordination grows (merge conflicts are a symptom).
Pluralistic ignorance: everyone privately disagrees but assumes others agree, so
nobody speaks. Diffusion of responsibility: in a large team, everyone assumes
someone else will handle it. Smaller teams and explicit ownership help with all
three.

```sh
git log --since=30.days --merges --grep='conflict' --oneline | wc -l   # a rising count hints at process loss
```

Common mistake: adding developers to a late project. Fix: look for process loss
first; split work so people do not step on each other.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A senior team: little control needed, mostly context. A team
new to the domain: more guidance on domain, less on code. A team of one: the
architect may also be the developer, and the risk is skipping the documentation.

Common mistake: leaving a junior team alone "to learn". Fix: tighter boundaries at
first, loosened as they grow.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Checklists plus experienced developers: too many items and
they stop reading. Pluralistic ignorance plus a dominant architect: nobody
contradicts the architect even when wrong.

Common mistake: checklists of everything. Fix: only items that are often
forgotten and not automated.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Checklists work when they are short and target what gets missed: a
code-completion checklist (no hard-coded secrets, error handling, logs with IDs),
a testing checklist (edge cases, failure paths), a release checklist (migrations,
feature flags, rollback). Anything that can be automated should move to CI and off
the checklist.

```md
Release checklist
- [ ] migration is backward compatible (expand, then contract)
- [ ] feature flag default is off
- [ ] rollback command tested in staging
```

Common mistake: checklists that duplicate what linters check. Fix: automate those
and remove them.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

The architect's success is the team's success. The habit is to ask what the team
needs from me this month: more guidance, more context, or more space.

Common mistake: measuring myself by the diagrams I produce. Fix: measure by the
team's flow and the architecture's health.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Layers layers={[
  { name: 'Architecture boundaries', note: 'set by architect: style, data, interfaces' },
  { name: 'Design space', note: 'owned by developers: classes, libraries within rules' },
  { name: 'Checklists and fitness functions', note: 'guard what people forget' },
]} caption="Where control sits. Boundaries are tight at the edges and the space inside belongs to the team." />

Common mistake: no clear design space. Fix: state what is the team's to decide.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I write the boundaries for my capstone (what any contributor may decide, what
needs an ADR) and a five-item release checklist. Done when a new contributor could
read one page and know what they can decide alone.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 25. Negotiation and Leadership Skills

**Covers:** Negotiation and facilitation (with business stakeholders, other architects, developers), the software architect as a leader (the 4 Cs of architecture: communication, collaboration, clarity, conciseness), being pragmatic yet visionary, leading teams by example, and integrating with the development team.

<Compare left="Weak negotiating" right="Strong negotiating" rows={[
  ['"we need five nines"', '"five nines allows 5 minutes down a year; three nines allows 9 hours; which fits?"'],
  ['argue from authority', 'demonstrate with a proof of concept'],
  ['win the argument', 'reach agreement the team will act on'],
  ['dictate to developers', 'explain the why, invite the how'],
]} caption="Negotiation moves from opinion to evidence and from winning to agreement." />

<Levels label="Chapter 25, Negotiation and Leadership Skills">

<Level name="Beginner">

Architects must negotiate, because decisions cost people something. With
business stakeholders, translate their words into numbers and options ("five
nines" becomes minutes of downtime and its cost). With other architects,
demonstrate rather than argue. With developers, explain why, and let them shape
how.

```ts
const downtimeMinutesPerYear = (nines: number) => 365 * 24 * 60 * 10 ** -nines;
console.log(downtimeMinutesPerYear(5).toFixed(1), downtimeMinutesPerYear(3).toFixed(0)); // 5.3 526
```

Common mistake: accepting "as available as possible". Fix: show what each level
costs and allows.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Negotiation techniques: pay attention to buzzwords and what concern sits behind
them; gather information before negotiating; when all else fails, state things in
terms of cost and time; divide and conquer (does the whole system need it, or
one part?); and demonstration defeats discussion. With developers, give the
justification and, where possible, let them arrive at the solution themselves.

Common mistake: winning an argument with developers by authority. Fix: they will
comply reluctantly; explain and let them own the solution.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Leadership is a large part of the role. The 4 Cs: communication, collaboration,
clarity and conciseness. Being pragmatic yet visionary means planning for the
future while staying within budget, time, skills and today's constraints.
Leading by example means earning respect through competence and attitude rather
than title: using people's names, turning requests into favours where
appropriate, and asking questions instead of issuing commands.

Common mistake: complexity to look clever. Fix: clarity and conciseness; the
simplest correct explanation wins.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A stakeholder who will not move: divide the requirement and
give them the part that matters. A fellow architect with a stronger title: a
proof of concept with numbers. A developer who disagrees publicly: discuss it, and
take the details offline.

Common mistake: arguing in a big meeting. Fix: gather information one to one
first.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Visionary plus pragmatic: the long-term design may cost too
much now; build towards it in steps. Meetings plus integration with the team: an
architect in meetings all day is not with the team.

Common mistake: accepting every meeting invite. Fix: protect time with the team;
ask for agendas, delegate where possible.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production negotiation produces artefacts: options with costs, a proof of
concept, a short decision memo, and an ADR. Integration with the team shows as
regular time pairing, reviewing and unblocking.

```md
Options for search availability
A. single instance: 99.5%, current cost
B. two instances + failover: 99.9%, +40% cost, 2 weeks
C. managed service: 99.95%, +120% cost, 1 week
Recommendation: B. Decision needed by Fri.
```

Common mistake: presenting one option. Fix: three options with costs and a
recommendation.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Influence without authority. The habit is to arrive with data, options and a
recommendation, and to listen for the concern behind the request.

Common mistake: treating stakeholders as obstacles. Fix: treat them as sources of
constraints I need.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Flow steps={['hear the request', 'find the concern behind it', 'gather information', 'options with cost and time', 'demonstrate', 'agree and record']} caption="A negotiation loop that ends in a decision the team acts on." />

Common mistake: skipping to options without the concern. Fix: ask why twice.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I take one capstone requirement I would push back on and write the three-option
memo with costs, then explain it to a peer in five minutes. Done when the memo
exists and the peer can restate the trade-off.

</Level>

</Levels>

### 26. Architectural Intersections

**Covers:** How architecture intersects with other parts of the organisation and technology: implementation, infrastructure and operations, data topologies, engineering practices, team topologies, systems integration, the enterprise, and the business environment.

<Figure art={`
                 [ data topologies ]
                        |
 [ engineering ] -- [ ARCHITECTURE ] -- [ infrastructure / operations ]
   practices            |       \\
                 [ team topologies ] [ business environment, enterprise ]
`} caption="Architecture does not live alone. Each intersection must be aligned, or it undermines the design." />

<Levels label="Chapter 26, Architectural Intersections">

<Level name="Beginner">

An architecture only works if the things around it support it. The
implementation must follow it (fitness functions check). Infrastructure must be
able to run it. The data topology must match the deploy topology. Team structure
must match the architecture, or communication patterns will reshape it.

```md
Architecture: modular monolith, 4 modules
Team: one team of 3 -> fine
Data: one Postgres, schema per module -> matches
Infra: one container + managed DB -> matches
```

Common mistake: microservices on a team structured by technical layer. Fix: align
teams with the architecture's partitions.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Data topology mismatches are common: services that deploy separately but share a
monolithic database are still coupled. Engineering practices matter: a style that
needs independent deploys needs CI/CD per unit. Team topologies (stream-aligned,
platform, enabling, complicated-subsystem teams) match naturally to
domain-partitioned styles.

```ts
// a mismatch detector: do any two services write to the same table?
const writers = new Map<string, Set<string>>();
for (const q of auditLog) if (q.write) (writers.get(q.table) ?? writers.set(q.table, new Set()).get(q.table)!).add(q.service);
for (const [t, s] of writers) if (s.size > 1) console.log(t, [...s]);
```

Common mistake: drawing service boundaries without checking data writes. Fix:
one writer per table.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Wider intersections: systems integration (how the system talks to others in the
enterprise), enterprise standards (security, technology choices), and the
business environment (growth, cost pressure, regulation). I add generative AI to
my own list, both as a component with its own characteristics (non-determinism,
cost) and as a tool I use. Each can force an architecture change: a cost
squeeze can make a distributed style unaffordable.

Common mistake: ignoring the business environment. Fix: review it when choosing
or revisiting the style.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. An enterprise standard that conflicts with a characteristic
(mandated database that cannot scale writes). Infrastructure that cannot run
containers. A regulation that forbids sending data to a hosted LLM.

Common mistake: discovering a constraint after building. Fix: check intersections
at design time.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. A generative AI component plus a strict latency
characteristic: the model call may dominate the budget. Team reorganisation plus
architecture: changing teams changes communication paths and eventually the
structure.

Common mistake: reorganising teams without considering the architecture. Fix:
plan both together.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production I keep an intersections checklist in the design doc: data, infra,
ops, teams, integration, enterprise standards, business, AI. Each gets a one-line
status.

```md
Intersections (2026-10)
data: matches | infra: matches | teams: one team | integration: one email API | enterprise: n/a | AI: summariser, budget 800 ms
```

Common mistake: architecture docs that ignore teams and data. Fix: an
intersections section.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Architecture is a system of systems. The habit is to look sideways at every
decision: what does this need from data, teams and infrastructure?

Common mistake: perfect architecture on paper. Fix: architecture that its
surroundings can support.

</Level>

### 27. The Laws of Software Architecture, Revisited

**Covers:** A return to the laws introduced in chapter 1, now applied with everything the book has built up, and closing advice on practising architecture.

<Flow steps={['find the trade-off', 'analyse it in context', 'decide the least worst', 'record why', 'practise on katas']} caption="The laws as a working loop. The book ends where it started, with trade-offs and reasons." />

<Levels label="Chapter 27, The Laws of Software Architecture, Revisited">

<Level name="Beginner">

The book closes by returning to its laws. Everything in architecture is a
trade-off; if I think I found something without one, I have not looked hard
enough. And why is more important than how. Two familiar decisions I use to
illustrate:
shared library or shared service for common code, and synchronous or
asynchronous communication.

```md
Shared library: + no network, + versioned per consumer; - redeploy all to patch, - language lock-in
Shared service: + one place to change; - runtime coupling, - network failure modes
```

Common mistake: treating these as solved questions. Fix: answer them per
context.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

My reading of the first law in practice: trade-offs only make sense in context. A
shared service is right when the shared code changes often and must be
consistent; a library is right when it is stable and performance matters. The
same goes for sync versus async: sync is simpler and gives immediate consistency;
async decouples and absorbs load.

```ts
const choose = (ctx: { changesOften: boolean; latencyCritical: boolean }) =>
  ctx.changesOften && !ctx.latencyCritical ? 'shared service' : 'shared library';
console.log(choose({ changesOften: false, latencyCritical: true })); // shared library
```

Common mistake: a rule of thumb without context. Fix: write the context next to
the rule.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

The second law means documentation of reasons outlives implementation details.
In practice: ADRs over diagrams, trade-off tables over feature lists, and
explanations that let someone else make the next decision. Practice builds the
skill, so I keep practising: katas, reading other systems' decisions, and reflecting on my own.

Common mistake: architecture skill as knowledge of styles. Fix: skill is trade-off
analysis, built by practice.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

One boundary each. A decision with one option (a regulator mandates it): still
write why, because the reason matters when the rule changes. A trade-off with
unknown sides: record the unknown and the experiment to resolve it.

Common mistake: "no choice, so no ADR". Fix: record the constraint.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Two boundaries meet. Async messaging plus a shared library for event schemas: two
trade-offs stacked; schema changes need library upgrades in every consumer. A
service to avoid library redeploys plus a latency budget: the trade-off flips.

Common mistake: analysing decisions in isolation. Fix: check how they interact.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production the laws show up as culture: reviews ask "what did we trade?" and
"why?", and decisions have revisit conditions.

```yaml
# PR template additions
- What did this change trade away?
- Why this option over the next best?
```

Common mistake: reviews that only check code style. Fix: two trade-off questions
in the template.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Trade-offs and reasons, always. The habit is to finish every design discussion by
stating the trade-off and the why in one sentence each.

Common mistake: certainty. Fix: least worst, with reasons.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

<Compare left="Synchronous" right="Asynchronous" rows={[
  ['simple, immediate answer', 'decoupled, absorbs bursts'],
  ['caller waits, shares fate', 'eventual consistency, harder errors'],
  ['easy to trace', 'needs async tracing and dead letters'],
]} caption="The second worked trade-off. Neither is default; the context decides." />

Common mistake: async everywhere because it is "scalable". Fix: async where its
benefits are needed.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I review my capstone's ADRs and add a one-sentence trade-off and why to any that
lack them, then do one kata a month for the next three months. Done when every
ADR has both sentences and the first kata's design is in my notes with its
trade-offs.

</Level>

</Levels>

## Discussion questions appendix

The book ends with discussion questions for each chapter. I use them as a
self-test after each week: if I cannot answer a chapter's questions out loud with
an example from my capstone, I reread that chapter's levels above.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Architecture is choosing a style."** The style is one of four dimensions;
  characteristics, decisions and principles matter as much.
- **"Microservices are the goal."** They are one style with heavy costs; a
  modular monolith is often the least worst choice.
- **"Characteristics are a wish list."** Keep the top few, scoped per quantum,
  and accept weakness elsewhere.
- **"Fitness functions are tests for code."** They are checks for the
  architecture's characteristics, from CI to production.
- **"Soft skills are optional."** Half of Part III is about people, because
  decisions only matter if they are adopted.
