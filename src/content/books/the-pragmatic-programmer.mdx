## Why this book

This is the primary book for Phase 0 of the <a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>,
and it comes back in Phase 11, when the subject is judgment. It does not teach
computer science. It teaches the habits that make the next fourteen months
stick: plain text, version control, a debugger, a daybook, failing loudly, and
taking responsibility for what I ship. The
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/#strategy-0')}>Phase 0 strategy</a>
leans on it directly.

The other plans cite it too, which is a good sign: it is the one book that is
about the developer rather than about a technology.

## How I would learn it

Not cover to cover. In Phase 0 I read chapters 1, 3 and 4, because they are
about the setup and the study system: ownership, the tools, and failing loudly.
The rest I read one topic a week, alongside whatever phase I am in, and only
when that phase gives me a place to apply it.

- **One topic, one change.** After each topic I make one concrete change to my
  study repository and write the commit message as the lesson. Adding a `make
  check` target that runs the tests, the warnings and the sanitizers is the
  starter-kit topic applied.
- **Do the exercises in writing.** Most topics end with short exercises; I
  answer them in the daybook before reading the possible answers at the back.
- **Keep a disagreement list.** Some advice depends on context. When I
  disagree, I write down why and what evidence would change my mind. That is
  the pragmatism the book is named for.
- **Re-read in Phase 11.** The second read is where it pays off.

I know I am done with the first pass when the Phase 0 gate is met using the
habits from chapters 1, 3 and 4, without a tutorial open.

## The chapters


### 1. A Pragmatic Philosophy

**Covers:** Topics 1–7: owning your career (It's Your Life), taking responsibility instead
of making excuses (The Cat Ate My Source Code), broken windows and decay
(Software Entropy), catalysing change and noticing slow decline (Stone Soup and
Boiled Frogs), quality as a requirement you negotiate (Good-Enough Software),
learning as an investment portfolio (Your Knowledge Portfolio), and
communicating as part of the job (Communicate!).

<Levels label="Chapter 1, A Pragmatic Philosophy">

<Level name="Beginner">

The smallest version: when something breaks, offer options, not excuses. Fix a
small mess when you see it, before it signals that mess is normal. Learn one
new thing on purpose every week.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

In a real codebase these combine. A broken window (a failing test someone
skipped, a `// TODO` from last year) lowers everyone's bar. Good-enough
software means agreeing the quality bar with the people who use it, not
polishing forever and not shipping junk. The knowledge portfolio is diversified:
some safe investments (the language I use daily), some speculative (C, proofs).

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Underneath, the chapter is about feedback loops. Entropy grows when nobody owns
the feedback. Boiled frogs are what happens when change is too slow to trigger
an alarm, so you need measurements, not feelings. Communication is a design
problem: know the audience, choose the moment, choose the format, and make it
two-way.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Responsibility has limits: you can own your work without owning a decision you
had no say in. Say so, in writing. "Good enough" depends on the domain: a
landing page and a payment ledger have different bars.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Broken windows meet good-enough software: a deliberately unpolished prototype
is not a broken window if it is labelled and has an expiry date. An unlabelled
one is.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In production, entropy shows up as flaky tests, ignored alerts and warning
spam. The pragmatic fix is a rule: zero warnings, every alert actionable, every
flaky test either fixed or quarantined with an owner.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Ownership plus curiosity. The study log for this roadmap is the knowledge
portfolio made visible.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

At system scale, broken windows are missing runbooks, undocumented ports and
dead feature flags. Good-enough becomes service-level objectives: an explicit,
negotiated quality bar.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I set up the study log and a weekly portfolio entry in Phase 0. The test: after
a month, can I point to four things I learned and one broken window I fixed? Done when the study log has a month of weekly entries and one broken window I fixed.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 2. A Pragmatic Approach

**Covers:** Topics 8–15: good design is easier to change (The Essence of Good Design); every
piece of knowledge has one authoritative representation (DRY); components that
do not affect each other (Orthogonality); no decision is final (Reversibility);
building a thin end-to-end skeleton first (Tracer Bullets); throwaway
experiments to answer one question (Prototypes and Post-it Notes); code that
speaks the problem's vocabulary (Domain Languages); and giving estimates with
units and uncertainty (Estimating).

<Levels label="Chapter 2, A Pragmatic Approach">

<Level name="Beginner">

Ask "does this make the code easier to change?" before any design choice. Do
not keep the same fact in two places. Build the thinnest version that runs end
to end, then thicken it.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

DRY is about knowledge, not text. Two functions that look alike but encode
different rules should stay separate. Orthogonality shows up as: can I change
the CSV parser without touching the statistics? A tracer bullet for the Phase 1
CLI is "read file, count one thing, print it, exit 0" with a test, before any
validation.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Prototypes are disposable by contract; tracer bullets are kept. Mixing them up
is how throwaway code reaches production. Estimates come from a model of the
work (break it down, estimate the parts, track your accuracy), and the unit you
give ("about 3 weeks" versus "15 working days") signals the precision you claim.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

DRY taken too far couples unrelated code through a shared helper. Reversibility
taken too far adds abstraction layers for changes that never come.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Orthogonality versus DRY: removing duplication can create a dependency between
two modules that were independent. Sometimes the duplicate is cheaper.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Configuration, schemas and documentation are where DRY usually breaks: the API
doc says one thing, the validator another. Generate one from the other.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Every decision is a bet that can be revisited. Keep the cost of revisiting low.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Tracer bullets at system scale are walking skeletons: one request through every
layer, deployed, before the features. Reversibility is why you put a vendor
behind an interface.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I apply this to the Phase 1 CLI: tracer bullet first, then one orthogonal module
per concern. I estimate each phase and record actual time in the log, to
calibrate. Done when the CLI's history shows the tracer bullet first and a later change that touched one module.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 3. The Basic Tools

**Covers:** Topics 16–22: keep knowledge in plain text; master the shell; know your editor
well; put everything in version control; debug methodically, not by guessing;
use text-manipulation tools and scripts; and keep an engineering daybook.

<Levels label="Chapter 3, The Basic Tools">

<Level name="Beginner">

Use a terminal every day. Commit small and often. When something breaks,
reproduce it before trying to fix it. Write down what you did in a dated log.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Combine tools: `grep -rn "malloc" src | wc -l`, `git bisect` to find the commit
that broke a test, a `Makefile` that builds and tests. The daybook records the
question, the attempt and the result, so the next debugging session starts from
evidence.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Debugging as a method: reproduce, read the error, binary-search the cause, write
a failing test, fix, explain why it happened. Plain text wins because every
tool can read it, diff it and version it, decades later.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Plain text has limits: binary data, large datasets and anything that needs a
schema. Then use a text format with a schema (JSON with validation, CSV with a
header contract).

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A bug that disappears under the debugger (timing, uninitialised memory) needs a
different tool: logs, sanitizers, or a core dump. That is exactly Phase 1's gate.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Shell scripts in production need `set -euo pipefail`, quoting, and exit codes
checked. Version control includes the build scripts, the config and the
infrastructure, not just the source.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Tools are leverage. An hour learning the debugger pays back every week.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Plain-text config, structured logs and reproducible builds are this chapter at
system scale.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

This is the Phase 0 checklist: shell, editor, Git, debugger, daybook. The gate
is that I can do each without a tutorial. Done when I can bisect a bug, script a text transformation and write the daybook entry without looking anything up.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 4. Pragmatic Paranoia

**Covers:** Topics 23–27: state what a function requires and guarantees (Design by
Contract); crash early rather than continue in a bad state (Dead Programs Tell
No Lies); check the things that "can't happen" (Assertive Programming); the code
that acquires a resource releases it (How to Balance Resources); and take small
steps so feedback arrives before you go too far (Don't Outrun Your Headlights).

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Pragmatic Paranoia">

<Level name="Beginner">

Check inputs at the boundary. If something impossible happens, stop with a clear
message instead of carrying on. Every `fopen` gets an `fclose`; every `malloc`
a `free`.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A contract has preconditions (the caller's job), postconditions (the function's
job) and invariants (always true). In TypeScript, types encode some of the
contract; runtime checks cover the rest. In C, `assert` documents and checks
internal assumptions.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Resource balance is about ownership and lifetime: the function that allocates
should free, or ownership must be transferred explicitly. Nested resources are
released in reverse order. This is the exact gate of Phase 1: explain the
lifetime of every allocation.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Assertions compiled out in release builds (`NDEBUG`) must not have side
effects. Validating user input is not an assertion; it is error handling.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Crashing early meets resource balance: a crash between acquire and release
leaks a lock file or a temp file. Design so the next run can recover.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

In a service, "crash early" means fail the request, log context, and let the
supervisor restart the process, not swallow the error and return 200.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Assume your code will be wrong and design it to tell you quickly.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Contracts become API schemas; crash-early becomes health checks and circuit
breakers; small steps become small deploys behind flags.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I add asserts, exit codes and resource pairs to the Phase 0 baseline programs,
and check them with ASan and Valgrind in Phase 1. Done when ASan and Valgrind are clean and every C function has its contract written above it.
</Level>

</Levels>

```c
/* Every acquire has a release, in reverse order, on every path. */
int count_lines(const char *path, long *out) {
    FILE *fp = fopen(path, "r");
    if (!fp) return -1;                 /* precondition failed: report, do not crash */
    char *buf = malloc(4096);
    if (!buf) { fclose(fp); return -1; }
    long n = 0;
    while (fgets(buf, 4096, fp)) n++;
    int err = ferror(fp);
    free(buf);                          /* released in reverse order */
    fclose(fp);
    if (err) return -1;
    *out = n;                           /* postcondition: *out set only on success */
    return 0;
}
```

### 5. Bend, or Break

**Covers:** Topics 28–32: reduce coupling between modules (Decoupling); handle events with
finite state machines, observers, pub/sub and streams (Juggling the Real World);
think of programs as pipelines of transformations (Transforming Programming);
prefer interfaces, delegation and mixins to inheritance (Inheritance Tax); and
keep environment-specific values out of code (Configuration).

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Bend, or Break">

<Level name="Beginner">

A function should not reach through three objects to get what it needs; ask for
it directly. Put settings in config, not constants.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Transforming programming: the CLI is `read → split → normalise → count → sort →
print`. Each step is a pure function, easy to test. State machines make event
handling explicit: every state and every transition is listed.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Inheritance couples a child to its parent's implementation, not just its
interface. Composition and interfaces keep the coupling at the contract.
Configuration as a service means config can change without a redeploy, which is
also a new failure mode.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

A pipeline with an error in the middle needs a way to carry the error: a Result
type, an exception, or a sentinel. Decide once.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Event-driven code plus shared configuration: a config change mid-stream can
leave events processed under two rule sets.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Config needs validation at startup and defaults that fail safe. Secrets are
config with stricter handling.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Coupling is the cost you pay later. Ask what a change would touch.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Pub/sub, queues and streams are the same decoupling at service scale; the
twelve-factor config rule is this chapter applied to deployment.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I revisit it in Phases 5 to 10. The test: refactor one module of a phase project
so a change touches one file. Done when a configuration change needs no code change and one refactor touches one file.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 6. Concurrency

**Covers:** Topics 33–36: find the work that does not need to happen in order (Breaking
Temporal Coupling); shared mutable state is where concurrent bugs live (Shared
State Is Incorrect State); isolate state inside actors or processes that only
exchange messages (Actors and Processes); and coordinate loosely through a
shared knowledge space (Blackboards).

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Concurrency">

<Level name="Beginner">

Concurrency is about structure (things that can happen independently);
parallelism is about running them at the same time. Two threads writing the same
variable without coordination is a bug even if the tests pass.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Activity diagrams reveal which steps can overlap. A check-then-act sequence
("if stock greater than 0, decrement") is a race unless it is atomic.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Actors remove shared memory by giving each piece of state one owner that
processes messages one at a time. Semaphores and mutexes protect shared state
but introduce deadlock risk.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

JavaScript's single thread does not prevent races: two `await`s can interleave
between a read and a write.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Retries plus shared state: a retried message processed twice by an actor needs
idempotency, not only isolation.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Races show up under load, not in tests. Make shared state rare, measure
contention, and test with stress and randomised scheduling.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Assume anything shared will be touched at the worst moment.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Blackboards resemble shared databases and event stores; actors resemble
services with their own data.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

This is the preview for Phase 6 (threads, mutexes) and Phase 10 (distributed
state). I read it again when I build the thread pool. Done when I can show a race in single-threaded JavaScript and fix it.
</Level>

</Levels>

```ts
// A race with no threads: both calls read 1 before either writes.
let stock = 1;
async function buy() {
  if (stock > 0) {
    await chargeCard();   // the other buy() runs here
    stock -= 1;           // stock can reach -1
  }
}
```

### 7. While You Are Coding

**Covers:** Topics 37–44: listen to the unease that says something is wrong (Listen to Your
Lizard Brain); know why code works, not just that it works (Programming by
Coincidence); estimate the order of an algorithm (Algorithm Speed); refactor
continuously in small steps (Refactoring); write tests to design code (Test to
Code); generate inputs to check properties (Property-Based Testing); think
about attackers (Stay Safe Out There); and name things carefully (Naming
Things).

<Levels label="Chapter 7, While You Are Coding">

<Level name="Beginner">

If you cannot explain why a line works, it is luck. Name variables for what
they mean. Write the test before or while you write the code.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

Algorithm speed: count the loops. Nested loops over the input are O(n²); a sort
is O(n log n). Check the estimate with a benchmark on 10×, 100× the input.
Refactoring is behaviour-preserving, which is why it needs tests first.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Property-based testing states invariants ("sorting twice equals sorting once",
"the word counts sum to the number of words") and lets a generator find the
counterexample, then shrinks it to the smallest failing input.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Coincidence shows up as code that depends on an undocumented order (object key
order, file system listing order) or on a default you never set.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A property test can pass because the generator never produces the hard case (an
empty string, a Unicode combining character). Check what it generates.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Security basics: validate input, least privilege, secure defaults, keep
dependencies patched, never roll your own crypto.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Deliberate programming: every line is there on purpose.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Algorithm speed scales into capacity planning; naming scales into API and domain
vocabulary.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I apply it in Phases 1 to 4: property tests for the data structures, benchmarks
for the algorithms, a naming pass in every review. Done when one benchmark confirms a Big-O estimate and one property test has found a real bug.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 8. Before the Project

**Covers:** Topics 45–48: requirements are discovered, not gathered (The Requirements Pit);
find the real constraints of a hard problem (Solving Impossible Puzzles); pair
and mob with users and colleagues (Working Together); and agility is a set of
values applied through feedback, not a process you buy (The Essence of Agility).

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Before the Project">

<Level name="Beginner">

Ask what the user is trying to achieve, not only what they asked for. Write it
down and show it back.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

A requirement is a need; a policy is a rule that can change; keep policies in
data. A glossary prevents two people using one word for two things.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Impossible puzzles usually have an assumed constraint that is not real. List the
constraints and challenge each one.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Users describe solutions. "Add an export button" may mean "I need this in my
spreadsheet every Monday."

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

A requirement that is fixed by law versus one fixed by habit look the same in a
ticket.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Short feedback loops: small releases, demos, and measuring what users actually
do.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Uncertainty is normal; reduce it in small steps.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

Requirements drive the non-functional constraints: latency, availability, data
retention. Those shape the architecture.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I use it in the Phase 12 capstone design doc: goals, non-goals and challenged
constraints. Done when the capstone design doc lists goals, non-goals and the constraints I challenged.
</Level>

</Levels>

### 9. Pragmatic Projects

**Covers:** Topics 49–53: teams that own quality and keep their own knowledge portfolio
(Pragmatic Teams); copying rituals without the reasons does not work (Coconuts
Don't Cut It); version control, regression tests and full automation as the
starter kit (Pragmatic Starter Kit); delight users by meeting their real
expectations (Delight Your Users); and sign your work (Pride and Prejudice).

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Pragmatic Projects">

<Level name="Beginner">

Automate the build and the tests. Run them on every change.

</Level>

<Level name="Intermediate">

The starter kit: everything in version control, tests that run without a human,
and a pipeline that builds, tests and deploys the same way every time.

</Level>

<Level name="Advanced">

Cargo-cult practice (copying another team's process without its context) fails
because the context was the point. Measure whether a practice helps you.

</Level>

<Level name="Edge cases">

Full automation still needs a manual escape hatch for incidents.

</Level>

<Level name="Corner cases">

Tests that only run in CI and not locally slow feedback until people stop
running them.

</Level>

<Level name="Production level">

Regression tests for every bug fixed; the pipeline blocks a bad change. That is
the Phase 11 gate.

</Level>

<Level name="Mindset">

Sign your work: be proud of it and accountable for it.

</Level>

<Level name="System design">

The starter kit is the CI/CD foundation that the DevOps Handbook expands on.

</Level>

<Level name="Strategy">

I set up `make check` in Phase 0, and a CI gate in Phase 11. Done when one command builds and tests the whole study repo and CI runs the same command.
</Level>

</Levels>

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"DRY means no repeated lines."** It means no repeated knowledge. Repeated
  lines with different reasons to change should stay repeated.
- **"Good-enough means low quality."** It means the quality bar is chosen with
  the user, explicitly.
- **"Crash early means crash the server."** It means stop the failing unit of
  work before it corrupts state.
- **"Tracer bullets and prototypes are the same."** One is kept, one is thrown
  away.
- **"The book is a rulebook."** It is a set of heuristics with reasons. When the
  reason does not apply, neither does the rule.
