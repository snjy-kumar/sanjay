## Why this book

This is the optional depth for phase 10 of the
<a href={withBase('/plans/traditional-software-developer/')}>traditional core</a>,
distributed systems. *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* is the
primary book: it explains data systems from the point of view of someone
building on them. This book is the textbook underneath: the general
principles, the classic algorithms (clock synchronisation, mutual
exclusion, election, consistency protocols) and the vocabulary that
papers assume. The authors give a personal digital copy away free.

I checked the nine chapter titles against the authors' site and a
university reading guide for the fourth edition. The summaries under each
title are my own description of what the chapter covers, not its list of
sections.

## How I would learn it

I would download the free copy and read it with DDIA open beside it. When
DDIA explains a system, this book explains the principle underneath, and
the two together stick better than either alone. I would not read
chapters 3 and 6 deeply unless my build needs them.

For every algorithm in chapters 5, 7 and 8, I would write a small
simulation in TypeScript with an in-memory network that can delay, drop
and reorder messages. If my simulation cannot break the algorithm, I have
not understood what it protects against. The bugs I find in my own
simulations go into my bug log, and the failure matrix I write at the end
of chapter 8 is the one I hand in for the phase 10 gate.

## The nine chapters

### 1. Introduction

**Covers:** From networked to distributed systems, distributed versus decentralised
systems, the design goals (resource sharing, distribution transparency,
openness, dependability, security, scalability), a classification of
distributed systems, and the pitfalls: the false assumptions developers
make.

<Levels label="Chapter 1, Introduction">
<Level name="Beginner">

A distributed system is many computers that appear to users as one
coherent system. The appearance is the hard part.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Transparency has kinds: where a resource is, whether it moved, whether it
is replicated, whether it failed. Each one hidden is a promise the system
has to keep.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Full transparency is impossible and sometimes harmful. Hiding a network
failure behind retries can turn a fast error into a slow hang. Good
systems choose what to expose.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Scaling has three dimensions: size (more users), geography (more
distance, more latency) and administration (more organisations). A design
that scales in one can break in another.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

The pitfalls are assumptions like "the network is reliable", "latency is
zero", "bandwidth is infinite", "there is one administrator". Each one is
cheap to make in a prototype and expensive to remove in production.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Most production incidents I will read about are a pitfall that became
true: a partition, a slow dependency, a clock jump, a misconfigured peer.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

One machine is the special case. Design for partial failure from the
start.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Decide early which transparencies the system promises and which it
exposes to clients, such as idempotency keys, retry-after headers or
read-your-writes guarantees.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Write the list of pitfalls from memory, then attach one real failure from
a public postmortem to each. Done when each false assumption on my list is paired with a real public failure it caused.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 2. Architectures

**Covers:** Architectural styles (layered, service-oriented, publish-subscribe),
middleware and its organisation, layered client-server and multitiered
architectures with examples like NFS and the Web, symmetric peer-to-peer
systems (structured, unstructured, hierarchical, BitTorrent), and hybrid
architectures: cloud, edge-cloud and blockchain.

<Compare left="Client-server" right="Peer-to-peer" rows={[["Roles are fixed", "Every node can be client and server"], ["Easy to reason about and secure", "No single point of failure"], ["Server is the bottleneck", "Lookup and churn are the hard parts"], ["NFS, the Web", "BitTorrent, distributed hash tables"]]} caption="Two families of architecture, and what each trades." />

<Levels label="Chapter 2, Architectures">
<Level name="Beginner">

An architecture is how components are arranged and how they talk:
layers, services, events or peers.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Middleware is the layer that hides heterogeneity: RPC frameworks, message
brokers, service meshes. It is where wrappers, interceptors and adapters
live.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Publish-subscribe decouples senders and receivers in space (they do not
know each other) and time (they need not be up together). The cost is
that nobody knows who depends on a message.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Structured peer-to-peer systems route lookups in a logarithmic number of
hops; unstructured ones flood or random-walk and may miss data that
exists.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Edge-cloud splits computation by latency and bandwidth. A decision that
needs global state cannot be made at the edge without stale data.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Most services I will build are multitiered client-server systems with
pub-sub for side effects. Knowing the other styles helps me recognise
when one fits better.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Architecture is a set of constraints chosen on purpose. Name the
constraint before choosing the style.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Draw the system's components, the arrows between them, and which arrows
are synchronous. Most availability problems are on synchronous arrows.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Draw the architecture of one system I use (the Web, a CDN-backed site, a
chat app) in each style the chapter describes, and note what changes. Done when I have drawn one system in two architectural styles and written down what changed.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 3. Processes

**Covers:** Threads and their use in clients and servers, virtualisation and how
virtual machines and containers apply to distributed systems, client-side
software (including software for replication transparency), server design
and server clusters, and code migration.

<Levels label="Chapter 3, Processes">
<Level name="Beginner">

A server waits for requests and handles them. Threads let it handle many
at once without one slow request blocking the rest.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Servers are iterative or concurrent, stateless or stateful. Stateless
servers are easier to scale and restart; stateful ones are faster for
sessions but must recover their state.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Virtual machines and containers give isolation and portability; the
chapter connects them to distributed systems as units of deployment and
migration.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A "stateless" server with a local cache is stateful for performance: a
restart is correct but slow. Know which state is soft.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Migrating code is easy; migrating code with its execution state and open
resources (sockets, files) is not, which is why most systems restart
instead of migrating.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Server clusters put a switch or load balancer in front of identical
servers. Session affinity is the moment stateless stops being true.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Push state to the edges: into the client or into a store designed to hold
it.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Client-side replication transparency (a client library that talks to
several replicas and hides it) is how many database drivers work.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Revisit my phase-6 thread pool and make it a server with a stateless
handler and an external store; measure throughput as threads grow. Done when my server's throughput curve as threads grow is measured and explained.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 4. Communication

**Covers:** Layered protocols and types of communication (transient and persistent,
synchronous and asynchronous), remote procedure call, message-oriented
communication including advanced transient messaging and message brokers,
and multicast communication.

<Flow steps={["Client stub marshals arguments", "Message over the network", "Server stub unmarshals", "Procedure runs", "Reply marshalled back", "Client stub returns"]} caption="An RPC: a local-looking call that is really two messages and two chances to fail." />

<Levels label="Chapter 4, Communication">
<Level name="Beginner">

Processes on different machines communicate by sending messages. RPC
dresses a message pair up as a function call.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Persistent communication stores a message until it is delivered (a
queue); transient communication drops it if the receiver is not there
(a socket). Synchronous waits; asynchronous does not.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

When an RPC times out, the client cannot know if the server ran the
procedure. At-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once semantics are
choices about how to handle that ignorance.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Marshalling: byte order, number sizes, references that do not mean
anything on another machine, versioned schemas.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A reply lost after the server committed a payment, followed by a client
retry. Only an idempotency key makes the retry safe.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

gRPC, HTTP APIs and message brokers are all here. Retries with backoff and
jitter, deadlines propagated across calls, and idempotent handlers are the
production version of this chapter.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

A remote call is not a local call. Pretending it is the original sin of
distributed programming.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Choose synchronous calls for queries that need an answer now, and queues
for work that can happen later. The choice decides how failures spread.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Build a tiny RPC over my phase-7 TCP code with a timeout and a retry, and
make the server deliberately drop replies to see duplicate execution. Done when my RPC with dropped replies shows duplicate execution, and an idempotency key removes it.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 5. Coordination

**Covers:** Clock synchronisation, logical clocks (Lamport and vector clocks), mutual
exclusion algorithms, election algorithms, and further coordination topics
such as location and gossip-based coordination and distributed
event matching.

```ts
// Lamport clock: a counter that respects happened-before.
class Lamport {
  t = 0;
  tick() { return ++this.t; }                      // local event or send
  recv(remote: number) { this.t = Math.max(this.t, remote) + 1; return this.t; }
}
```

<Levels label="Chapter 5, Coordination">
<Level name="Beginner">

Machines do not share a clock. Their clocks drift, and synchronisation
protocols like NTP keep them close, not equal.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Lamport clocks give every event a number such that if A happened before B,
A's number is smaller. Vector clocks go further: they can tell when two
events are concurrent.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Mutual exclusion and leader election are coordination problems with
centralised, distributed and token-based solutions, each with different
message counts and failure behaviour.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A Lamport timestamp being smaller does not mean an event happened first;
the implication only goes one way.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A leader that is paused (for example by garbage collection) and wakes up
still believing it leads. Fencing tokens, numbers that increase with each
new leader, let storage reject the stale one.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Never order events across machines by wall-clock time without a bound on
skew. Use logical clocks, a single sequencer, or a system with bounded
clock uncertainty.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

"When" is a question of causality, not of clocks.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Leases, locks and leaders in production (etcd, ZooKeeper) are built on
consensus, not on the simple algorithms here, because those assume
failures they cannot handle.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Implement Lamport and vector clocks, simulate three processes exchanging
messages, and print which events the vector clocks call concurrent. Done when my simulation prints concurrent events correctly from vector clocks in a test.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 6. Naming

**Covers:** Names, identifiers and addresses, flat naming (including distributed hash
tables), structured naming and name spaces such as DNS, attribute-based
naming, and naming in decentralised systems.

<Levels label="Chapter 6, Naming">
<Level name="Beginner">

A name refers to an entity; an address says where it is now; an
identifier refers to exactly one entity forever.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Flat names (like hashes) need a lookup service such as a distributed hash
table. Structured names (like DNS paths) are resolved by walking a tree.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

A distributed hash table maps keys to nodes with consistent hashing, so
adding or removing a node moves only a fraction of the keys.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Caching name resolutions makes lookup fast and makes moves slow to
propagate; DNS TTLs are that trade written down.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

An entity that moves while a request is in flight: forwarding pointers or
a home location help, but each adds a hop and a failure point.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Service discovery, DNS, consistent hashing in caches and sharded stores
are this chapter in production.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Separate "who" from "where". Things that move need stable identifiers.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Consistent hashing is the basis of partitioning in Dynamo-style stores;
I will meet it again in DDIA's partitioning chapter.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Implement consistent hashing with virtual nodes and measure how many keys
move when a node is added, with and without virtual nodes. Done when I have measured key movement with and without virtual nodes when a node joins.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 7. Consistency and Replication

**Covers:** Why replicate, data-centric consistency models (continuous consistency,
sequential and causal consistency, consistent ordering of operations),
client-centric consistency models (eventual consistency, monotonic reads
and writes, read-your-writes, writes-follow-reads), replica management,
and consistency protocols such as primary-based and replicated-write
protocols.

<Compare left="Data-centric model" right="Client-centric model" rows={[["What all processes see", "What one client sees"], ["Sequential, causal", "Read-your-writes, monotonic reads"], ["Stronger, more coordination", "Weaker, cheaper, per session"], ["Shared state for many writers", "Mobile clients, caches, eventual stores"]]} caption="Two ways the chapter classifies consistency." />

<Levels label="Chapter 7, Consistency and Replication">
<Level name="Beginner">

Replicating data makes it faster to read and safer from failure, but
copies can disagree. A consistency model says how much they are allowed
to disagree.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Sequential consistency: all processes see the same order of operations.
Causal consistency: only causally related operations must be seen in
order. Eventual consistency: replicas converge if writes stop.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Client-centric models give guarantees per session: a user always sees
their own writes and never sees time go backwards, even if other users see
different states.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Monotonic reads break when a client is load-balanced to a lagging replica
after reading from a fresh one.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Read-your-writes across devices: a write from the phone and a read from
the laptop belong to the same user but different sessions.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Primary-based replication with followers for reads is the default in
relational databases. The bugs are replication lag showing up as
"my change disappeared".

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Consistency is a contract with users. Pick the weakest one they will not
notice, and no weaker.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Route reads after a write to the primary, or wait for the replica to catch
up to the write's position, for the paths where users would notice.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Simulate a primary and two lagging replicas, and write a test that fails
for monotonic reads, then fix it with sticky routing. Done when a test that fails for monotonic reads passes after I add sticky routing.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 8. Fault Tolerance

**Covers:** Basic concepts, failure models, synchronous and asynchronous systems,
failure masking by redundancy, process resilience and consensus (including
Paxos and Byzantine agreement), reliable client-server communication,
reliable group communication and message ordering, distributed commit,
and recovery.

<Compare left="Failure model" right="What the node does" rows={[["Crash", "stops, and stays stopped"], ["Omission", "fails to send or receive some messages"], ["Timing", "responds, but outside the allowed time"], ["Response", "responds with the wrong value"], ["Arbitrary (Byzantine)", "anything, including lying"]]} caption="Failure models, from easiest to hardest to tolerate." />

<Levels label="Chapter 8, Fault Tolerance">
<Level name="Beginner">

Fault tolerance means the system keeps providing its service when some
parts fail. Redundancy, in information, time or physical copies, is the
only tool.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

To tolerate k crash failures, a group needs k + 1 members; to tolerate k
Byzantine failures, it needs 3k + 1 for agreement.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

In an asynchronous system, a crashed process and a slow one look the
same. Consensus there is impossible to guarantee with even one crash (the
FLP result), which is why practical protocols use timeouts and accept
occasional unavailability.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Two-phase commit blocks if the coordinator crashes after participants
have voted yes: they hold locks and cannot decide alone.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A message delivered to some group members but not others before the
sender crashes. Reliable multicast must deliver to all survivors or none.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Raft and Paxos run real systems (etcd, many databases). Checkpoints and
logs are how a crashed node recovers to a consistent state.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Name the failure model before choosing the protocol. A protocol is only
correct relative to the failures it assumes.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Write a failure matrix: for each component, each failure mode, what the
user sees, and what recovers it. It is a phase-10 gate item.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Read this chapter beside the Raft paper while building the phase-10
replicated store; test every failure model I claim to handle. Done when my replicated store's failure matrix is written and each row has a test.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 9. Security

**Covers:** Security threats, policies and mechanisms, design issues, cryptography
basics, secure channels and authentication, trust in distributed systems,
authorisation and access control, and monitoring.

<Levels label="Chapter 9, Security">
<Level name="Beginner">

Security in a distributed system means protecting data in transit,
proving who is talking, and limiting what each party can do.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Secure channels combine authentication, integrity and confidentiality,
usually with TLS. Authentication protocols are where subtle mistakes
(replays, reflection) live.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Trust has to be bootstrapped: certificates, key distribution and who
vouches for whom. Every chain of trust ends at something assumed.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Authenticating the client but not the server (or the reverse) leaves a
man-in-the-middle path open.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Delegation: a service acting on behalf of a user needs a token scoped to
that user and that action, not its own full privileges.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

Mutual TLS between services, short-lived credentials, least-privilege
policies and audit logs are the production shape of this chapter.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Every message is from a possibly hostile network until proven otherwise.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Put authorisation decisions where the data is, not only at the edge, so a
compromised edge cannot bypass them.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

Read it as a bridge into phase 11 and Ross Anderson's *Security
Engineering*; write a threat model for my replicated store. Done when my replicated store has a one-page threat model with at least three real findings.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Distribution transparency should be total."** Hiding failures can
  make them worse; expose what clients must handle.
- **"Lamport clocks order events in real time."** They respect causality,
  nothing more.
- **"Eventual consistency means it is wrong for a while."** It means it
  converges; client-centric guarantees can still make it feel correct to
  each user.
- **"Two-phase commit solves distributed transactions."** It is correct
  and it blocks; that is why sagas exist.
- **"More replicas means more availability."** More replicas also means
  more coordination for strong consistency.
