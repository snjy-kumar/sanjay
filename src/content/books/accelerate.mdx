It is the communication lens of the <a href={withBase('/plans/underrated-fundamentals/')}>underrated fundamentals</a>
and part of phase 5 (DevOps and production) of the
<a href={withBase('/plans/software-ai-developer/')}>software AI developer</a> roadmap. It gives me a
measured way to talk about delivery: not "we ship fast" but four numbers anyone can check, and the
capabilities that research links to improving them.

It is also a book about evidence. Half of it explains how the research was done, which teaches me how
to judge any claim about engineering practice, including my own.

## Part I: What We Found


### 1. Accelerate

**Covers:** Why software delivery matters to every organisation, why maturity models are the wrong frame, and the shift to measuring capabilities and outcomes.

<Compare left={"Maturity model"} right={"Capability model"} rows={[["A fixed end state to reach", "Continuous improvement, no end"], ["Same levels for every team", "Each team picks its constraint"], ["Measures activity", "Measures outcomes"], ["Done when certified", "Never done"]]} caption={"Two ways to frame improvement. The book argues for the second."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 1, Accelerate"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Every company now depends on software, so how well it delivers software affects how well it does. The book
asks which practices actually improve delivery, measured, not assumed.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Maturity models describe levels a team climbs and then stops. The book prefers capabilities: specific
practices that can each be improved, chosen by where a team is weakest.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

The research links capabilities to delivery performance, and delivery performance to organisational
outcomes. The claim is predictive (practices predict performance), based on surveys analysed statistically,
not anecdotes.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A team already shipping many times a day is the boundary: a maturity model says it is "done"; a capability
view still finds the next constraint (perhaps recovery time).

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A capability adopted without the culture to use it: a CI server everyone ignores when it is red shows the
tool without the capability.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice, improvement starts with measuring the current state and choosing one constraint, not a
company-wide framework rollout.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Improve the outcome, not the checklist.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Delivery capability is a system property spanning code, pipeline, architecture and team. No single
team or tool owns it.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write one paragraph on my project's biggest delivery constraint today. Done when I can name it and the
capability that addresses it.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 2. Measuring Performance

**Covers:** Why common productivity measures fail, and the four measures of software delivery performance: lead time, deployment frequency, time to restore service and change failure rate.

<Layers layers={[{"name": "Throughput", "note": "lead time for changes, deployment frequency"}, {"name": "Stability", "note": "time to restore service, change failure rate"}]} caption={"The four measures in two pairs. The research found the pairs move together rather than trading off."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 2, Measuring Performance"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Lines of code, velocity points and utilisation are bad measures: they reward output, not outcomes, and are
easily gamed. Four measures describe delivery: how long a change takes to reach production, how often you
deploy, how fast you recover, and how often changes fail.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

I can compute them from my own data:

```ts
type Deploy = { at: Date; commits: Date[]; failed: boolean; restoredAt?: Date };
const median = (xs: number[]) => xs.sort((a, b) => a - b)[Math.floor(xs.length / 2)];
const leadTimeH = median(deploys.flatMap((d) => d.commits.map((c) => (+d.at - +c) / 3.6e6)));
const changeFailRate = deploys.filter((d) => d.failed).length / deploys.length;
const restoreH = median(deploys.filter((d) => d.restoredAt).map((d) => (+d.restoredAt! - +d.at) / 3.6e6));
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Lead time here starts at commit, not at idea, because the design-to-commit part is too variable to
measure. The key finding: speed and stability are not a trade-off; the best performers do well on all four.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A project with zero deploys in a period has undefined change failure rate (division by zero) and infinite
lead time. Report "no deploys" explicitly rather than a number.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Very few deploys plus one failure: one failed deploy out of two is a 50 percent failure rate. Small samples
swing wildly; use rolling windows and show the count.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In production these come from pipeline and incident data automatically, shown on a dashboard per team, never
used to rank individuals.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Measure the system, not the people. A metric used as a target stops being a good measure.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

The four metrics are system-level health signals for the delivery pipeline, in the same way SLOs are for
services.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I compute all four for my phase 5 pipeline from git and deploy logs. Done when a script prints them for any
date range.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 3. Measuring and Changing Culture

**Covers:** How to model and measure organisational culture (using Westrum's typology of pathological, bureaucratic and generative cultures), why culture predicts performance, and how to change it by changing practices.

<Compare left={"Pathological"} right={"Generative"} rows={[["Messengers punished", "Messengers trained"], ["Failure leads to scapegoating", "Failure leads to inquiry"], ["Bridging discouraged", "Bridging encouraged"], ["Novelty crushed", "Novelty implemented"]]} caption={"Two ends of Westrum's typology, as I understand it, seen through how information flows."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 3, Measuring and Changing Culture"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Culture can be measured by asking how information flows: is bad news shared or hidden? Westrum's typology
names three types: pathological (power-oriented), bureaucratic (rule-oriented) and generative
(performance-oriented).

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Survey items ask people to agree or disagree with statements like "failures are treated as opportunities
to learn". Answers combine into a culture score.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

The research suggests culture can be changed by changing what people do: adopting practices (like
blameless postmortems and continuous delivery) changes how people think, not only the other way round.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A team of one: culture still shows in how I treat my own failures, for example whether I write
postmortems or quietly fix and forget.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A generative team inside a pathological organisation: local practices work until an incident reaches
management, and the blame culture returns.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: blameless postmortems, cross-team collaboration, and leaders who reward raising problems early.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

How a team handles bad news predicts how it will handle everything else.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Culture is part of the system: it decides whether the failure signals designed into the software are
acted on.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write a blameless postmortem for a real bug of mine. Done when it contains no "should have" about a person
and at least one systemic fix.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 4. Technical Practices

**Covers:** Continuous delivery and its practices: version control, deployment automation, continuous integration, trunk-based development, test automation, test data management, shifting left on security, and their effect on quality and burnout.

<Flow steps={["commit to trunk", "automated build and tests", "artifact", "automated deploy to staging", "automated deploy to production"]} caption={"Continuous delivery: every commit is releasable, and releasing is a button or nothing at all."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 4, Technical Practices"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Continuous delivery means keeping software always releasable, so releasing is routine. It rests on version
control for everything, automated tests, and automated deployment.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Trunk-based development: short-lived branches (merged within a day or so) instead of long feature branches.
Continuous integration means everyone integrates into trunk often and the build stays green.

```yaml
on: [push]
jobs:
  ci: { runs-on: ubuntu-latest, steps: [ { uses: actions/checkout@v4 }, { run: npm ci }, { run: npm test } ] }
```

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

The research associates these practices with higher delivery performance, less rework, and less deployment
pain and burnout. Test data management (having the right data to run tests) and reliable tests (failing
means something is wrong) matter as much as having tests.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A trunk with no tests: trunk-based development without automated tests merges breakage faster.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Feature flags plus trunk-based development: unfinished work ships behind flags, which works until flags are
never removed and the code fills with dead paths.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In production: every commit runs CI, main is always deployable, deploys are automated, and flaky tests are
treated as bugs.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Make the safe path the easy path. If releasing is painful, do it more often until it is not.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

The pipeline is part of the architecture. Its speed and reliability bound the whole team's lead time.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I move my project to trunk-based work with branches under a day and automate deploys. Done when a one-line
change reaches production in under an hour without manual steps.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 5. Architecture

**Covers:** Which architectural characteristics predict performance: loosely coupled architecture, teams that can test and deploy independently, and the freedom for teams to choose their own tools.

<Compare left={"Tightly coupled"} right={"Loosely coupled"} rows={[["Release needs other teams", "Team deploys on its own"], ["Integration test environment shared", "Tested in isolation"], ["Schema shared by many services", "Each owns its data"], ["Changes need sign-off chains", "Contracts and tests are enough"]]} caption={"The architecture question as the book frames it: can a team change and deploy its part without coordinating with others?"} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 5, Architecture"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

The type of system (monolith, microservices, mainframe) mattered less than whether teams could change and
deploy their part independently.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Loose coupling means a team can make most changes without permission from or coordination with other teams,
and test without an integrated environment.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Conway's law runs both ways: the communication structure of teams shapes the system; designing team
boundaries is designing architecture. Tool choice by teams correlated with performance too.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A single developer: coupling still shows in how many modules a change touches and whether tests need the
whole system running.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Microservices plus a shared database: deployed separately, coupled through the schema. Independence is in
the data, not the deployment unit.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: contract tests, versioned APIs, owned data, and the ability to deploy one service without
a release train.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask "who must I talk to before I ship this?" The answer measures coupling.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

This chapter turns the design goal into a measurable property: deployability and testability in isolation.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I check whether each module of my project can be tested without the others. Done when each has its own
fast test suite.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 6. Integrating Infosec into the Delivery Lifecycle

**Covers:** Shifting security left: involving security early, making secure options easy, and building security checks into the pipeline rather than at the end.

<Flow steps={["design review with security", "secure defaults and libraries", "automated scans in CI", "deploy", "monitor"]} caption={"Shift left: security moves from a gate at the end into every stage."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 6, Integrating Infosec into the Delivery Lifecycle"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Security reviewed only before release finds problems when they are most expensive to fix. Shifting left
means including it from design onwards.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Practical forms: security in design reviews, preapproved libraries and patterns, and automated scanning
(dependencies, secrets, static analysis) in CI.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Making the secure way the easy way (a shared auth library, a hardened base image) does more than checklists.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A brand-new project has no security debt; that is the cheapest time to set defaults.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Automated scanners plus alert fatigue: hundreds of low findings bury the one critical one. Tune severity
thresholds and fix or suppress with reasons.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: CI fails on high-severity findings, base images are patched automatically, and security is
part of the definition of done.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Security is part of quality, not a separate phase.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Security tooling is part of the delivery pipeline's design.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I add dependency, secret and static scans to CI. Done when each runs on every PR and one real finding is fixed.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 7. Management Practices for Software

**Covers:** Lean management practices that predict performance: limiting work in progress, visual management, using monitoring to make decisions, and lightweight change approval.

<Flow steps={["change ready", "peer review", "automated checks", "deploy"]} caption={"Lightweight change approval. The research found external change boards did not improve stability."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 7, Management Practices for Software"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Lean management: limit work in progress, make work visible, and use data from production to make
decisions.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

WIP limits reduce context switching and expose bottlenecks. Visual boards show where work is stuck.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Heavyweight change approval (an external board approving each change) was associated with lower
performance and no better stability; peer review plus automation worked better.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A WIP limit of one: no parallel work, so any blocker stops everything. Limits need to fit the team.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

A change board plus continuous delivery: every deploy waits for a weekly meeting, which turns continuous
delivery back into batch releases.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: peer review, automated tests and deploys, and monitoring used to decide what to work on.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Make work visible and small, then let feedback from production steer it.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Approval processes are part of the delivery system and set its lead time.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I limit myself to two items in progress for two weeks and record lead time. Done when I can compare it with
the two weeks before.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 8. Product Development

**Covers:** Lean product practices: working in small batches, making the flow of work visible, gathering customer feedback, and team experimentation.

<Flow steps={["idea", "smallest testable slice", "ship", "measure with users", "decide"]} caption={"Small batches with feedback: each slice can teach something before the next is built."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 8, Product Development"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Work in small batches that can be delivered in days, not months, so feedback arrives early.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Teams that gather customer feedback and can experiment without lengthy approval perform better.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Small batches reduce risk and speed learning; they need continuous delivery underneath, or small batches
pile up waiting for a release.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A feature too small to measure: if a slice cannot produce a signal, it is too small to teach anything.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Experiments plus no feedback loop: shipping fast without measuring is just shipping.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: feature flags, analytics on key events, and a team allowed to change its plan based on data.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Treat each feature as a hypothesis.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Product and delivery are one system: delivery speed only matters if it feeds learning.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I split my next feature into slices under two days each and measure one. Done when one decision is made
from the data.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 9. Making Work Sustainable

**Covers:** Deployment pain and burnout: what causes them, how technical and lean practices reduce them, and what organisations can do.

<Flow steps={["painful deploys", "fear of releasing", "bigger batches", "more painful deploys", "burnout"]} caption={"A reinforcing loop. Automation and small changes break it."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 9, Making Work Sustainable"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Deployment pain (fear and anxiety around releases) is a measurable signal of a weak delivery system, and it
contributes to burnout.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Practices that reduce pain: automated deploys, small changes, monitoring, and easy rollback.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Burnout is linked to organisational factors (workload, control, fairness, values) more than individual
weakness; fixing it means fixing the environment.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A deploy that only one person can do: their absence stops releases and their presence is never optional.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

On-call plus painful deploys: the person who deploys late is also paged when it breaks.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: no Friday-night heroics, automated rollbacks, sustainable on-call, and attention to people's
workload.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Pain is information about the system.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Sustainability is a property of the delivery system and the organisation together.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I note my own stress before each deploy for a month. Done when it trends down after automating the
most painful step.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 12. The Science Behind This Book

<Levels label={"Chapter 12, The Science Behind This Book"}>

Research can describe, explore, infer or predict. This book's analysis is inferential: it starts with

</Level>

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Ask what kind of evidence a claim rests on before acting on it.

</Level>

### 13. Introduction to Psychometrics

**Covers:** How surveys measure things that cannot be observed directly (latent constructs), using several questions per construct, and the validity and reliability checks that make the measures trustworthy.

<Figure art={`
          [ culture (latent) ]
           /       |        \\
      item 1    item 2    item 3     each item is one survey statement
`} caption={"A latent construct measured by several items."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 13, Introduction to Psychometrics"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Some things (culture, burnout) cannot be measured directly. Psychometrics measures them through several
survey questions that each reflect part of the idea.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

Validity checks whether questions measure the intended construct; reliability checks whether they agree
with each other consistently.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Statistical checks (for example, measures of internal consistency and how items group together) confirm
that items behave as a construct should.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

A single question per construct is fragile: one ambiguous wording breaks the measure.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Two constructs whose questions overlap: they cannot be told apart, so a measure seems to predict
itself.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: well-designed surveys can measure team health over time.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Measure carefully what cannot be counted directly.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

Survey data can complement system data in a complete picture of delivery.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I write a three-item measure for "deployment pain" and check agreement. Done when the items agree on a small sample.

</Level>
</Levels>

### 14. Why Use a Survey

**Covers:** Why surveys are useful for this research compared with system data: speed, breadth, and their own limits, including the risk of people lying.

<Compare left={"Surveys"} right={"System data"} rows={[["Fast and broad", "Precise but narrow"], ["Measures perceptions and culture", "Measures events"], ["Comparable across organisations", "Specific to one toolchain"], ["Self-reported", "Recorded"]]} caption={"Two sources of evidence, each strong where the other is weak."} />

<Levels label={"Chapter 14, Why Use a Survey"}>
<Level name="Beginner">

Surveys collect data from many organisations quickly, which system logs from one company cannot.

</Level>
<Level name="Intermediate">

They also measure what logs cannot: culture, satisfaction, practices.

</Level>
<Level name="Advanced">

Bad actors and bias are handled through careful design and statistical checks.

</Level>
<Level name="Edge cases">

Questions with an obvious "good" answer invite social desirability bias.

</Level>
<Level name="Corner cases">

Survey data plus system data disagreeing: both have errors; the disagreement itself is information.

</Level>
<Level name="Production level">

In practice: use both when possible.

</Level>
<Level name="Mindset">

Know the strengths and limits of each evidence source.

</Level>
<Level name="System design">

A measurement system for engineering mixes both.

</Level>
<Level name="Strategy">

I compare my survey item for deployment pain with my deploy logs. Done when I can explain any mismatch.

</Level>
</Levels>

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Use the four metrics to rank developers." They measure systems, not people.
- "Speed costs stability." The book's finding is that they go together.
- "Adopt microservices to be high performing." The finding is about loose coupling and independent deployment.
- "Correlation in a survey proves my practice causes success." The research is careful about its claims; I should be too.
